HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test an LED Bulb With a Multimeter

You can't test a household LED bulb the way you test a single LED, because a driver circuit sits between the screw base and the LEDs, so a resistance or diode reading across the base tells you almost nothing. Instead, use the multimeter to prove the fixture: about 120 V AC at the socket, a switch that closes, and no dimmer the bulb can't handle. If the socket checks out and a known-good bulb lights, the LED bulb is bad. This guide covers standard A19 and BR30 screw-in bulbs and 12 V MR16 spotlights, with the readings to expect at each step.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 18, 2026

A CAT III 600V meter such as the Fluke 115 is the right class of tool for probing a live light socket. See our Fluke 115 review.

Light sockets are live at 120 V

  • Turn the switch off before changing a bulb or touching the socket tab. For anything beyond a voltage check, switch off the breaker and confirm the socket is dead with your meter.
  • Use a meter rated CAT III 600V for home wiring, with shrouded probes, and keep your fingers behind the probe guards.
  • Don't open an LED bulb while it is connected, and treat a just-removed bulb with care: the driver's capacitor can stay charged for a short time, and the housing can be hot.
  • Scorch marks, a melted socket, a burning smell or a breaker that trips mean a wiring fault. Stop and call a licensed electrician.

Why a reading across the bulb base is useless

An incandescent bulb is a single filament between the two base contacts, so a resistance check gives a clear answer: a few ohms when cold means good, OL means the filament broke. An LED bulb is different. The center contact and the threaded shell feed a small power supply called the driver, which rectifies the 120 V AC, smooths it with a capacitor and feeds a controlled current to the LEDs under the dome.

Put a meter on Ω across that base and you are measuring the driver's input: a rectifier, a capacitor and often a fuse resistor. A good bulb may read OL, a high value, or a number that climbs as the meter's small test current charges the capacitor. A dead bulb can read exactly the same. Diode mode is no better, because the LEDs inside are wired in series strings whose combined forward voltage is far above the roughly 2 V a typical diode test supplies. That is why the useful multimeter work is on the fixture, and the bulb itself is judged by substitution. Single 5 mm LEDs are a different story; for those, see how to check LED polarity with a multimeter.

1Swap with a known-good bulb

The fastest test needs no meter. Move the suspect bulb to a lamp you know works, and put a bulb you know works into the suspect fixture. If the suspect bulb stays dark in the good lamp, it has failed. If the good bulb stays dark in the suspect fixture, the problem is the fixture, the switch or the circuit, and the meter steps below find which. Test on a plain on/off switch where you can, so a fussy dimmer doesn't mislead you.

2Inspect the socket with the power off

With the switch and breaker off, look into the socket. The brass tab at the bottom is the center contact. It should spring up slightly; years of tight bulbs can flatten it so a new bulb no longer touches it. With power confirmed off, you can lift it slightly with a wooden stick or insulated tool. Look for darkened plastic, corrosion on outdoor fixtures, or a cracked socket shell. The National Electrical Code (NEC 410.50) requires the threaded shell to be connected to the grounded conductor, the neutral, so the tab is the hot connection and the part to treat with most respect.

3Measure the socket voltage

Set the meter to AC volts (V~) with the red lead in the V jack. Restore power and turn the switch on. Touch one probe tip to the center tab and the other to the inside of the threaded shell, without letting either probe bridge both. A healthy US circuit reads about 120 V. ANSI C84.1, the US voltage standard, treats 114 to 126 V at the service entrance as the normal range, so 115 to 125 V at a socket is unremarkable.

Read 0 V with the switch on? Check the breaker, then the switch (step 4). A reading well below 110 V suggests a loose connection somewhere upstream that drops voltage under load, worth a call to an electrician. With the switch off you should see close to 0 V; a digital meter's high input impedance can show a few volts of induced "ghost" voltage that disappears under any load. If you see 50 V or more with the switch off, the switch may be in the neutral leg, which is a wiring fault.

4Check the switch and dimmer

With the breaker off and the switch cover removed, you can check a plain toggle switch for continuity: disconnect it and read ohms across its terminals. It should beep or read near 0 Ω when on and OL when off. Our continuity testing guide covers the method. If you are not comfortable working in a switch box, stop at the socket voltage test and call an electrician.

Dimmers are the most common reason a good LED bulb misbehaves. Older dimmers were designed for incandescent loads, and many list a minimum load that a few LED bulbs drawing 8 to 10 W each can fall below. The result is flicker, buzzing, a dim glow when off, or a bulb that never turns on. The bulb's box says whether it is dimmable; a non-dimmable LED bulb on a dimmer can fail early. A meter can't judge dimmer compatibility directly, but a steady 120 V with the dimmer at full and a bulb that still flickers point squarely at the dimmer. Our guide on why LED lights flicker goes further.

5Testing 12 V MR16 LED bulbs

Two-pin MR16 spotlights run on 12 V from a transformer, so they are safer to probe at the pins. Set the meter to AC volts and measure across the two socket contacts with the light on. A magnetic transformer gives a steady reading near 12 V AC. Electronic transformers output a high-frequency waveform that many meters read poorly, and many print a minimum load sized for halogen lamps; a low-wattage LED replacement can sit below that minimum and make the transformer cycle on and off, which shows up as flicker. A True RMS meter reads these outputs more sensibly than an average-responding one, but the lasting fix is a transformer or driver the LED bulb's maker lists as compatible. If the socket reads 0 V, test the transformer's 120 V input before blaming the bulbs.

6Judge the bulb

Once the socket reads about 120 V and lights a known-good bulb, any LED bulb that stays dark, flickers or glows dimly in it is faulty. Most LED bulb failures are in the driver, especially its electrolytic capacitor, which ages faster with heat. That is why LED bulbs in enclosed globes and recessed cans without an enclosed-fixture rating fail early. Screw-in LED bulbs are not designed to be repaired; replace it, and if it was still young, check whether it was rated for the fixture it was in.

Test the fixture, judge the bulb

The meter's job is to prove the socket, switch and dimmer. A bulb that stays dark in a socket proven at 120 V, which lights another bulb, has failed, whatever its base reads on the ohms range.

LED bulb power and current: the numbers

ENERGY STAR equates an 800-lumen bulb with a traditional 60 W incandescent. LED bulbs that give 800 lumens typically draw around 8 to 10 W, printed on the box as the actual wattage. That gap explains the dimmer problem. Current is power divided by voltage: 60 W ÷ 120 V = 0.5 A for the incandescent, and 9 W ÷ 120 V ≈ 0.075 A for a 9 W LED, before allowing for the driver's power factor, which makes the real RMS current somewhat higher.

You can confirm a bulb's draw with a clamp meter on a line splitter, but at under 0.1 A most clamp meters are near the bottom of their range, and a plug-in watt meter is easier. For bulbs on separate drivers, such as LED panels, under-cabinet lights and tape, the driver output is measurable with a meter; see how to test an LED driver and how to test an LED strip.

Which multimeter to use on light fixtures

Any meter with AC volts and continuity works, but for mains fixtures choose one with a CAT III 600V rating and shrouded leads. True RMS helps with electronic transformers and dimmed circuits, where the waveform is not a clean sine wave.

Frequently asked questions

Can you test an LED bulb with a multimeter?

Not directly through the base. The bulb's driver circuit sits between the base contacts and the LEDs, so ohms or diode mode across the base gives an open or drifting reading even on a good bulb. Use the meter to confirm the socket has about 120 V AC and the switch works, then try the bulb in a socket you know is good.

Why does my LED bulb not work but an old incandescent does?

Usually a dimmer or switch that wasn't designed for LED loads. Old dimmers and some lighted or smart switches leak a small current or chop the waveform in a way an LED driver can't use, while an incandescent filament just glows. Check the bulb's packaging for dimmer compatibility, or replace the dimmer with an LED-rated model.

What voltage should a light socket have?

In the US, about 120 V AC between the center contact and the threaded shell, with the switch on. ANSI C84.1 treats 114 to 126 V at the service entrance as the normal band, so 115 to 125 V at a socket is ordinary. With the switch off you should read close to 0 V, apart from a little phantom voltage on a high-impedance meter.

How do I know if my LED bulb is burned out?

If the bulb stays dark in a socket that reads about 120 V and lights another bulb, it has failed. Flickering, a dim glow when switched off, buzzing or one dark section in a filament-style bulb are also signs. Most failures are in the driver, not the LEDs, and the bulb isn't designed to be repaired, so replace it.

Why does my LED bulb glow faintly when the switch is off?

A small current is still reaching it. Common causes are a lighted or smart switch that passes a trickle to power itself, a switch wired in the neutral instead of the hot, or induced voltage on long cable runs. An incandescent bulb hides it, but an LED driver can store that trickle and glow. Have an electrician check the switch wiring.
