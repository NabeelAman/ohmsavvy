HANMATEK HO52 vs OWON HDS272S

The HANMATEK HO52 vs OWON HDS272S choice is between two pocket-sized scope-meters whose published specs line up almost row for row: two channels, 250 MSa/s on one channel and 125 MSa/s on two, an 8K record, a 3.5-inch 320 × 240 screen and a 20,000-count true-RMS multimeter. The OWON adds two things: 70 MHz of bandwidth instead of 50 MHz, and a single-channel signal generator. Here is when those two extras are worth paying for.

Spec by spec

Put the two manuals side by side and most rows read the same: 5 ns/div to 1000 s/div timebase, 10 mV/div to 10 V/div, 10,000 waveforms per second, a 20 MHz bandwidth limit, 1 MΩ inputs and an edge trigger. The charts below cover the rows that actually differ, plus the sampling figures that are worth seeing next to the bandwidth numbers.

Two source notes. OWON's web spec table is misaligned and appears to put 500 MSa/s under the HDS272S; OWON's PDF datasheet and the Amazon listing both give 250 MSa/s, which is what we use. And the Amazon listing mentions a 4400 mAh battery good for about 6 hours, while OWON specifies two 2200 mAh 18650 cells and 3 to 6 hours. HANMATEK, for its part, doesn't state the HO52's converter resolution at all.

What 50 MHz and 70 MHz handhelds can measure

The usual rule is to buy three to five times more bandwidth than the highest frequency you want to see with its shape intact. That puts the HO52's comfortable range at roughly 10 to 17 MHz and the HDS272S's at roughly 14 to 23 MHz. Either covers audio, servo and PWM signals, switching regulators in the hundreds of kilohertz, UART and I2C, and the clocks on most 8-bit and many 32-bit hobby boards. Our bandwidth explainer shows where the rule comes from.

Sampling is the part buyers skip. With both channels on, each one gets 125 MSa/s, which is 2.5 samples per cycle of a 50 MHz sine and fewer than two for 70 MHz. Nyquist says two samples per cycle is the bare minimum, and a readable waveform needs several more. So on either handheld, two-channel work is limited by sampling long before the front-end bandwidth matters, and the HDS272S's extra 20 MHz pays off mainly when one channel is running at 250 MSa/s. See what GSa/s means for more on the trade-off.

Where the rise-time gap shows

A common estimate is that the rise time you see is the square root of the signal's rise time squared plus the scope's rise time squared. For a 10 ns edge, a 7 ns scope shows about 12 ns and a 5 ns scope about 11 ns. For a 100 ns edge the two readings are practically the same. The gap only matters on fast logic.

Short records, short windows

An 8K record at 250 MSa/s spans about 33 µs. Stretch the timebase to catch a slower event and both scopes must drop their sample rate to fit it in 8K points, so zooming into an edge afterwards shows little detail. Neither handheld is the tool for long captures.

Both stop at an edge trigger and seven automatic measurements (period, frequency, mean, peak-to-peak, max, min and amplitude), and neither maker lists serial decoding. If you need to trigger on a pulse width or decode I2C, a bench scope such as the ones in HANMATEK DOS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10 is the better tool.

Is the HDS272S generator worth having?

A scope only watches. Plenty of field jobs need nothing more: a crank sensor, a fan's tach line, the ripple on a supply. The generator becomes useful the moment the circuit under test has no signal of its own. With the HDS272S you can set a 1 kHz sine at a few hundred millivolts, feed it into a preamp, probe the input on CH1 and the output on CH2, and read the gain straight off the two amplitudes. Raise the level until the output flattens and you have found its clipping point; sweep the frequency and you can see a filter roll off.

OWON's figures set the limits: sine from 0.1 Hz to 25 MHz, square and pulse to 5 MHz, ramp to 1 MHz, arbitrary shapes to 5 MHz, and up to 2.5 Vpp into 50 Ω. That is ample for audio, sensor simulation and logic inputs, but it is not a lab function generator: you get one output, and it can't source more than a few volts. If you would use it only now and then, the HO52 plus a cheap standalone generator is another route; if you want everything in one bag, the HDS272S is the tidier kit.

Voltage limits on a handheld

The meter section is rated to 1000 V DC and 750 V AC, but each scope input is limited to 400 V (DC plus AC peak-to-peak) at the connector, and 230 V mains alone swings about 650 V peak-to-peak. Use the meter for mains voltage, use a probe rated for the job before putting a scope channel near line voltage, and assume the two channel grounds are tied together, since neither maker lists isolated channels. Don't leave the unit on its USB charger while probing mains-connected equipment.

The bottom line

These two handhelds share a sample rate, screen, record length and multimeter specification, so the decision comes down to two extras. Pick the OWON HDS272S if you will use its 25 MHz generator or want 70 MHz and a quicker rise time for single-channel work on faster logic. Pick the HANMATEK HO52 if you only need to watch signals and take meter readings, since it covers that for less.

Frequently asked questions

Is the OWON HDS272S just the HANMATEK HO52 with a generator?+

Not quite. The published specs match closely: two channels, 250 MSa/s on one channel and 125 MSa/s on two, a 3.5-inch 320 x 240 screen and a 20,000-count meter. But the HDS272S is rated at 70 MHz instead of 50 MHz, quotes a 5 ns rather than 7 ns rise time, and adds a 25 MHz signal generator.

Does the extra 20 MHz of the HDS272S make a real difference?+

Sometimes. With one channel at 250 MSa/s, 70 MHz keeps clocks in the 10 to 20 MHz range looking more square. With both channels on, each samples at 125 MSa/s on both scopes, so sampling rather than bandwidth sets the limit. For audio, PWM and slow serial lines, 50 MHz is already plenty.

Can the HANMATEK HO52 output a test signal?+

No. HANMATEK's manual lists the arbitrary waveform generator as an option fitted only to the HO52S. The plain HO52 is a two-channel scope plus a multimeter. If you want a signal output in a HANMATEK handheld, look for the HO52S variant instead, or choose the OWON HDS272S, which includes one.

Can I measure mains voltage with these handheld scope-meters?+

Use the multimeter section, rated to 750 V AC and 1000 V DC on both. Each scope input is limited to 400 V DC plus AC peak-to-peak at the connector, which 230 V mains exceeds. Neither maker lists isolated channels, so treat the probe grounds as common and use a properly rated probe for any line-voltage waveform.

Which handheld runs longer on a charge?+

HANMATEK quotes about 4 hours for the HO52 from two 2200 mAh cells. OWON quotes 3 to 6 hours for the HDS272S from two 2200 mAh 18650 cells, and says it charges over USB-C and from a power bank. Runtime depends on use, so treat both as rough figures.
