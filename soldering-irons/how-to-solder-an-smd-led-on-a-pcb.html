HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Solder an SMD LED on a PCB

To solder an SMD LED on a PCB, find the cathode mark on both the LED and the board, put a small blob of solder on one pad, hold the LED in place with tweezers and reheat that pad to tack it, then solder the other pad and briefly reflow the first. Keep every touch to a few seconds, because the plastic body and lens are the most heat-sensitive part of the job. This guide covers reading polarity marks on common packages, iron and hot air settings, the tack technique, LEDs with a center thermal pad, and how to test the result with a multimeter.

Heat, small parts and fumes

  • The iron tip and hot air nozzle run at several hundred degrees, and the board stays hot after you lift the iron. Always use the stand, and don't touch the board right after reflow.
  • Wear eye protection. Tweezers can launch tiny LEDs, and molten solder can spit when flux boils.
  • Use a fume extractor or ventilation for flux smoke, and wash your hands after using leaded solder.
  • Disconnect power before soldering. For LED strips and fixtures that run from mains drivers, unplug the driver and let it discharge; never solder on a powered strip.

SMD LED packages and polarity marks

SMD LEDs come in chip packages named after their size, the same codes used for resistors and capacitors, and in larger plastic-leaded packages such as 3528 and 5050 used on LED strips. The name tells you the size of the job: an 0603 LED is 1.6 mm long and needs a fine tip and a steady hand, while a 5050 is 5 mm square and much easier to handle. The polarity mark varies by maker, so treat the table as a guide and confirm with the datasheet or a meter.

1Confirm the polarity before you heat anything

Find the cathode mark on the LED and the cathode pad on the board, often a line, a K, a bar on a diode symbol, or a filled corner on the silkscreen. If the marks are unclear, put the meter in diode mode and touch the probes to the LED's ends: when it shows a forward voltage (and red or green parts often glow faintly), the red probe is on the anode. Our guide to checking LED polarity with a multimeter explains why blue and white LEDs may not light on some meters.

2Set up the iron, flux and tweezers

Use a fine conical or small chisel tip, a little flux on both pads, thin solder of 0.5 mm (0.020 in) or less, and fine tweezers. Many LED datasheets set a hand-soldering limit in the region of 300 to 350 °C for about three seconds per terminal; read yours and stay inside it. With leaded solder, which melts at 183 °C, that leaves plenty of margin; lead-free alloys around 217–220 °C need the iron near the top of that window. Clean the board first so solder wets quickly; a pad that takes ten seconds to wet will cook the LED.

3Tin one pad only

Touch the tip to one pad, usually the one easiest to reach for your hand, and add a small blob of solder, about the size of the pad. Leave the other pad bare. If you tin both, the LED rocks on two bumps and won't sit flat. Right-handed people usually find it easiest to pre-tin the right pad and hold the tweezers in the left hand.

4Tack the LED in place

Pick the LED up by its sides with the tweezers, never by the lens: silicone domes on many strip LEDs dent and tear easily. Line up its cathode mark with the board's, hold it against the board beside the tinned pad, then touch the iron to the solder blob and slide the LED into the melted solder until it sits flat and centered. Lift the iron and keep holding the LED for a second while the joint freezes. Check alignment now; one joint is easy to reheat and straighten.

5Solder the second pad and reflow the first

Touch the iron to the bare pad and the LED's terminal together and feed a little solder into the junction. You want a small, concave fillet up the end of the LED, not a ball. Then return to the first pad for a second with a touch of flux so its joint reflows cleanly; the tack joint is often slightly cold. If you have several LEDs in a row, tack them all first, check the alignment of the whole row, then finish the second pads. For more on tiny two-terminal parts, see soldering surface-mount resistors without hot air.

6Test the LED on the board

With the board unpowered, put the meter in diode mode with red on the anode pad and black on the cathode pad. A healthy LED shows a forward voltage, and many glow faintly; reversed, it should read OL. A reading near 0 V both ways points to a bridge; OL both ways points to an open joint or a damaged LED, though some meters can't supply enough voltage for blue and white LEDs. Then power the circuit and confirm the LED lights at normal brightness. If it's dim, check the current-limiting resistor; see how to test a current-limiting resistor.

LEDs with a center thermal pad

High-power LEDs and many COB and 3030-style parts have a large pad under the body that conducts heat into the board. An iron can't reach it, so solder it with hot air, a hotplate or reflow. Apply a thin layer of solder paste to all pads, place the LED, and heat the board area from a little way off with low airflow until the paste turns shiny and the LED settles. On aluminum-core LED boards, a hotplate under the board works better than air from above, because the metal core pulls heat away so quickly.

The same caution applies when removing a dead LED from a strip: heat the area, lift it gently once the solder melts, and clean the pads with wick before fitting a new one. For fine-pitch parts on the same board, see how to solder fine-pitch SMD components, and for a strip that still won't light, how to test an LED strip with a multimeter.

Polarity first, heat last

Most failed SMD LED jobs are either backwards or overheated. Confirm the cathode before the iron touches anything, tack one pad, check, then finish with short touches.

Frequently asked questions

Which side of an SMD LED is negative?

It depends on the maker, so check the datasheet. Common marks are a green line or dot on top, a notched or cut corner, or a T, triangle or arrow on the underside, where the bar or point usually marks the cathode. On the board, the cathode pad is often marked with a line, a K or a filled corner. If in doubt, a multimeter in diode mode settles it in seconds.

Can you solder SMD LEDs with a regular soldering iron?

Yes. Two-pad packages such as 0603, 0805, 1206 and 3528 solder well with a temperature-controlled iron, a fine tip, flux and tweezers. LEDs with a large center thermal pad underneath, common on high-power LEDs, need hot air, a hotplate or reflow, because the iron can't reach the pad that carries most of the heat away.

How hot can an SMD LED get when soldering?

Check the datasheet's soldering section. Many LED datasheets give a hand-soldering limit in the region of 300 to 350 °C for about three seconds per terminal, often once only, and a reflow profile peaking near 260 °C. Exceeding those can discolor the lens, crack the package or damage the bond wires, so keep the iron moving and each touch short.

Why doesn't my SMD LED light after soldering?

The most common causes are reversed polarity, a dry joint on one pad, a solder bridge across the two pads, or heat damage. Test in diode mode across the LED on the board: no reading both ways suggests an open joint or dead LED, a reading near zero suggests a bridge. Also check the current-limiting resistor and that the circuit is actually driving the LED.

Should I use hot air for SMD LEDs?

Hot air is useful for removing LEDs and for parts with thermal pads, but it must be gentle. Use a low airflow so the tiny part doesn't blow away, keep the nozzle moving, and heat the board around the LED rather than blasting the lens. Silicone-lens LEDs, common in 5050 strips, are easy to damage with too much heat or with tweezers pressing on top.
