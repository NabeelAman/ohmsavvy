FNIRSI DPOS350P vs 2C53P Plus

FNIRSI DPOS350P vs 2C53P Plus compares two FNIRSI-made all-in-ones that both run on a battery and both include a signal generator, yet aim at different places. The DPOS350P is a 7-inch bench tablet: a 350 MHz two-channel scope, a 50 MHz generator, a spectrum analyzer and a frequency response analyzer. The 2C53P Plus, sold under the WESTOBiG store name, is a 360 g handheld with a 50 MHz scope, a 10 MHz generator, a 19999-count multimeter and a carrying case. Pick the DPOS350P for bench analysis, the 2C53P Plus for a tool bag.

Spec by spec

The speed charts use a log scale because the gaps are large. The weight chart runs the other way: the handheld is about a quarter of the tablet's weight.

Samples per cycle at full bandwidth tell you how well each scope draws its fastest signal. The 2C53P Plus has 250 MS/s for 50 MHz, five samples per cycle. The DPOS350P has 1 GSa/s for 350 MHz, about 2.9 samples per cycle, and FNIRSI doesn't say how the rate divides with both channels on. Expect the DPOS350P to draw a 300 MHz sine as a sketch that relies on interpolation, and to be at its best below about 100 MHz. See sample rate explained for why that ratio matters.

FNIRSI DPOS350P vs 2C53P: what each extra instrument replaces

Both are sold as several tools in one, but they replace different tools. The DPOS350P replaces bench analyzers that cost more than it does. The 2C53P Plus replaces the multimeter already in your bag.

Frequency response analyzer: the DPOS350P's real extra

FNIRSI lists a sweep from 100 Hz to 50 MHz with 20 to 500 points. In use, the generator drives the input of a filter, amplifier or speaker crossover, a channel reads the output, and the unit plots gain against frequency. Without it you would step a generator by hand and read amplitudes off the screen at each point, which takes most of an hour for a 20-point curve. The spectrum analyzer covers 200 kHz to 350 MHz with a 32K-point FFT and a 3D waterfall, useful for finding which harmonic of a switching supply is leaking or where a clock spur sits.

Multimeter: the 2C53P Plus's real extra

The meter reads AC and DC voltage, current, capacitance, resistance, diode, continuity and temperature at 19999 counts, through its own probe set. On a service call that means one device checks the battery voltage, the fuse's continuity and the sensor waveform. The listing doesn't give the meter's ranges, accuracy or a CAT rating, so treat it as a convenience meter for low-voltage work rather than a replacement for a rated electrician's meter.

Two generators, two jobs

The 2C53P Plus's 10 MHz source is plenty for audio, PWM and simulating a sensor. The DPOS350P's 50 MHz sine with ±2.5 V offset and 0–5 Vpp reaches into IF filters and fast logic clocks, and it feeds the response analyzer. If you only ever inject a 1 kHz tone, the extra range buys nothing.

What each one can and can't measure

On the 2C53P Plus, the three-to-five-times rule puts the comfortable range at roughly 10–17 MHz: microcontroller clocks at the low end, I2C, UART, slower SPI, ignition and injector waveforms, and audio. It can't show a 50 MHz clock as anything better than a rounded sine, and FNIRSI doesn't state its memory depth, so long captures are an unknown. On the DPOS350P, the scope side is comfortable to about 70–100 MHz given its sample rate, the analyzers extend reach to 350 MHz in the frequency domain, and it has no meter at all.

Battery units and mains

Running on battery with the charger unplugged, both float free of mains earth, which avoids the ground-clip short a bench scope can cause. That doesn't make mains safe to probe: the 400 V figures on both listings are input protection, not CAT ratings, and neither listing gives one. Don't probe mains while charging, and use a differential probe or a rated meter for line voltage. If a battery swells or gets hot while charging, stop using the unit.

The trust question: Amazon-only paperwork

Both specs come from FNIRSI's Amazon listings; FNIRSI's site shows the DPOS350P's specs only as images, and the 2C53P Plus is sold under a separate WESTOBiG store with a FNIRSI bezel. Neither listing gives memory depth, trigger types or the two-channel sample rate. The DPOS350P listing also contradicts itself on charging time (about 5 hours in the spec table, 3 hours in a promo panel), and it has been on sale only since April 2025. Buy either from a seller with an easy return window, and test the features you bought it for in the first week.

  • ✓You want to plot a filter or amplifier frequency response without a separate analyzer

Choose the WESTOBiG 2C53P Plus if…

  • ✓Your signals stay under about 10 MHz: sensors, audio, PWM, car circuits

  • ✓You want a carrying case and meter probes in the box

Neither if…

You need serial decoding, published memory depth or a CAT-rated meter for mains work. Neither listing states any of those.

The bottom line

The FNIRSI DPOS350P is the stronger instrument for bench analysis: 350 MHz, a 50 MHz generator, a spectrum analyzer and a frequency response analyzer on a 7-inch screen. The WESTOBiG 2C53P Plus is the better carry tool: a 50 MHz scope, 10 MHz generator and 19999-count meter in 360 g with a case. Both rely on Amazon-only specs with gaps.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 2C53P Plus the same as the FNIRSI 2C53P?+

The listing is sold by the WESTOBiG store, names FNIRSI as maker, shows a bezel printed FNIRSI and 2C53P, and bundles a carrying case, which is what Plus refers to. The specs on the listing (50 MHz, 250 MS/s, 19999-count meter, 10 MHz generator, 4.3-inch screen) are what we compare here.

Does the FNIRSI DPOS350P have a multimeter?+

No. Its four instruments are the oscilloscope, a DDS generator, a spectrum analyzer and a frequency response analyzer. If you need volts, ohms and continuity in the same unit, the 2C53P Plus has a 19999-count meter with its own probes. Otherwise you would pair the DPOS350P with a separate meter.

Can the 2C53P Plus do a frequency response sweep?+

Not as a built-in function. It has a 10 MHz generator and a two-channel scope, so you can step the generator by hand and read the output amplitude at each frequency. The DPOS350P automates that: its analyzer sweeps 100 Hz to 50 MHz with 20 to 500 points and plots the curve.

Which has the longer battery life?+

The listings say about 4 hours for the 2C53P Plus on its 4000 mAh battery and about 3 hours for the DPOS350P on 8000 mAh, which powers a bigger screen and more circuitry at 10 W. The DPOS350P charges from a QC18W 12 V adapter; its listing disagrees with itself on charge time.

Is 350 MHz on the DPOS350P useful with a 1 GSa/s sample rate?+

Partly. At 350 MHz, 1 GSa/s gives about 2.9 samples per cycle, enough to show the frequency but not a clean shape. The scope works best below about 100 MHz. The spectrum analyzer, which uses FFT, makes better use of the full 350 MHz range for finding frequencies rather than drawing waveforms.
