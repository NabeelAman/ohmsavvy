Fluke 116 vs Fluke 117: Which Is Better for Electricians?

For most electricians the Fluke 117 is the better meter: it has VoltAlert non-contact detection and 6 A and 10 A current ranges, and the Fluke 116 has neither. The 116 is Fluke's HVAC model, trading the amp ranges for a 600.0 µA range and a thermocouple input. Both share the same case, CAT III 600V rating, True RMS core and AutoVolt LoZ input, so this Fluke 116 vs Fluke 117 comparison comes down to amps and NCV against microamps and temperature.

The difference is in the dial and the input jacks: Fluke built the 117 around building wiring and the 116 around gas heating equipment. Put those two charts together and the meters barely overlap. The 116's current input tops out at 0.0006 A, which is below the 117's smallest displayed step of 0.001 A. One reads tiny control-circuit currents, the other reads loads.

What each current input can see

The diagram below puts both current ranges on one logarithmic axis and marks three currents an electrician meets. A flame rod sits a few microamps from zero, inside the 116's window and invisible to the 117. A 4–20 mA process loop sits in the gap between them: it overloads the 116, and on the 117 a 12 mA reading shows as 0.012 A with ±(1.0% + 3) DC accuracy, roughly ±3 mA, too coarse to calibrate a transmitter. A loaded 15 A branch circuit sits past the 117's 10.00 A continuous limit.

The practical lesson is that neither meter is the right amp tool for most electrical work. Branch-circuit and motor currents belong to a clamp meter, and loop currents to a meter with a real milliamp range; our 4–20 mA loop testing guide lists those. The 117's amp ranges still earn their place for small loads, such as a bathroom fan, a doorbell transformer or a low-voltage lighting run. The 116 cannot take those readings at all.

The electrician's day with each meter

Picture a service call for a dead kitchen circuit. With the 117, you sweep the receptacles with VoltAlert to find the last live box, switch to AutoVolt to see whether the 40-odd volts on the dead leg is coupling or a loose neutral, prove dead with the leads, then check continuity on the pigtails. The 116 does every step except the first: AutoVolt and LoZ are on its dial too, so ghost voltage fools neither meter. Without VoltAlert, the 116 owner touches leads to each box or pulls a pen tester from a pocket.

Next comes a lighting retrofit where you want to confirm a fixture draws what its label says. The 117 reads it in series on the 6.000 A range. The 116 can't, so you clamp it with a second tool. Then a rooftop unit won't fire. Here the 116 pulls ahead: it reads the flame-rod microamps and the supply-air temperature with the included probe, where the 117 can only confirm 24 V at the gas valve.

Why VoltAlert matters more than it sounds

VoltAlert is a capacitive sensor at the top of the 117. Fluke gives it two sensitivities: Lo for flush receptacles, power strips and cords, and Hi for recessed sockets and bare conductors from 24 V. Its value for an electrician isn't precision; it is speed and habit. You check every box you open before your hands go in, without unholstering the leads. On the 116 that check needs a separate non-contact tester, and a second tool is one more thing to leave in the truck.

LoZ is a tie, not a tiebreaker

Some buyers assume the electrician's model has features the HVAC model lacks across the board. On ghost voltage it doesn't: Fluke lists AutoVolt with a low-impedance input of about 3 kΩ on both the 116 and the 117, with the same ±(2.0% + 3) accuracy on the 600.0 V range. That matters on long thermostat cables as much as on shared conduit. The meter that lacks LoZ in this family is the 115. For how phantom voltage forms, read our guide to measuring voltage on a floating circuit.

Where the 116 earns a place in an electrician's bag

Electricians who also service residential heating, or who work in facilities where boilers and air handlers are part of the job, run into the two readings only the 116 can take. A gas furnace that lights and then shuts down within seconds often points to a weak flame signal, and the only way to see it is a microamp reading in series with the flame rod. Our guide to measuring microamps walks through the hookup. Temperature checks on supply air or a pipe are the other job, and the 116 ships with the 80BK probe to do them.

What you give up is the A jack. The only current range Fluke lists for the 116 is the 600.0 µA one, so every amp reading on that kit goes to a clamp. Many HVAC technicians carry exactly that pair. An electrician who reads small loads in series most weeks will miss the 117's 10 A range more than they gain from microamps. If you want temperature without losing amps, the Fluke 117 vs Fluke 179 page covers a Fluke that has both.

Fluke 116 vs 117 for HVAC work

Swap the electrician for an HVAC technician and the scorecard changes, though less than you might expect. Both meters share several readings HVAC calls depend on: run-capacitor microfarads (1 nF to 9,999 µF at ±(1.9% + 2) on each), 24 V control voltage with a LoZ position for idle thermostat conductors, and pilot thermocouple millivolts on the same 600.0 mV DC range. What splits them is the season. A heating call leans on the 116's microamps and Type K input; the 117 answers with VoltAlert at the condenser disconnect and a 6.000 A range that reads heat-anticipator and other small control currents in series.

Safety: both meters stop at CAT III 600V

The 116 and 117 are rated CAT III 600V, which covers panels, feeders and branch circuits but not the meter base, service drop or outdoor service conductors, where CAT IV applies. VoltAlert can miss a live conductor in shielded cable and cannot sense DC, so treat silence as "not proven" and confirm dead with the leads on a known live source before and after. Never clip a thermocouple probe to an energized part, and never put either meter in series with a circuit that could exceed its current input; the 116's µA jack is for flame and control circuits only. If you're not qualified for live work, call a licensed electrician.

Choose the Fluke 117 if…

  • ✓You wire and troubleshoot branch circuits, receptacles, lighting and panels
  • ✓You want a non-contact voltage sensor built into the meter you already carry
  • ✓You need to read load current through the leads, up to 10 A

Choose the Fluke 116 if…

  • ✓Your electrical work leans toward furnaces, boilers and HVAC controls
  • ✓You need to read flame-sensor microamps and thermocouple temperature
  • ✓You already carry a clamp meter for every amp reading

The bottom line

Electricians should buy the Fluke 117: VoltAlert and the 6 A and 10 A ranges serve wiring work every day, and it shares the 116's LoZ input, True RMS core and CAT III 600V rating. Buy the Fluke 116 only if furnaces, boilers or HVAC controls make up a real share of your calls, and carry a clamp meter with it for amps.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between the Fluke 116 and 117?+

The 117 is built for electricians: it adds VoltAlert non-contact detection and 6 A and 10 A current ranges. The 116 is built for HVAC: it swaps those for a 600.0 µA range at 0.1 µA and a Type K temperature input, with an 80BK probe in the box. Voltage, resistance, capacitance, LoZ and the case are shared.

Does the Fluke 116 have non-contact voltage detection?+

No. Fluke's 116 product page and the 110 Series datasheet list VoltAlert only on the Fluke 117. If you carry a 116 for wiring work, add a separate non-contact pen tester for quick checks, and still prove circuits dead with the test leads before touching anything.

Can the Fluke 116 measure amps?+

Only microamps. Its single current input reads up to 600.0 µA AC or DC with 0.1 µA resolution, which suits flame sensors and small control currents. There is no milliamp or amp range, so motor, compressor or appliance current needs a clamp meter. The 117 reads up to 10 A through its A jack.

Do both the Fluke 116 and 117 have LoZ?+

Yes. Both have an AutoVolt dial position that selects AC or DC automatically and drops the input to about 3 kΩ, which drains capacitively coupled ghost voltage. Fluke gives the same ±(2.0% + 3) accuracy on its 600.0 V range for both. In the 110 Series, the 115 is the model without LoZ.

Can an electrician use the Fluke 116 instead of the 117?+

Yes, for voltage, resistance, continuity, capacitance and ghost-voltage checks, since those functions match the 117. You lose built-in non-contact detection and any way to read load current through the leads. Electricians who already carry a clamp meter and a pen tester can manage, but most are better served by the 117.

Can the Fluke 117 test a furnace flame sensor?+

Not usefully. Flame-sensor current is typically a few microamps, and the 117's finest current step is 1 mA, so the display reads zero. Fluke designed the 116 for this job with its 600.0 µA range at 0.1 µA resolution. HVAC clamp meters with a µA range, such as the Klein CL445, also do it.

Is the Fluke 116 or 117 better for HVAC?+

For technicians who service gas furnaces, the 116: it reads flame-rod current to 0.1 µA and Type K temperature with the included 80BK probe, and the 117 can do neither. On cooling calls the two are close, since both test run capacitors and have LoZ for 24 V controls. Carry a clamp meter with either for compressor and blower amps.

Are the Fluke 116 and 117 the same size?+

Yes. Fluke lists both at 167 × 84 × 46 mm and 550 g, running on one 9V battery with about 400 hours of typical life without the backlight. Both carry a 3-year warranty and the same CAT III 600V rating, so holsters, hangers and accessories for one fit the other.
