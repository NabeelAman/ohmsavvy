Rigol DS1054Z vs Siglent SDS1104X-E

Buy the Rigol DS1054Z if your signals stay below about 10 MHz and you want the cheapest proven four-channel bench scope; buy the Siglent SDS1104X-E if you work with faster clocks, SPI or CAN buses, or want serial decoding without paying for options. Both have four channels, 1 GSa/s and a 7-inch screen. The Siglent doubles the bandwidth to 100 MHz, keeps 500 MSa/s per channel with all four on against the Rigol's 250 MSa/s, and includes I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN decoding as standard.

DS1054Z vs SDS1104X-E spec by spec

The headline "1 GSa/s" is the same on both boxes, but it only applies with one channel running. Four-channel scopes earn their keep with all four probes connected, and that is where the two split.

A common rule of thumb is to pick a scope with three to five times the bandwidth of the fastest fundamental you need to see with recognizable edges. By that rule the DS1054Z is comfortable to roughly 10 to 15 MHz, and the SDS1104X-E to roughly 20 to 30 MHz. An Arduino Uno's 16 MHz clock sits right at the Rigol's edge; its PWM, UART and I2C traffic, all well below 1 MHz, are easy for either.

What 50 MHz vs 100 MHz does to an edge

A scope's front end behaves like a low-pass filter. Every edge you feed it comes out slower, by roughly 0.35 divided by the bandwidth. On a 20 MHz clock, each half cycle lasts 25 ns, so a 7 ns rounded edge eats a noticeable share of it, while 3.5 ns keeps the trace looking square. That matters when you measure rise time, overshoot or setup-and-hold margins on fast logic; it does not matter for audio or a 1 kHz PWM signal.

Measured rise time includes the scope

If a signal's true edge is close to the scope's own rise time, the reading is inflated. A common correction is root-sum-of-squares: a real 5 ns edge measured through a 7 ns scope shows about √(5² + 7²) ≈ 8.6 ns, and through a 3.5 ns scope about 6.1 ns. For edge measurements, the faster front end is closer to the truth. Our guide to measuring rise time with cursors walks through the method.

Serial decoding: included or extra

Decoding turns a burst of ones and zeros into readable bytes and addresses on screen. Siglent lists I2C, SPI, UART/RS232, CAN and LIN decoding as free on the SDS1000X-E series. Rigol lists RS232/UART, I2C and SPI trigger and decode for the 1000Z as options, and no CAN or LIN. If you debug sensor buses, automotive CAN or a misbehaving UART link, the Siglent arrives ready; on the Rigol, factor the decode option into the cost.

Safety: neither scope is for direct mains probing

Both are mains-powered bench scopes whose probe ground clips connect to protective earth. Clipping a ground lead to a non-ground point on a mains-referenced circuit, such as the primary side of an offline power supply, creates a short through the probe and can destroy the probe, the scope or the circuit. Use an isolation transformer on the device under test or a differential probe, and never defeat the scope's earth ground.

The bottom line

Four channels and a 1 GSa/s peak are common ground. The Rigol DS1054Z remains the practical first scope for students and hobbyists whose signals stay below about 10 MHz. The Siglent SDS1104X-E earns its higher price with 100 MHz, twice the per-channel sample rate with all channels on, and free serial decoding, so buy it if you work on fast logic, buses or switching supplies.

Frequently asked questions

Is 50 MHz enough bandwidth for a beginner oscilloscope?+

For most beginner work, yes. Audio, PWM, UART, I2C, power-supply ripple and most microcontroller signals sit well below 10 MHz, where a 50 MHz scope like the DS1054Z draws clean edges. You outgrow it with fast clocks, high-speed SPI, FPGA I/O or RF, where the SDS1104X-E's 100 MHz shows more of the true edge.

Does the Siglent SDS1104X-E include serial decoding?+

Yes. Siglent lists I2C, SPI, UART/RS232, CAN and LIN decoding as standard on the SDS1000X-E series. On the Rigol DS1054Z, RS232/UART, I2C and SPI trigger and decode are sold as options, and Rigol's 1000Z page doesn't list CAN or LIN decoding.

Can the Rigol DS1054Z's bandwidth be upgraded past 50 MHz?+

Rigol sells and specifies the DS1054Z as a 50 MHz scope, and its 1000Z page lists no official bandwidth upgrade, only an extended warranty option. Plan around 50 MHz. If you need more bandwidth with full manufacturer support, buy a scope rated higher as sold, such as the 100 MHz SDS1104X-E.

Why does sample rate drop when I turn on more channels?+

Both scopes share their converters between channels. With one channel on, all of it goes to that channel for 1 GSa/s. With three or four on, the Rigol drops to 250 MSa/s per channel and the Siglent to 500 MSa/s. For a 20 MHz signal that means 12.5 versus 25 samples per cycle.

Which has more memory, the DS1054Z or the SDS1104X-E?+

On paper the Rigol: 12 Mpts standard and 24 Mpts as an option, against the Siglent's 7 Mpts per channel or 14 Mpts interleaved. Deep memory keeps the sample rate high on long time bases. The Siglent offsets this with segmented sequence capture and an 80,000-frame history for rare events.
