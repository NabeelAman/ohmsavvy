Fluke 787B vs Fluke 789: Which ProcessMeter Is Better?

The Fluke 789 is the better ProcessMeter if you ever work on 2-wire transmitters away from a powered panel: Fluke's data sheet lists a 24 V loop power supply and a HART mode that adds a 250 Ω resistor as 789-only features. Everything else Fluke publishes is shared. Both are 1000 V True RMS multimeters with the same accuracy table, both source or simulate 0–20 or 4–20 mA at 0.05% of span with 1,200 Ω drive, and both share the step and ramp modes, CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating, battery life and 3-year warranty. If you only feed signals into PLC inputs and valves, the 787B does that job.

  • →HART: the 789 has a HART mode that adds a 250 Ω resistor in series with its loop supply; Fluke lists nothing similar for the 787B.

Two ProcessMeters, one spec table

Fluke sells these as a pair and describes the split in one sentence of its data sheet: whether you only need to source and simulate mA, or need a 24 V loop power supply, there is a ProcessMeter for you. The data sheet then prints a single accuracy table for both models and a short list headed "789 additional features" with two entries, the loop supply and the HART resistor. That is the whole difference in measuring and output capability.

Source accuracy works out the same on both: 0.05% of a 16 mA span (4–20 mA) is ±0.008 mA, and on the 0–20 mA span it is ±0.010 mA.

One line on Fluke's 787B page to read carefully

The 787B product page has a feature bullet that says "Measure/Source/Simulate loop currents while powering the loop". Its own spec table lists no loop supply, and Fluke's joint data sheet marks the 24 V loop power supply as "789 only". We follow the data sheet: the 787B can drive a passive input from its internal source, but it can't power a 2-wire transmitter.

Powering a transmitter on the bench

A 2-wire transmitter takes its power from the same pair of wires that carries its 4–20 mA signal, so it needs a DC supply in the loop before it outputs anything. In a plant, that supply lives in the control system. On a bench, in a shop, or when a new instrument arrives before the panel is energized, it doesn't exist yet, and that is the moment the two ProcessMeters part ways.

With a 787B you wire a separate 24 V supply, a 250 Ω resistor and the 787B on its mA measure range in series with the transmitter, then clip the HART communicator across the resistor. With a 789 you connect the transmitter to the meter, select loop power, and switch in the internal 250 Ω resistor; the 789 supplies the loop and reads the current the transmitter draws, with % of span on the second line of the display.

The resistor costs voltage, so check the budget. At 20 mA, 250 Ω drops 250 × 0.020 = 5 V, leaving about 19 V of a 24 V supply for the transmitter and the test leads. At 4 mA the drop is only 1 V. Most 2-wire transmitters need somewhere around 10 to 12 V at their terminals to operate, but that figure varies by model, so confirm the minimum supply voltage in your transmitter's data sheet before relying on either setup.

Jobs where the 787B is all you need

Much loop work never touches a transmitter's power. Sourcing 4, 12 and 20 mA into a PLC or DCS analog input to check scaling and alarms uses the internal source, which both meters drive into up to 1,200 Ω. Stroking a valve through an I/P converter or positioner is the same. Simulating a transmitter inside a live loop, so you can check the wiring and the input card with the field device disconnected, uses the plant's own 15–48 V supply, which both meters accept. Reading loop current in series, with % of span alongside, is also identical.

Safety: loop wiring often shares a cabinet with mains

4–20 mA loops run at 24 V, but the terminal strips and marshalling cabinets they pass through often carry 120 V or higher control power. Both ProcessMeters are rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V; use leads rated to match, and choose the dial position and output mode before the leads touch anything, so the meter is never sourcing into a circuit you meant only to measure. Breaking a loop to measure in series can drive a valve or trip a shutdown, so get the operator's permission and put the loop in manual or bypass first. In hazardous (classified) areas, use only equipment and permits your site allows; neither meter is described by Fluke as intrinsically safe.

Choose the Fluke 789 if…

  • ✓You bench-test, configure or commission 2-wire transmitters and want the meter to power them
  • ✓You use a HART communicator and don't want to carry a loose 250 Ω resistor and a separate supply
  • ✓You want the two-level backlight and the optional IR3000FC Fluke Connect logging Fluke lists for the 789

Choose the Fluke 787B if…

  • ✓Your mA work is sourcing into PLC analog inputs, I/P converters and valve positioners
  • ✓You simulate transmitters inside live loops that already have a 15–48 V control-system supply
  • ✓You don't use HART, or you already carry a loop supply and resistor in your kit

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 789 if you work on 2-wire transmitters away from a powered control panel or use HART: its built-in 24 V loop supply and switchable 250 Ω resistor replace a separate supply and resistor. Buy the Fluke 787B if your mA work is sourcing into PLC inputs and valves or simulating transmitters in live loops; Fluke publishes identical measurement, source and simulate specs, safety rating, battery life and warranty for both.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Fluke 787B and 789?+

Fluke's joint data sheet lists two 789-only features: a 24 V loop power supply and a HART mode that adds a 250 Ω resistor. Everything in the spec table is shared, including DMM accuracy, the 30 mA range, 0.05%-of-span source and simulate, 1,200 Ω drive, step and ramp modes, battery life and the CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating.

Can the Fluke 787B power a 4-20 mA transmitter?+

No. Fluke's data sheet marks the 24 V loop power supply as 789 only, even though one bullet on the 787B product page mentions powering the loop. The 787B can source current into passive inputs and simulate a transmitter in a loop that already has a 15–48 V supply, but a 2-wire transmitter on the bench needs an external supply.

Does the Fluke 787B have a HART resistor?+

Fluke doesn't list one. The HART mode, which switches a 250 Ω resistor in series with the loop supply, is listed only for the 789. With a 787B, carry a separate 250 Ω resistor and a supply when you need a HART communicator to talk to a transmitter that isn't in a powered loop.

Are the Fluke 787B and 789 equally accurate?+

Yes, according to Fluke's single spec table: DC volts 0.1% + 1, True RMS AC volts 0.7% + 2, the 30.000 mA range at 0.05% + 2, resistance 0.2% + 1, and source and simulate at 0.05% of span. On a 4–20 mA span, 0.05% works out to ±0.008 mA from either meter.

How long do the batteries last on the 787B and 789?+

Fluke's data sheet gives the same figures for both: about 140 hours measuring and about 10 hours sourcing 12 mA, on four AA cells. Fluke's 789 page quotes the 10 hours at 20 mA. Simulating draws on the loop's own supply, so on a long commissioning day, simulate rather than source wherever the plant supply is available.

Can either ProcessMeter measure motor current?+

Only small currents. Both have a 1.000 A range rated 0.440 A continuous and no 10 A input, so motor, heater and contactor currents need a clamp meter. Both still measure AC and DC volts to 1000 V as True RMS, which covers control power and supply checks inside a panel.
