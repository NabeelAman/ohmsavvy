HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Set Volts per Division on an Oscilloscope

To set volts per division on an oscilloscope, turn the channel's vertical scale knob until the waveform fills about six of the screen's eight vertical divisions: divide the signal's peak-to-peak voltage by six and pick the next standard step up. Before you do, set the channel's probe setting to match the probe (1x or 10x), or every number on the screen will be off by a factor of ten. This guide covers how to change the setting on a typical digital scope, how to choose the right step for a given signal, and when to use fine adjustment, offset and AC coupling instead of just turning the knob.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 24, 2026

On bench scopes like the Rigol DS1054Z, each channel shares one vertical scale knob: select the channel, then turn. For reading the result, see how to read an oscilloscope.

Before you probe anything powered

  • Start at the least sensitive setting when you don't know the signal's size, especially on power circuits. A V/div that's too sensitive doesn't hurt the scope; a voltage above its rated input can.
  • The ground clip on a mains-powered bench scope is tied to earth. Never clip it to a mains-referenced point; use a differential probe or an isolated, battery-powered handheld rated for the job.

What the volts-per-division setting controls

The screen of almost every oscilloscope is a grid of 10 horizontal by 8 vertical divisions. The vertical scale setting, written V/div, says how many volts each of those vertical divisions is worth. At 1 V/div a 5 V signal stands five divisions tall; at 2 V/div the same signal is two and a half divisions. The setting moves in a 1-2-5 sequence: 1 mV, 2 mV, 5 mV, 10 mV, 20 mV, 50 mV, 100 mV and so on, up to several volts per division.

The setting does more than scale the picture. It selects the input attenuator and amplifier gain, which decides how the signal is spread across the analog-to-digital converter. An 8-bit converter has 256 levels in total, roughly 25 to 32 per division depending on the model. A waveform two divisions tall uses only about 50 to 64 of them, so it looks stepped and noisy; one six divisions tall uses roughly 150 to 190, giving smoother traces and more precise automatic measurements.

Accuracy follows the same logic. Scope makers usually state DC gain accuracy as a percentage of full scale, and entry-level instruments commonly quote a few percent; check your datasheet for the exact figure. A percentage of full scale is a fixed number of millivolts at a given setting, so it is a small share of a waveform that fills the screen and a large share of one that is only a division tall. The habit of filling about six divisions therefore improves both the picture and the numbers. When you need better than a few percent on a DC level, a multimeter is the better instrument; see why a scope and a multimeter disagree.

1Match the probe setting first

Look at the switch on the probe body. If it says 10x, open the channel menu and set the probe or attenuation to 10x as well. The scope then multiplies everything by ten, so the V/div readout and every measurement show true circuit volts. Skip this, and you'll set a V/div that seems right but is ten times off. Most general-purpose probes ship with a 1x/10x switch; use 10x for most work because it loads the circuit less and extends the probe's bandwidth. If the square-wave calibrator output looks rounded or peaky, compensate the probe before trusting any amplitude; see how to compensate an oscilloscope probe.

2Estimate the peak-to-peak voltage

You usually know roughly what to expect: a 5 V logic line, a 12 V rail, a 1 V audio signal. Divide the expected peak-to-peak by six to get the ideal V/div, then round up to the next standard step. A 3.3 V logic signal gives 3.3 ÷ 6 = 0.55, which rounds up to 1 V/div (3.3 divisions) or, if you're happy with less headroom, 0.5 V/div (6.6 divisions). A 24 V peak-to-peak motor drive signal gives 4, which rounds up to 5 V/div.

If you don't know, press Auto or Autoscale. It picks V/div, timebase and trigger automatically. It is a good starting point, not a final answer: autoscale sometimes chooses a setting that hides small details or triggers on the wrong edge.

3Turn the vertical scale knob

Press the channel button to select it, then turn that channel's vertical scale knob. On bench scopes with one set of vertical knobs, it adjusts whichever channel is selected. On handhelds and small scopes such as the FNIRSI DSO152, the vertical scale is a menu item or an up/down button pair. Watch the readout change at the screen edge; it typically shows the channel number and value, such as 1 500mV.

4Use fine adjustment when a step is too coarse

Sometimes the waveform fills 3 divisions at one step and runs off screen at the next. Fine adjustment, also called vernier, lets you pick values between the standard steps. On many digital scopes, pressing the vertical scale knob toggles between coarse and fine. Use it to stretch a waveform across the screen for a better look, but remember that odd values like 0.73 V/div are harder to convert in your head. Automatic measurements and cursors still report true volts whichever mode you use.

5Center the trace with position or offset

The vertical position knob moves the trace up and down so it sits where you want it. A 0 to 5 V logic signal at 1 V/div fills the top five divisions if its ground sits at center; moving the ground marker down two and a half divisions centers the whole waveform. On many scopes, pressing the position knob returns the trace to center.

When a small signal sits on a big DC level, like 50 mV of ripple on a 12 V rail, position alone can't help at a sensitive V/div. Switch the channel to AC coupling, which removes the DC level, or use DC coupling with vertical offset if your scope allows enough offset at that setting. Turning V/div down to 10 mV with a 12 V rail on a DC-coupled input overdrives the front end; see how to avoid overdriving an oscilloscope.

6Verify the setting with a measurement

Count divisions and multiply by V/div, then compare with the scope's own peak-to-peak measurement. If the trace is 4.2 divisions tall at 1 V/div, you expect 4.2 V; if the automatic Vpp says 42 V or 0.42 V, the probe setting is wrong. If the measurement shows a warning symbol or a greater-than sign, part of the waveform is off screen and the setting is too sensitive.

Six divisions, then refine

Fill about six divisions, match the probe setting, and check the reading against a number you can predict. Those three habits fix nearly every vertical-scale problem.

Frequently asked questions

How do I change volts per division on an oscilloscope?

Select the channel you want, usually by pressing its numbered button, then turn the vertical scale knob for that channel. Clockwise normally makes the setting more sensitive (fewer volts per division, taller trace). The current value is shown in the channel readout at the bottom or side of the screen, for example 1.00V or 500mV next to the channel number.

What is the best volts per division setting?

There's no single best setting; it depends on the signal. Aim for the waveform to fill about six of the eight vertical divisions. That uses most of the converter's resolution while leaving headroom so peaks and overshoot aren't clipped. Divide the expected peak-to-peak voltage by six and round up to the next 1-2-5 step.

Why are my oscilloscope voltages ten times too small?

Almost always the probe is switched to 10x while the scope channel is set to 1x. The probe divides the signal by ten, and the scope doesn't multiply it back. Set the channel's probe or attenuation menu to 10x, or switch the probe to 1x, so they match. The V/div readout then shows true circuit volts.

What does fine adjustment of V/div do?

Fine or vernier adjustment lets you set values between the standard 1-2-5 steps, such as 1.2 V/div, so a waveform can exactly fill the screen. On many digital scopes you enable it by pressing the vertical scale knob. It is handy for comparing amplitudes against graticule lines, but the coarse steps are easier to read in your head.

Should volts per division be the same on every channel?

Not necessarily. Each channel has its own setting so that a 5 V logic signal and a 100 mV sensor signal can both be shown at a useful size. Matching them is helpful when you compare amplitudes by eye or use math functions such as subtraction, because the traces then share one vertical scale.
