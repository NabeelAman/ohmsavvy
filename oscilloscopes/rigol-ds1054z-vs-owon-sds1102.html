Rigol DS1054Z vs OWON SDS1102

Rigol DS1054Z vs OWON SDS1102 is a trade between channels and bandwidth. Both are benchtop scopes with 7-inch displays and 1 GSa/s headline sample rates. The Rigol gives you four channels at 50MHz; the OWON gives you two channels at 100MHz in a slim, light body, but with a short 10K-point record and only edge and video triggers.

Spec by spec

On paper these two pull in opposite directions: the OWON doubles the bandwidth, the Rigol doubles the channel count. The charts below use only the figures each maker publishes, which is why the OWON's sample rate appears once, without a per-channel breakdown.

Both sample fast enough for their bandwidth. At 50MHz the Rigol takes 20 samples per cycle on one channel, or 5 with three or four channels on. If the OWON's 1 GSa/s applies to the channel in use, it takes 10 samples per cycle at 100MHz. All of these are well above the Nyquist minimum of two. For background, see what oscilloscope bandwidth is and what GSa/s means.

What each one can and can't measure

Bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine wave is shown about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its real amplitude. The usual rule of thumb is to buy 3–5 times the frequency you want to see accurately, and more for square waves, whose sharp edges are made of harmonics well above the fundamental. Both scopes clear the bar for the bulk of hobby work; the differences show up at the edges.

Rigol DS1054Z: wide, at moderate speed

With 50MHz the DS1054Z is comfortable to roughly 10–17MHz. That covers UART, I2C and SPI at typical hobby clock rates, PWM motor drives, audio, and many switching regulators. Its strength is breadth: with four channels you can trigger on a chip select and see clock, data and the resulting output in the same capture, which is how most timing faults get found. Its limit is speed. A 20MHz clock or a fast logic edge is past its comfortable range, so rise times and overshoot on those signals can't be trusted, and its review flags it as modest for RF.

OWON SDS1102: faster, but narrower

With 100MHz the SDS1102 is comfortable to roughly 20–33MHz, so faster clocks and edges look closer to their true shape. The trade-offs are elsewhere. Two channels means a four-wire bus can only be seen two lines at a time. Edge and video triggers suit repetitive signals, but there's no pulse-width trigger to catch a single short glitch. And the 10K-point record limits capture time: at 1 GSa/s, 10,000 points fill in 10 µs. To capture a 10 ms event, any scope with 10K points has to drop to about 1 MSa/s, and then the 100MHz bandwidth no longer helps much.

Record length decides your real sample rate

Capture time equals record length divided by sample rate. A scope only holds its top sample rate while the screen's time window fits in memory; beyond that it slows down. With 10K points, the SDS1102 runs at 1 GSa/s only up to a 10 µs window. That is fine for looking at repeating clocks and PWM, and a real limit for one-off events like start-up transients or a burst of serial data.

Mains and high voltage

On a mains-powered bench scope like the Rigol, the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth through the power cord. Clipping it to a live or non-earthed point creates a short through the scope. Treat any mains benchtop the same way, use a differential probe or proper isolation for mains work, and check the OWON's manual for its input ratings, which aren't in our verified data.

The bottom line

For most beginners and hobbyists the Rigol DS1054Z is the better buy: four channels let you see cause and effect across a whole bus, and 50MHz covers most microcontroller, audio and power work. The OWON SDS1102 makes sense when two channels are enough and you want 100MHz of headroom in a slim, light body, as long as a 10K-point record and edge and video triggers suit your measurements.

Frequently asked questions

Is the OWON SDS1102 better than the Rigol DS1054Z because it has 100MHz?+

Only for faster signals. Its 100MHz bandwidth keeps signals up to roughly 20–33MHz accurate, against roughly 10–17MHz on the Rigol. But it has two channels instead of four, a 10K-point record and only edge and video triggers. For most hobby work below 10MHz, the Rigol's extra channels matter more than the extra bandwidth.

Do both scopes really sample at 1 GSa/s?+

Both quote 1 GSa/s. Rigol's figure is for a single channel and falls to 250 MSa/s per channel with three or four channels on. OWON quotes 1 GSa/s real-time for the SDS1102, but the specs we verified don't state whether that rate is shared when both channels run.

Is 10K points of record length enough?+

For repetitive signals like clocks, PWM and audio tones, usually yes, because the scope redraws them continuously. For one-off events it is limiting: at 1 GSa/s, 10K points cover only 10 µs, so longer windows force the sample rate down. If you plan to capture start-up transients or long serial bursts, look for deeper memory.

Can the OWON SDS1102 decode I2C or SPI?+

Not according to OWON's specifications: it lists no serial decoding, no logic channels and only edge and video triggers. You can still view I2C or SPI waveforms two lines at a time and read bits by eye, but a scope with decoding saves a lot of time on bus debugging.

Which is better for a small desk?+

The OWON SDS1102. OWON lists it at 301 × 152 × 70 mm and about 1.1 kg, a slim body for a benchtop scope with a 7-inch display. Our verified data for the Rigol DS1054Z doesn't include its dimensions or weight, so we can't give an exact comparison, but it is a conventional benchtop design.
