Siglent SDS1104X-E vs Rigol DHO814: Speed or Resolution?

Siglent SDS1104X-E vs Rigol DHO814 matches two 100 MHz, four-channel bench scopes that split along one line. The SDS1104X-E keeps 500 MSa/s per channel with all four on and updates at up to 100,000 waveforms a second; the DHO814 drops to 312.5 MSa/s but quantizes in 12 bits instead of 8, on a 1024 × 600 touchscreen. Fast edges favor the Siglent; small signals favor the Rigol.

Spec by spec

The charts trade off against each other on purpose. The Siglent wins the speed figures with all channels on; the Rigol wins detail and memory.

With four channels on, 500 MSa/s gives 5 samples per cycle at the full 100 MHz bandwidth, and 312.5 MSa/s gives about 3. Both reconstruct sine waves with interpolation, but on a 20 MHz clock that is 25 samples per period against about 16, and the edges of a fast square wave show the difference first. See sample rate explained for why the ratio matters.

What each one can and can't measure

Siglent SDS1104X-E: speed across four channels

Siglent's own application note explains why the SDS1104X-E keeps 500 MSa/s with all four channels on: it uses two analog-to-digital converters, one per channel pair. That makes it the better four-channel scope for digital timing work. Its weak point is small signals. At 10 mV/div the 8-bit converter steps in about 0.31 mV, so a 1 mV ripple is drawn as three or four levels and noise looks blocky. It is also the one with an upgrade path: a 16-channel logic option and Bode plot control of an external Siglent generator.

Rigol DHO814: detail on a slower four-channel clock

The same 10 mV/div range on the DHO814's 12-bit converter steps in about 0.02 mV, so the 1 mV ripple has around fifty levels to draw with. That matters for supply ripple, sensor outputs and audio, where the signal you care about is small. Its 25 Mpts memory also holds longer captures with one channel on. What it gives up is four-channel sample rate, logic inputs and Bode plot. Rigol lists no logic option for the DHO800 series.

12 bits helps most when you can't zoom in

If the small signal sits alone, both scopes can switch to a sensitive range and AC-couple it, and 8 bits is fine. The 12-bit advantage shows when the small detail rides on something large you also need to see, such as ringing on a 12 V switching edge. Then the scale is set by the big signal and the extra bits carry the detail.

Mains-powered scope, earthed probes

The SDS1104X-E runs from mains and its probe grounds connect to earth. Clipping a ground lead to a non-ground point in a mains-powered circuit causes a short through the scope. Use a differential probe for anything mains referenced, on either model.

The question with the DHO814: does 12-bit hold up with four channels on?

The DHO814's resolution doesn't change with channel count, but its sample rate and memory do: 312.5 MSa/s and 5 Mpts each with three or four on. That is still a 16 ms full-rate window, close to the 14 ms the SDS1104X-E's 7 Mpts per channel at 500 MSa/s gives. For most hobby work the DHO814 stays the stronger analog instrument. The SDS1104X-E is the scope with our Hands-on Tested badge; this page doesn't quote bench measurements from it, only Siglent's and Rigol's published figures.

  • ✓You routinely run all four channels on fast digital signals
  • ✓You want the optional 16-channel MSO upgrade path
  • ✓You want Bode plots driven from a Siglent generator
  • ✓You prefer physical buttons to a touchscreen

Choose the Rigol DHO814 if…

  • ✓You measure ripple, noise or small signals riding on large ones
  • ✓You want a 1024 × 600 touchscreen and HDMI output
  • ✓You want a 1.78 kg scope that runs on USB-C power
  • ✓You want deeper memory: 25 Mpts against 14 Mpts at best

Neither if…

You need more than 100 MHz. Both top out there; for faster clocks and edges look at a 200 MHz or 250 MHz model such as the Rigol DHO924S.

The bottom line

Buy the Siglent SDS1104X-E if you run all four channels on fast digital signals, hunt rare glitches or want a logic and Bode plot upgrade path: it keeps 500 MSa/s per channel and updates at 100,000 wfm/s. Buy the Rigol DHO814 if your signals are small, such as supply ripple, sensor outputs or audio: its 12-bit converter resolves 16 times finer steps, with 25 Mpts of memory, a touchscreen and USB-C power.

Frequently asked questions

Is 12-bit worth it over 8-bit on a hobby scope?+

It is when small details sit on large signals, such as ripple on a supply or ringing on a switching edge. At 10 mV/div an 8-bit scope steps in about 0.31 mV and a 12-bit scope in about 0.02 mV. For logic timing, serial decoding and most digital work, 8 bits shows everything you need.

Why does the SDS1104X-E keep 500 MSa/s with four channels on?+

Siglent's application note says the four-channel SDS1000X-E models use two analog-to-digital converters, each shared by a channel pair, so all four channels can run at 500 MSa/s. Rigol's data sheet lists 312.5 MSa/s per channel for the DHO814 with three or four channels on.

Does the DHO814 have a touchscreen and the SDS1104X-E not?+

Yes. Rigol lists a 7-inch 1024 × 600 capacitive multi-touch screen on the DHO814. Siglent lists a 7-inch 800 × 480 TFT-LCD on the SDS1104X-E, operated through buttons and knobs, with 10 one-button shortcuts. Some people prefer knobs at the bench; touch helps when dragging cursors and zooming.

Which one decodes more serial buses?+

Close to a tie. Siglent lists I2C, SPI, UART, RS232, CAN and LIN as free on the SDS1104X-E. Rigol lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C, SPI and CAN as standard on the DHO814. LIN is the Siglent's extra; parallel is the Rigol's. Both decode common microcontroller buses out of the box.

Can either scope run without mains power?+

The DHO814 takes power through USB Type-C at 15 V and 3 A (45 W max), so a USB-C PD source that offers 15 V at that current can run it. The SDS1104X-E takes 100 to 240 V AC per Siglent's data sheet and needs mains or an inverter.
