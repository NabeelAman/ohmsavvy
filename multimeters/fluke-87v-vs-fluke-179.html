Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179

Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179 is the choice between Fluke's two top general-purpose meters. Both are True RMS, rated CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, read temperature and carry a lifetime warranty. The 87V is the industrial troubleshooter, with tighter DC accuracy, a 19,999-count high-resolution mode and 250 µs peak capture; the 179 covers most of the same ground in a lighter package.

Accuracy and range, charted

On paper the gap is about precision and reach rather than safety. A lower DC accuracy figure means a reading closer to the true value; a higher frequency limit lets you check faster signals such as drive carrier frequencies.

What the accuracy gap means at real voltages

A percentage on a spec sheet is easier to judge once it becomes volts. The table applies each meter's published basic accuracy to four everyday readings, using the resolution of the range the reading lands on (0.01 V on the 60 V range, 0.1 V on the 600 V range, 0.01 Hz for frequency). This is worst-case arithmetic from Fluke's figures, not a test result; a meter in calibration is usually well inside its limits.

For checking that a 480 V feeder is within a typical ±5% utilization band (456 V to 504 V), a ±5 V window is perfectly usable, so the 179 does the job. The 87V's edge pays off when you're trending a small change, calibrating a 4-20 mA transmitter loop, or reading a DC sensor where 20 mV matters.

Why drive work favors the 87V

A variable-frequency drive doesn't output a clean sine wave. It switches the DC bus on and off thousands of times a second, varying the pulse width so the motor sees an average that rises and falls at the output frequency. A True RMS meter without filtering reads the energy of those fast edges too, which can push the displayed voltage well away from what the motor responds to. Fluke equips the 87V with a selectable low-pass filter, 3 dB down at 1 kHz, that strips the carrier so the reading tracks the fundamental. Fluke's 179 page lists no such filter.

The 87V's 200 kHz frequency limit and 250 µs peak capture belong to the same toolkit: one reads the carrier itself when you need it, the other catches the brief spikes that trip drives or reset controllers. On a fixed-speed motor or a resistive heater, none of this applies and the 179 reads the circuit just as well.

Same safety rating, different jobs

The CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V rating is identical, so neither meter is "safer" at a service entrance. The choice is about measurement features, not protection. Before probing a drive, isolate and verify per your site's lockout procedure; DC bus capacitors can stay charged after power is removed.

Fluke 87V vs 179 for industrial maintenance

A plant maintenance shift mixes very different jobs: verifying 480 V at a motor control center, chasing a drive that trips once a day, checking a 4-20 mA transmitter, taking a bearing or enclosure temperature, and confirming a fixed-speed motor is drawing sensible current. Both meters carry the same CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V rating, so neither limits where you may probe. What separates them is how many of those jobs each finishes without a second instrument.

Loop work shows how far the 179 actually goes. A 4-20 mA signal spans 16 mA, and the 179's 0.01 mA step is 0.0625% of that span, fine for confirming a transmitter is at 12 mA when it should be. The 87V's finer steps and ±(0.05% + 1) DC volts matter when you are trimming a transmitter or verifying a calibration, not when you are fault-finding a dead loop.

Drives are the clear dividing line. Most modern plants have dozens of variable-frequency drives, and the 87V's low-pass filter plus 250 µs peak capture let one meter read a drive's output properly and catch the transient behind a nuisance trip. The 179's Min/Max-Average will log a sag that lasts long enough to register, but Fluke lists no peak-capture mode or filter for it. On temperature, both cover typical maintenance readings such as motor frames, bearings and panel hot spots; the 87V's wider range only pays off with a suitable probe, since its supplied 80BK is rated −40 °C to 260 °C.

On ruggedness, the Fluke specifications we cite don't give an IP rating or a drop height for either meter, so we don't rank them on it. The 87V is the lighter meter on its own at 355 g, against 420 g for the 179, but rises to 624 g in its holster. When is the 179 enough? If your plant runs mostly fixed-speed motors, lighting, heaters and controls, and drive work goes to a specialist or a scope, the 179 does every job in this table except the drive rows, with the same safety rating and the same lifetime warranty.

Industrial safety still comes first

A CAT IV rating protects you from transients, not from arc flash. Follow your site's lockout/tagout and arc-flash PPE rules at MCCs and switchgear, check test leads match the meter's rating, and verify the meter on a known live source before and after a dead test.

Frequently asked questions

Do the Fluke 87V and 179 have the same safety rating?+

Yes. Fluke rates both CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, so both can be used from the service entrance down to branch circuits.

Which is more accurate, the 87V or the 179?+

The 87V. Fluke lists its basic DC accuracy as ±(0.05% + 1) against ±(0.09% + 2) for the 179, and the 87V adds a 19,999-count high-resolution mode.

Does the Fluke 179 have peak capture?+

Fluke doesn't list it for the 179. The 87V captures transients as short as 250 µs; the 179 instead offers Auto Hold and Min/Max-Average recording.

Which has the better temperature range?+

The 87V reads from −200 °C to 1090 °C (the probe itself limits this; the supplied 80BK covers −40 °C to 260 °C). The 179 reads −40 °C to 400 °C with its included thermocouple.

Is the Fluke 87V worth it over the 179 for HVAC work?+

Usually not. HVAC work rarely needs 0.05% DC accuracy or peak capture, and the 179's temperature probe and Min/Max-Average cover most calls. Choose the 87V if you also service variable-frequency drives.

Is the Fluke 179 good enough for industrial maintenance?+

For many plants, yes. It has the same CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V rating and lifetime warranty as the 87V, True RMS, temperature and 0.01 mA current steps for 4-20 mA loops. What it lacks is the 87V's drive low-pass filter and 250 µs peak capture, so if your shift includes variable-frequency drives or intermittent trips, the 87V is the better tool.

Can the Fluke 179 read a 4-20 mA loop?+

Yes. Its finest current step is 0.01 mA, which is 0.0625% of a 16 mA loop span, enough to confirm a transmitter's output or find a dead loop. The 87V resolves far finer and has tighter DC accuracy, which matters more when you are calibrating or trimming a transmitter than when you are fault-finding.

Are the Fluke 87V and 179 waterproof or drop rated?+

The Fluke specifications we cite don't list an IP rating or a drop height for either meter, so we treat both as not stated and don't rank them on ruggedness. Both are built as daily field meters with lifetime warranties. If you work in wet or washdown areas, check Fluke's current manual or choose a meter with a published IP rating.
