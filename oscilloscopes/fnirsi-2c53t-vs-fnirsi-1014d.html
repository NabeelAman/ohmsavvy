FNIRSI 2C53T vs FNIRSI 1014D

FNIRSI 2C53T vs FNIRSI 1014D is a choice between two shapes of instrument from the same maker. The 2C53T is a 300 g battery handheld that folds a 50 MHz two-channel scope, a 19,999-count True RMS multimeter and a 50 kHz generator into one box. The 1014D is a 100 MHz, 1 GSa/s two-channel benchtop with a 7-inch screen and a DDS generator reaching 10 MHz. Where you work, and on what, decides it.

Spec by spec

On paper the 1014D wins every scope figure, which is what you would expect when one product spends its whole case on an oscilloscope and the other splits a pocket-sized box three ways. The charts show how big each gap is. All figures are FNIRSI's, except the 1014D's weight, which comes from its Amazon listing.

Each scope pairs its converter sensibly with its front end. The 2C53T takes five samples per cycle of a 50 MHz sine and the 1014D ten per cycle of a 100 MHz sine, both above the Nyquist minimum of two. The trouble for the handheld is not its peak rate but how long it can keep that rate up, which the next section works through. If sample rate is new to you, read what GSa/s means first.

Handheld or benchtop: what each is for

Think about where the circuit lives. A bench scope is the right tool when the board comes to you: it sits on the desk beside the soldering iron, the probes stay clipped on, and a big screen lets you study a waveform for minutes. A handheld is right when you go to the circuit: under a dashboard, inside a washing machine, at a solar inverter on a wall, or at a control cabinet where there is no free socket for an adapter.

The 2C53T: a meter that can also draw waveforms

Most field fault-finding starts with a multimeter, and that is the 2C53T's strongest section. FNIRSI rates it at 19,999 counts with True RMS, voltage ranges to 1000 V DC and 750 V AC, current to 9.999 A, resistance to 19.999 MΩ and capacitance to 99.99 mF, plus temperature, diode and continuity. When a reading looks odd, you switch to scope mode on the same tool to see whether a rail is noisy, a sensor signal drops out or a PWM drive is present at all. The 167 × 89 × 35 mm body, 3,000 mAh cell and USB-C charging make it something you can leave in a tool bag. FNIRSI quotes up to 6 hours, which it labels a laboratory theoretical maximum.

The 1014D: a scope that can also feed the circuit

The 1014D gives up the meter and the battery for a better scope and a better signal source. On a desk, the 7-inch 800 × 480 screen shows two traces with room for measurements, and the DDS generator lets you inject a sine up to 10 MHz, or square and other shapes up to 2 MHz, at up to 2.5 Vpp. That is enough to sweep an audio or low-RF filter, stand in for a missing clock, or practice triggering on a signal whose frequency you already know. FNIRSI also lists 1 GB of internal storage for 1,000 screenshots and 1,000 waveforms, which helps when you are comparing a board before and after a repair.

The diagram is the practical difference in one view. Everything the 2C53T does comes out of a single sealed box, so there is no cable to trail across a workshop floor and nothing to plug in before a quick check. The 1014D stays tethered: the adapter has to reach a socket, and any USB lead you add for saving captures to a computer joins the same ground. That tether is harmless on a hobby bench and simply part of how a benchtop works, but it is why the Callout below matters more for the desk model.

Why 1 Kpts limits a handheld on slow timebases

A scope fills its record across the screen, so its real sample rate falls as you slow the timebase. If 1 Kpts spans a 10-division screen, then at 1 ms/div (10 ms across the screen) the 2C53T samples at about 100 kSa/s, not 250 MSa/s, which caps what it can resolve in that view at roughly 50 kHz. Zoom out to watch a slow event and fast detail disappears. A longer record keeps the rate up for longer; FNIRSI quotes the 1014D's only as "240Kbit", so its advantage here can't be put in points.

What each can and can't measure

Rated bandwidth is where a sine has already lost 3 dB, about 30% of its amplitude, so keep the signals you care about well below it. With the usual three-to-five-times margin, the 2C53T is comfortable up to roughly 10–17 MHz and the 1014D up to roughly 20–33 MHz. Both cover the everyday list: mains-frequency signals through a suitable probe, audio, PWM from a motor driver, UART, 100 kHz and 400 kHz I2C, and the switching node of a typical small buck converter. Our bandwidth explainer covers why the margin matters.

The differences show at the edges. Using the common 0.35 ÷ bandwidth estimate, the fastest rise time each front end can show is about 7 ns on the 2C53T and 3.5 ns on the 1014D (FNIRSI lists the 1014D's as under 3 ns). If you are judging overshoot on a fast logic edge or a gate drive, the benchtop gives the more honest picture. If you only need to know that a 10 kHz PWM signal exists and what its duty cycle is, either will do. On the generator side the 2C53T's 50 kHz ceiling is fine for audio tests and slow control signals but cannot stand in for a 1 MHz clock; the 1014D can.

Mains and high-voltage work

The 2C53T's meter reaches 750 V AC and its scope input is quoted at ±400 V, but FNIRSI's spec page gives no CAT safety rating, so treat mains measurements with the meter's rated leads and real caution. On either tool the probe ground clip is tied to the instrument's own ground, and on the 1014D that ground also connects to whatever its adapter and USB lead are plugged into. Never clip a scope ground lead onto a mains-referenced node such as an off-line supply's primary; use a differential probe.

Choose the FNIRSI 2C53T if…

  • ✓Your work moves around: a car, an appliance on the floor, a machine on site
  • ✓You want a True RMS multimeter and a scope in one tool rather than two
  • ✓Your signals are slow: sensor outputs, audio, low-frequency PWM, battery and supply behavior
  • ✓Battery power and USB-C charging matter more than screen size

Choose the FNIRSI 1014D if…

  • ✓You build and debug circuits at a desk and the scope can stay plugged in
  • ✓You need a test signal above 50 kHz, such as a 1 MHz sine for a filter or a clock stand-in
  • ✓Fast digital edges and megahertz signals are part of your projects
  • ✓You want a 7-inch screen and a kit with a 100X high-voltage probe included

Neither if…

You need a CAT-rated, isolated instrument for mains-referenced work: FNIRSI gives no CAT safety rating for either model, and on the 1014D the probe ground also ties to whatever its adapter and USB lead are plugged into.

The bottom line

Pick by where you work. The FNIRSI 2C53T is the better buy if you fault-find away from a bench and want a True RMS multimeter first, with a 50 MHz scope and a 50 kHz generator as backup, all on a battery. The FNIRSI 1014D is the better buy for desk-based electronics: twice the bandwidth, four times the sample rate, a 7-inch screen and a generator that reaches 10 MHz, at the cost of portability and a built-in meter.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FNIRSI 2C53T a replacement for a benchtop scope like the 1014D?+

Not for bench electronics. The 2C53T has half the bandwidth, a quarter of the sample rate, a 1 Kpts record and a 2.8-inch screen. It replaces a multimeter plus a basic scope for field work. If you build and debug circuits at a desk, the 1014D's 100 MHz, 7-inch screen and 10 MHz generator suit that work better.

Does the FNIRSI 1014D have a multimeter or a battery?+

No to both in FNIRSI's specifications. The 1014D is an oscilloscope with a built-in DDS signal generator and runs from a 5 V adapter; FNIRSI states no battery. The 2C53T is the one with a 19,999-count True RMS multimeter and a built-in 3,000 mAh battery charged over USB-C.

Which FNIRSI has the better signal generator?+

The 1014D. Its single DDS channel outputs sine up to 10 MHz and other waveforms up to 2 MHz at 2.5 Vpp. The 2C53T offers 13 waveforms with adjustable amplitude to 3 V and duty cycle, but only up to 50 kHz, which suits audio and slow control signals rather than clocks or RF filters.

How long does the FNIRSI 2C53T run on battery?+

FNIRSI quotes a standby time of up to 6 hours from the built-in 3,000 mAh lithium battery and labels that figure a laboratory theoretical maximum, so expect less in heavy use. It charges over USB-C at 5 V and 1 A, so a power bank can top it up in the field.

Can the FNIRSI 2C53T measure higher voltages than the 1014D?+

FNIRSI quotes ±400 V for the 2C53T's scope input and 40 V with the 1014D's 1X probe or 400 V with its 10X probe, and the 1014D kit adds a 100X probe. The 2C53T's meter reads up to 1000 V DC and 750 V AC. No CAT rating is given on either spec page, so take great care near mains.
