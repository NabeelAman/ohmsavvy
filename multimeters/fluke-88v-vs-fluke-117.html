Fluke 88V vs Fluke 117 for Automotive Diagnostics

For automotive diagnostics the Fluke 88V is the better meter, by a wide margin. It reads injector pulse width in milliseconds, duty cycle, RPM with an inductive pickup and temperature, holds 250 µs peaks during cranking, measures milliamps for parasitic drain, and is rated CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V for hybrid work. The Fluke 117 is an excellent electrician's meter, but on a car it lacks every one of those functions and stops at 600 V. Its strengths, VoltAlert and a LoZ input, are aimed at building wiring.

Catching the cranking dip

A no-crank or slow-crank complaint often turns on one number: how far battery voltage falls the instant the starter engages. That first dip lasts only milliseconds. Fluke's 88V manual gives its normal Min/Max a nominal response of about 100 ms on DC, which smooths a short dip into a higher number. Peak Min/Max responds in 250 µs on repetitive events (1 ms for a single event), so the true low point is what you see.

The 117 records minimum, maximum and average too, and its continuity mode flags opens as short as 500 µs, which helps on a wiggle test. What Fluke doesn't list for it is a peak-capture mode, so on a cranking test you're reading the plateau, not the dip. Our guides to a no-crank with a new battery and a starter that clicks but won't crank walk through what that low point tells you.

Injectors, PWM and RPM

Modern engines control almost everything with pulses: injectors, idle-air and EVAP solenoids, variable-cam actuators and electric cooling fans. Diagnosing them means reading how long the pulse lasts or what share of each cycle it's on. The 88V reads pulse width from 0.002 ms to 1999.9 ms with a stated accuracy of ±(0.002 ms + 3 counts), and duty cycle from 0.0% to 99.9%. Fluke lists neither for the 117, which reads frequency to 50 kHz and stops there.

RPM is read through Fluke's RPM80 inductive probe clipped around a plug lead or coil wire, in two settings: 30 to 9,000 RPM and 60 to 12,000 RPM, each ±2 RPM. One buying note: Fluke's 2006 manual describes the K-type thermocouple as included, while the current listing we track says Fluke treats the inductive pickup and temperature probe as optional for the bare 88V. Budget for them if the box doesn't have them. Step-by-step checks are in our guides to testing a fuel injector and testing a crankshaft position sensor.

Parasitic drain without guesswork

A battery that goes flat overnight usually means something is drawing current with the key off. Once modules fall asleep, many vehicles settle to a few tens of milliamps; the exact acceptable figure varies by maker and model, so check the service data. Resolving that needs a milliamp range. The 88V has 60.00 mA at 0.01 mA steps and 400.0 mA at 0.1 mA, with a published burden of 1.8 mV per mA. The 117's only current ranges are 6.000 A and 10.00 A, where 1 mA is the smallest step.

Worked example: a 35 mA draw on the 88V's 60 mA range reads to the hundredth of a milliamp and drops 35 × 1.8 = 63 mV across the meter, small next to a 12.6 V battery. On the 117 the same draw shows as roughly 0.035 A, close enough to spot a gross drain but too coarse to watch a module drop from 38 mA to 34 mA as it sleeps. A caution for both: a door opening or a module waking can draw amps, so start on the 10 A input and move down. Our guide to measuring parasitic battery drain covers the sequence.

LoZ is a shop-wiring tool, not a car tool

The 117's AutoVolt position drops the input to about 3 kΩ to drain ghost voltage on building wiring. On a car, that load can pull down a sensor reference or signal line and make a good circuit read bad. Use the 117's normal high-impedance V positions on vehicle electronics; the 88V's 10 MΩ input is what an ECU circuit expects.

Hybrids and EVs: why the rating matters

On a 12 V system the CAT rating is rarely the limiting factor. On a hybrid or EV, the traction battery, inverter and motor cables run at several hundred volts DC, and the figure varies by vehicle. The 117's highest range is 600.0 V and it's rated CAT III 600V. The 88V reads to 1000 V and is rated CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, the same protection as Fluke's 87V.

A higher rating doesn't make high-voltage work safe on its own; it means the meter won't be the weak link when the procedure is followed. That procedure is the manufacturer's, and it includes disabling the system and verifying zero energy with rated gloves and leads.

Safety: orange cables, fuses and the 10 A input

Hybrid and EV high-voltage systems can be lethal. Follow the maker's service-disconnect procedure, wear insulated gloves rated for the system, and use leads matching the meter's CAT rating; the 117 should not be used on systems above 600 V. On 12 V systems, never put either meter's A input across a battery: the A jack is a near short and will blow the meter's fuse or worse. Both allow 20 A for only 30 seconds, so measure starter current with a clamp, not in series.

The bottom line

For automotive diagnostics, buy the Fluke 88V. It has the functions car work runs on: injector pulse width, duty cycle, RPM, 250 µs peak capture, milliamp ranges for drain testing and a CAT IV 600V rating with a 1000 V range for hybrids. Keep the Fluke 117 for building wiring, where VoltAlert, LoZ and True RMS make it one of the best electrician's meters; on a car it is limited to volts, ohms and 10 A current.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 117 be used for automotive work?+

For basics, yes: battery voltage, fuse and continuity checks, resistance of coils and 10 A current. It can't read injector pulse width, duty cycle or RPM, has no peak capture and no milliamp range, so parasitic drain and most driveability work are beyond it. Use its high-impedance V positions on car electronics, not the 3 kΩ AutoVolt position.

Does the Fluke 88V come with the RPM pickup?+

Check the listing. The 88V reads RPM through Fluke's RPM80 inductive probe, and the current listing we track says Fluke treats the inductive pickup and temperature probe as optional for the bare 88V. Fluke's older 2006 manual describes the thermocouple as included. Kits vary, so confirm the box contents before relying on RPM or temperature.

Is the Fluke 88V good for parasitic drain testing?+

Yes. Its 60.00 mA range resolves 0.01 mA with a burden of 1.8 mV per mA, so a typical sleeping-vehicle draw reads cleanly without disturbing the circuit much. Start on the 10 A input in case something wakes and draws amps, then move to the mA jack once the reading settles below the range limit.

Is the Fluke 117 safe on a hybrid vehicle?+

It's rated CAT III 600V and its highest voltage range is 600.0 V, so it should not be used on any system that can exceed 600 V. Many hybrid and EV traction systems run at several hundred volts DC and some go higher. The 88V reads to 1000 V and carries CAT IV 600V, which is the better match.

Why is the Fluke 88V not True RMS?+

Fluke designed it around DC and pulse measurements, where True RMS adds nothing; its manual states the AC conversion is average responding, rms indicating. On cars that rarely matters. It does matter on non-sine AC such as alternator ripple from a failed diode or inverter outputs, where an average-responding reading can be off by several percent.

Which Fluke is better for a mobile technician who also does electrical work?+

If vehicles are the main income, the 88V, paired with a separate non-contact voltage pen for shop wiring. If building wiring dominates and cars are occasional, the 117. The 88V's CAT IV 600V rating also covers service-entrance work, but it lacks the 117's LoZ ghost-voltage check and True RMS for distorted building loads.
