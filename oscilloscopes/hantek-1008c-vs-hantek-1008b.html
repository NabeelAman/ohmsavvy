Hantek 1008C vs 1008B

Hantek 1008C vs 1008B is a question with a short answer: on Hantek's own product pages, the two publish the same spec table, line for line. Both are eight-channel, 12-bit USB scopes at 2.4 MSa/s with 4K points, 400 V inputs, an eight-channel 250 kHz signal simulator and the same "over 80" automotive tests. What differs is the Amazon listing: the 1008C itemizes leads and an auto probe, the 1008B lists only the unit. Buy whichever is cheaper on the day once you know what is in the box.

Hantek 1008C vs 1008B: what the spec sheets say

We put Hantek's two product pages side by side on November 8, 2026. Every row matched: eight channels, 1 MΩ DC-coupled inputs, 12-bit, 4K memory, 2.4 MSa/s, a 400 V (DC + AC peak) limit, 1 ns/div to 20000 s/div, edge triggering on any channel, the same voltage and time measurements, the same current-clamp ranges, 190 × 167 × 35 mm and 0.63 kg. Both even repeat the same internal inconsistency, "over 80" tests in the overview and "78 types" in the function list.

If you have read that the C is the higher tier with more software, Hantek's current pages and both Amazon listings don't support it: they describe the same function set in the same words. If a seller claims extra functions on the 1008C, ask them to name the tests the 1008B lacks.

Spec by spec: the numbers both share

Since the hardware matches, the useful charts are about what that shared hardware can do on a car. The key limit is 4K points. To fit one full engine revolution on screen, the scope must slow its sample rate to 4,000 divided by the revolution time.

In practice that is fine for crank and cam sensors, injector pulses, alternator ripple and starter or charging current through a clamp, which are slow next to 100 kHz. It is short for a CAN bus at 500 kbit/s, where each bit lasts 2 µs and 2.4 MSa/s gives under five samples per bit. Hantek doesn't say how 2.4 MSa/s divides with all eight channels on, so assume less per channel when you use them all.

12 bits matters more than speed here

The 1008's 12-bit converter splits each range into 4,096 levels against 256 for an 8-bit scope. On a 5 V sensor signal that is the difference between seeing a 20 mV glitch and losing it in the quantization steps. It is the main reason to pick this platform over a faster general-purpose USB scope for sensor work.

What both can and can't measure

Eight channels let you watch the crank sensor, both cam sensors and several injectors at once, which a two- or four-channel scope can't. The 250 kHz LVTTL generator can simulate crank and cam signals to test an ECU input on the bench. The software adds guided tests with reference waveforms and video help, and a one-key report. Neither has a screen; both need a Windows laptop over USB. Neither reaches past the 100 kHz bandwidth in Hantek's manual, so electronics work beyond audio is out of range. For a general-purpose USB scope, see PicoScope 2204A vs Hantek 1008C.

Running engines and ignition voltage

Hantek rates the inputs at 400 V (DC + AC peak). Ignition secondary voltage is many times that, so it must only be read through a capacitive ignition pickup, never by connecting a lead directly. Injector and coil primary circuits can spike to hundreds of volts when they switch off; use an attenuator (Hantek's software supports 20:1, x10 and higher probe scales). Keep leads and the laptop clear of belts and fans, and never work on a running engine in a closed garage.

The trust question: kit contents

The only real risk in this choice is the box. Hantek's pages list a high-pressure ignition probe for both models, but the 1008C box list on Amazon doesn't name one, and the 1008B kit photo doesn't show one. The 1008B listing names no leads at all. Before ordering, message the seller and ask for the packing list in writing. That costs nothing and settles the one difference these two actually have.

The bottom line

Hantek publishes identical specs for the 1008C and 1008B: eight 12-bit channels, 2.4 MSa/s, 4K points, 400 V inputs, a 250 kHz eight-channel simulator and the same automotive test library. Buy the 1008C if it costs the same or less, since its listing itemizes the leads. Buy the 1008B if it is cheaper and the seller confirms what ships.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Hantek 1008B and 1008C?+

On Hantek's product pages, none in hardware: the spec tables match line for line, and both claim the same automotive function count. The difference we found is the Amazon listing. The 1008C's box list names an auto probe, four alligator-to-coax leads and four coax connectors; the 1008B's names only the unit.

Does the Hantek 1008C have more automotive tests than the 1008B?+

Not according to Hantek's current pages or the two Amazon listings. All four describe "over 80" automotive functions, and both Hantek pages say "78 types" in their function lists. If a seller says the 1008C has extra tests, ask which ones; we couldn't find a published list that differs.

Do the 1008B and 1008C come with an ignition probe?+

Hantek's pages list one high-pressure ignition probe for each. The 1008C Amazon box list doesn't name one, and the 1008B listing's kit photo doesn't show one. Ask the seller before buying if you need it for secondary ignition, because direct connection to ignition secondary would far exceed the 400 V input rating.

Can the Hantek 1008B or 1008C run on a Mac?+

Hantek lists only Windows: XP, 7, 8 and 10 on the 1008C page, and 7, 8 and 10 on the 1008B Amazon listing. Neither source mentions macOS or Linux. On a Mac you would need a Windows installation, and USB driver support in that setup isn't something Hantek documents.

Is the Hantek 1008 fast enough for CAN bus?+

Not for decoding. High-speed CAN at 500 kbit/s has 2 µs bits, and 2.4 MSa/s gives under five samples per bit, with a 100 kHz bandwidth in Hantek's manual that rounds the edges. You can see bus activity, but for CAN shape and decoding a faster scope such as the Hantek DSO2C10 fits better.
