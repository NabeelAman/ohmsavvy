Why Is My Laptop Charger Not Charging the Battery? How to Test the Adapter

For a traditional barrel-connector charger, measuring open-circuit DC voltage right at the connector tip is a safe, direct way to confirm the adapter itself is producing its rated output. For USB-C chargers, voltage alone is less conclusive since USB Power Delivery negotiates voltage digitally — if your charger is specifically USB-C, see USB-C charger not delivering full power for that case instead.

Only measure open-circuit voltage; don't probe inside the laptop

  • Measure the adapter's output with nothing plugged into the laptop — an open-circuit voltage reading at the connector tip.
  • Don't open the laptop or probe its internal charging circuitry unless you're specifically qualified to do so.
  • Check the adapter's polarity symbol before probing, so you know which part of the connector is positive.

1Check the rating label

Read the output voltage and current printed on the adapter itself (often something like "Output: 19V, 3.42A"). This is the number you're testing against — there isn't one universal voltage across all laptop chargers.

2Measure the adapter's output voltage

With the adapter plugged into power but not connected to the laptop, set the meter to DC volts and touch the probes to the connector tip and outer sleeve (check the polarity symbol on the label first).

ReadingLikely meaning
Close to the rated voltageAdapter output is healthy
Noticeably low or unstableFailing adapter — internal component degradation
0 VDead adapter, or a break in the cable before the connector

The polarity symbol on the label (a dot inside a C shape, with + and − at the ends) tells you which contact is positive. Most laptop bricks are center-positive, so a correctly connected meter shows a positive number. If the display shows a minus sign in front of the rated voltage, the probes are simply reversed; the adapter is still producing its output, and the reading is just as valid.

3Check the cable and connector

If voltage reads 0 V or unstable, gently flex the cable near both ends while watching the meter — a reading that comes and goes with flexing points to a broken conductor inside the cable, most often right where it exits the adapter brick or the connector itself, since those are the points under the most repeated stress.

Try a known-good cable or adapter if you have one

Swapping in a compatible cable or adapter you know works is often the fastest way to confirm whether the fault is in the adapter, the cable, or the laptop itself.

What voltage alone can't tell you

A correct open-circuit voltage confirms the adapter can produce its rated voltage with no load, but doesn't fully confirm it can supply its rated current once the laptop actually draws power. An adapter that reads correct voltage but still can't charge the laptop, especially under heavier use, may still be failing under real load — a symptom voltage alone won't always catch.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to probe a laptop charger's barrel connector?

Yes, for a simple open-circuit voltage reading — touch the meter's probes to the connector's tip (usually positive) and outer sleeve (usually negative; check your adapter's polarity symbol) with nothing else connected. This is a low-risk, standard DC voltage measurement.

What should the adapter's output voltage read?

It should be close to the voltage printed on the adapter's label — for example, an adapter rated 19V should read close to 19V DC with nothing connected. A reading significantly off that number, or 0 V, means the adapter itself has failed.

The adapter reads the correct voltage but the laptop still won't charge — what next?

Check the cable for damage (especially near the connector ends, where cables flex most and fail most often), then try a different outlet, in case the issue is upstream. If those check out, the fault is likely inside the laptop's charging circuit or battery itself rather than the adapter.

Can a laptop battery fail in a way that looks like a charger problem?

Yes — many laptops will show a 'not charging' or 'plugged in, not charging' status specifically when the battery itself has failed or is severely degraded, even with a perfectly good adapter. If the adapter tests correctly and a different known-good adapter shows the same symptom, suspect the battery.

Why does my adapter read the correct voltage but feel warm or buzz?

Mild warmth under load is normal for many adapters, but noticeable heat, a burning smell, or an audible buzz/hum suggests internal component stress or failure that voltage alone won't reveal, even if the output currently measures correctly. Stop using an adapter showing these signs regardless of what it measures.
