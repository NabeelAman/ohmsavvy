What solar work demands from a meter

A photovoltaic array is a DC source that stays live whenever light falls on it. Testing one means reading open-circuit voltage (Voc) that can reach hundreds of volts on a single string, comparing short-circuit or operating current between strings, and confirming polarity before anything is connected to an inverter. Those jobs reward a high DC voltage rating with a matching CAT rating, a DC-capable clamp and a meter that stays readable on a hot, bright roof. A sign error on the display is worth understanding first: see why a multimeter shows negative voltage, and for bypass diodes in a junction box, our guide to testing a diode.

We ranked these ten on the DC voltage and CAT ratings first, then on whether they can read DC current through a clamp, then on logging, accuracy and toughness. Off-grid owners will also find our guide to checking a 12V leisure battery useful, and for clamp basics read the clamp meter buying guide. If two Flukes on this list are your shortlist, see Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179 and our Fluke 87V review.

PV arrays can't be switched off in daylight

Opening a DC isolator or inverter breaker stops current flowing, but every module in the string is still generating voltage as long as light reaches it, and a string's Voc can be several hundred volts. DC arcs do not self-extinguish the way AC arcs do, so never unplug a connector under load, never break a short-circuit test while current flows, and only work on strings if you are trained for it. Use a meter and leads whose CAT rating covers the full DC voltage, and wear insulated gloves and eye protection.

What to look for in a solar meter

DC voltage rating

The meter's maximum must exceed the string's cold-weather Voc, not just its nameplate figure.

CAT rating on DC

CAT III 1000V is the floor for most strings; 1500 V systems need a CAT III 1500V meter and leads.

DC clamp

A Hall-effect jaw reads string current without opening a connector under load.

Polarity warning

Reversed strings at a combiner are a costly mistake; an audible polarity alarm catches them.

Logging

Recording voltage across a day separates shading and soiling problems from a failed module.

Field toughness

Rooftops are hot, bright and dusty: look for IP ratings, readable displays and drop ratings.

Why the meter's limit must beat cold-weather Voc

Crystalline silicon modules gain voltage as they cool, typically a few tenths of a percent per degree Celsius below the 25 °C test condition. A string labeled at 900 V Voc can therefore read above 1000 V on a freezing morning, beyond every 1000 V meter here. Check the module's temperature coefficient and the lowest site temperature before you choose a meter, and see unstable multimeter readings if a Voc reading wanders as clouds pass.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Size the voltage rating for cold mornings

A module's Voc rises as its temperature falls, so a string that reads comfortably below a meter's limit in summer can exceed it on a frosty morning. Work out the string's cold-weather Voc from the module datasheet and pick a meter whose DC rating and CAT voltage sit above it. For 1500 V commercial arrays that narrows the field to the Fluke 393 FC.

Buy a DC clamp if you commission or service strings

Comparing string currents at a combiner is the quickest way to spot a damaged module or a loose connector, and it needs a DC clamp. Only two meters here clamp DC: the Fluke 393 FC and the KAIWEETS HT208D. HVAC and electrician clamps such as the Fieldpiece SC480 read AC current only, which suits the inverter output but not the array.

Use leads and probes rated for the system

A meter's CAT rating only protects you if the leads match it. For 1000 V strings use CAT III 1000V leads with shrouded tips; for 1500 V systems Fluke rates the 393 FC's leads at CAT III 1500 V DC. Replace leads with cracked insulation, and keep a set of MC4-compatible test adapters so you never have to strip a cable on site.

Add logging and temperature for diagnosis, not just commissioning

A single Voc reading tells you a string is alive. Logging voltage through a day, with module temperature alongside, shows whether a low reading tracks shading, heat or a fault. The Fluke 289 and UNI-T UT181A log on the meter, and the UT181A's twin thermocouple inputs can read module and ambient temperatures together.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What multimeter do I need to test solar panels?+

For a single panel or a small off-grid array, any meter with a 1000 V DC range and a CAT III 1000V rating will read open-circuit voltage safely. For grid-tied strings, add a DC clamp so you can read operating current without breaking a connector. Commercial 1500 V systems need a meter rated for 1500 V DC, such as the Fluke 393 FC.

How do I measure the open-circuit voltage (Voc) of a solar panel?+

Disconnect the panel or string from the charge controller or inverter with its isolator, set the meter to DC volts, and probe positive to positive and negative to negative at the connectors. Compare the reading with the label's Voc, allowing for temperature: cold panels read higher and hot panels lower. Our negative voltage guide explains what a minus sign means.

Can I measure solar panel short-circuit current with a multimeter?+

On one small panel, yes, if its label Isc is well below the meter's current rating: you connect the meter's amps input directly across the panel. Don't do it on strings, and never disconnect the leads while current flows, because the DC arc won't self-extinguish. A DC clamp across a short-circuit link is the safer professional method.

Is a 600V multimeter enough for solar panels?+

Only for small systems. Residential strings in the US are commonly designed up to 600 V, and Voc rises in cold weather, so a 600 V meter leaves no margin. Commercial strings run to 1000 V and utility arrays to 1500 V. Every meter on this list reads at least 1000 V DC, and only the Fluke 393 FC is rated for 1500 V.

Why do I need a DC clamp meter for solar work?+

Most clamp meters, including many HVAC and electrician models, only read AC current through the jaw. PV strings produce DC, so you need a Hall-effect DC clamp such as the Fluke 393 FC or KAIWEETS HT208D to compare string currents at a combiner box. Zero the clamp before each DC reading to remove offset.

How do I find a bad solar panel with a multimeter?+

Compare like with like. Measure each string's Voc at the combiner under the same sunlight; a string that reads one module's worth low points to a failed module or a shorted bypass diode. Then compare string currents with a DC clamp. Logging voltage across a day, as the Fluke 289 or UNI-T UT181A can, helps separate shading from failure.
