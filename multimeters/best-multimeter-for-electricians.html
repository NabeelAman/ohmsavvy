The short answer for working electricians

The best multimeter for most electricians is the Fluke 117, because it combines LoZ ghost-voltage rejection, automatic AC/DC selection, built-in non-contact voltage and True RMS in a CAT III 600V meter we have tested ourselves. If you work at the service entrance, the Klein Tools MM720 adds CAT IV 600V and 1000V ranges while keeping LoZ, and the Fluke 87V is the step up for industrial panels and drives. The rest of the list fills the gaps a single meter leaves: a combo kit, two clamps, an open-jaw tester and a waterproof voltage checker.

Electrical work asks different questions from bench electronics. Resolution in microvolts matters little; what matters is whether the meter survives a transient on a 480V bus, whether it can tell a dead conductor from one carrying induced voltage, and how quickly you can read load current without opening a circuit. We weighted the ranking that way, so a few meters that top general roundups sit lower here, and tools that are not full multimeters earn a place because they do one electrician's job well. Our Fluke 117 review and Fluke 87V review go deeper on the two tested meters, and Fluke 117 vs 87V explains when the bigger meter pays off. If budget is the constraint, see our electrician's meters on a budget.

Panel work is not a place to learn

Opening a panel exposes you to arc-flash and shock hazards that a meter rating alone does not remove. Wear the PPE your work requires, use leads and probe covers rated to the same category as the meter, and follow your lockout/tagout procedure. Test before touch every time, proving the meter on a known live source before and after. If you are not qualified for the equipment in front of you, stop and call a licensed electrician or the utility; in an emergency involving fire or injury, call 911.

What separates an electrician's meter from a hobby meter

CAT rating matched to the job

CAT III covers distribution panels and branch circuits; CAT IV covers the service drop, meter base and outdoor feeders. Buy for the highest point you will probe.

LoZ or AutoV mode

A low-impedance input loads down induced voltage on dead conductors, so a ghost reading collapses while a real supply stays put.

True RMS

Drives, LED drivers and switch-mode supplies distort current and voltage. An average-responding meter can read those waveforms wrong.

A way to read current safely

Opening a 20 A circuit to put a meter in series is slow and risky. A clamp jaw or an open-fork tester reads load current around the conductor.

Built for the van and the ladder

Drop ratings, IP ratings, a readable display in a dark closet and leads that store in the case all matter more on site than extra functions.

NCV as a first warning

A built-in non-contact detector speeds up the first sweep of a box. It never replaces a contact test-before-touch with the leads.

A ghost reading is a high-impedance meter telling the truth

A 10 MΩ input barely loads the circuit, so it faithfully reports voltage coupled onto an open conductor from its neighbors. That is correct physics and a wrong conclusion. LoZ changes the question from "is there voltage?" to "can this voltage push current?" Our explainer on voltage on a disconnected circuit shows the effect, and measuring AC current with a clamp meter covers the clamp technique. Before you buy

Buying advice

Rate the meter for the worst place you will put it

A meter that spends most days on receptacles still has to survive the one day you check a meter base. Transients near the service entrance carry far more energy than those at a branch outlet, which is why CAT IV exists. If your work ever reaches the service side, the MM720, 87V, 179 or 16030A belongs in the bag; if it never does, a CAT III 600V meter like the 117 is enough.

Pair a meter with a clamp instead of buying one tool for everything

Multimeters with 10 A inputs are poor tools for load current on live circuits: you have to open the circuit, the fuse limits you and a mistake with the leads in the amps jack can arc. A clamp like the Fluke 323 or Milwaukee 2235-20, or the open fork on the T5-600, reads current around the conductor instead. Most working electricians end up carrying one of each.

Use LoZ to settle ghost voltage arguments

When a circuit you have switched off still shows 30 or 40 volts on a high-impedance meter, LoZ tells you whether that voltage can drive a load. If the reading collapses under the low-impedance input, it is coupling from a neighboring conductor; if it holds, something is still connected. That saves call-backs and stops you treating a live circuit as dead.

Check the whole measuring system, not just the meter

The CAT rating printed on the meter only holds if the leads, probe tips and fuses match it. Fit probe-tip covers where the code calls for them, replace fuses only with the exact rating listed in the manual, and prove the meter on a known live source before and after every dead test. Those habits matter more than any single feature on this list.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Why do electricians need a LoZ setting on a multimeter?+

Long runs of cable next to energized conductors pick up capacitively coupled voltage, and a high-impedance digital meter can show tens of volts on a wire that is actually dead. A low-impedance (LoZ) input puts a small load across the leads, so that induced voltage collapses toward zero while a genuine supply holds. The Fluke 117, Klein MM720 and Brymen BM235 in this list have it.

Is CAT IV necessary for residential electrical work?+

Inside a house, most work at receptacles, fixtures and the load side of the breaker panel sits in CAT III territory. CAT IV applies at the origin of the installation: the service drop, the meter socket and outdoor feeders before the main disconnect. If you replace meter bases or trace service-entrance faults, choose a CAT IV 600V meter such as the MM720, 87V, 179 or Southwire 16030A.

Should an electrician buy a clamp meter or a multimeter first?+

Most electricians start with a multimeter, because voltage, continuity and resistance cover the majority of service calls, and then add a clamp for load current. If you mostly balance panels or chase overloaded circuits, the clamp may earn its keep sooner. The Fluke 117/323 Kit sidesteps the question by putting both in one case, and the T5-600 combines basic volts with open-jaw amps.

Can a non-contact voltage detector replace testing with leads?+

No. A non-contact detector senses the electric field around an insulated conductor and can miss voltage behind shielding, in metal conduit or on a neutral that is carrying current. Use it to speed up the first sweep, then confirm with the test leads on a meter you have just proved on a known live source, and prove it again afterwards. That live-dead-live routine is what keeps you safe.

Does a multimeter's CAT rating cover the test leads too?+

The system is only as safe as its lowest-rated part. A CAT IV meter used with CAT II leads is a CAT II setup. Check the rating printed on the leads, the probe tips and any clip-on accessories. Klein notes that the CAT IV figure on some MM450 listings applies to the leads alone, while the meter itself is CAT III 600V, which shows why both markings matter.

How often should an electrician replace multimeter test leads?+

There is no fixed interval. Replace them as soon as the insulation is cracked, nicked or melted, the probe shrouds are missing, or the conductor shows through near the plug. A quick check is to measure resistance with the probes shorted; a reading that jumps or climbs well above a fraction of an ohm points to a broken strand. Our lead-testing guide walks through it.
