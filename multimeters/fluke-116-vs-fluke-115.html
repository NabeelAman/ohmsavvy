Fluke 116 vs Fluke 115 for HVAC Troubleshooting

For HVAC troubleshooting the Fluke 116 is the better meter: it reads flame-rod current on a 600.0 µA range with 0.1 µA resolution, takes Type K temperature with the included 80BK probe, and has a LoZ input for ghost voltage on 24 V control wiring. The Fluke 115 has none of those three. What the 115 keeps is 6 A and 10 A current ranges. On capacitors, line voltage and resistance, the two read identically.

Fluke's 115 page lists temperature; its datasheet doesn't

The comparison table on Fluke's 115 product page shows a temperature range for the 115. The 110 Series datasheet, the users manual and the 115's own accuracy table list temperature for the 116 only, and the 115 has no 80BK probe in its box. We follow the datasheet and manual: don't buy the 115 to read temperature.

Flame-rod microamps: the 116's reason to exist

A gas furnace proves flame by flame rectification. The control board puts AC on the flame rod; the flame conducts better in one direction than the other, so a small DC current flows from rod to the grounded burner. If that current is too weak, the board shuts the gas valve a few seconds after ignition. The classic symptom is a burner that lights, runs briefly and drops out, then retries until lockout. The only way to measure the signal is a meter on a microamp range in series with the rod wire.

Flame signals are commonly a few microamps; the minimum each control accepts is set by its maker and is usually printed in the furnace manual. Work the arithmetic on an illustrative 3.0 µA reading. The 116's DC µA line allows 1.0% of 3.0 µA (0.03 µA) plus 2 counts of 0.1 µA, so about ±0.23 µA: easily good enough to tell a healthy signal from a marginal one. The 115 on its 6.000 A range shows 0.000 A for the same current, because 3 µA is a third of a percent of its smallest step.

A low reading usually points to an oxidized rod, a poor burner ground or a cracked insulator rather than the board. Our guide to measuring microamps with a multimeter covers the series hookup in detail.

Temperature and thermocouples

Temperature comes up on almost every HVAC call: supply and return air for a temperature split, a line temperature for superheat or subcooling, a heat-exchanger outlet. The 116 does it with the bundled 80BK probe. Fluke's accuracy is ±(1% + 10) on the 0.1 °C scale, so 10 counts add 1.0 °C; at 13 °C supply air that is about ±1.1 °C, before probe error, which Fluke excludes from the figure. In Fahrenheit the line is ±(1% + 18) at 0.2 °F steps, about ±4.2 °F at 55 °F.

That is fine for a temperature-split check. A rule of thumb many technicians use for cooling is a split around 15 to 22 °F, but the equipment maker's chart wins. Taking both readings with the same probe and meter helps, because a fixed offset largely cancels when you subtract one reading from the other.

The pilot thermocouple on a standing-pilot appliance is a different measurement, and here the two meters tie. It is a millivolt reading, not a temperature reading, and both meters have the same 600.0 mV DC range at ±(0.5% + 2). Open-circuit values in the 20s of millivolts are commonly quoted for a healthy pilot thermocouple; check the valve maker's figure. On an illustrative 25.0 mV reading, either meter is good to about ±0.3 mV. Our guide to checking a thermocouple with a multimeter walks through it.

Capacitors and LoZ on 24 V controls

Run capacitors are the most common failed part on a condenser, and on this job the meters are equal: both read 1 nF to 9,999 µF at ±(1.9% + 2). On an illustrative 45 µF compressor section, read on the 100.0 µF range, the allowed error is 0.86 µF plus 0.2 µF, about ±1.1 µF. On a 5 µF fan section, read on the 10.00 µF range, it is about ±0.12 µF. Either is tight enough to compare against the tolerance printed on the can. See how to test a fan motor capacitor for the procedure.

Control wiring is where LoZ pays off. Thermostat cable often has spare conductors, and long runs sit next to powered ones, so an unused or open wire can pick up a coupled voltage that a high-impedance input displays as a believable number. On the 115, every volts position stays above 5 MΩ, so you must load-test a suspicious reading yourself. The 116's AutoVolt position drops the input to about 3 kΩ, which drains coupling and leaves a real 24 V supply standing.

Where the 115 is the better HVAC meter

The 115's amp ranges are not useless on HVAC work. Setting the heat anticipator on an older mechanical thermostat calls for the current in the heat circuit, which is a fraction of an amp; the 115 reads it in series on its 6.000 A range, and the 116's 600 µA ceiling can't. Small inducer or damper motors and 24 V control loads can also be read in series. Blower, compressor and electric-heat amps are a different story: they are big, often above 10 A, and sit inside running equipment, so a clamp meter is the right tool with either Fluke.

A technician who works mainly on heat pumps, mini-splits and electric heat, with no gas appliances, never needs a flame-sensor range. If that technician already owns an HVAC clamp with a temperature input, the 115 covers the remaining multimeter jobs and adds amps. Our Fluke 115 review is based on the owner's own unit; the 116 assessment here is desk research against Fluke's published specifications.

HVAC specialist or general-purpose meter?

Fluke built the 116 as a specialist and the 115 as a field-service all-rounder, and the split shows the moment the meter leaves the mechanical room. Off an HVAC call, nobody needs flame-rod microamps, but plenty of jobs need amps: a parked car's battery drain, a bench appliance, a small motor. The 116 has no input for any of them, because its only current range tops out at 600.0 µA. Everything voltage-, resistance- and capacitance-based is identical, so the general-purpose gap is current and nothing else.

Safety on furnace and condenser work

Shut off power at the disconnect before you open a series path, and reconnect the meter before restoring power for a flame-rod reading; keep your hands clear of the burner and line terminals while the unit runs. Discharge run and start capacitors with a suitable resistor before you test them, because they can hold a dangerous charge after the power is off. Both meters are rated CAT III 600V with a 600 V maximum between any terminal and earth; neither belongs on the utility side of the service. If you smell gas, leave the building and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

The bottom line

For HVAC troubleshooting, buy the Fluke 116: flame-rod microamps, Type K temperature with a probe in the box and a LoZ input for control wiring are the readings that fill service calls. Pair it with a clamp meter for amps. The Fluke 115 suits technicians with no gas equipment who already own an HVAC clamp, or who want series current for anticipators and small loads.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 115 test a flame sensor?+

Not in practice. Its finest current step is 1 mA on the 6.000 A range, and flame signals are typically a few microamps, so the display stays at zero. The Fluke 116 has a 600.0 µA range with 0.1 µA resolution designed for this check, and several HVAC clamp meters also include a µA range for flame rods.

Does the Fluke 115 measure temperature?+

Fluke's sources disagree. The comparison table on the 115 product page shows a temperature range, but the 110 Series datasheet, the users manual and the 115's accuracy table list temperature only for the 116, and no probe ships with the 115. We follow the datasheet and manual, so treat the 115 as having no temperature function.

Is the Fluke 116 good for HVAC?+

Yes, it is Fluke's dedicated HVAC model. It reads flame-sensor current to 0.1 µA, Type K temperature from -40 to 400 °C with the included 80BK probe, capacitance to 9,999 µF, and has a LoZ input against ghost voltage. Its gap is amps: there is no amp range, so carry a clamp meter for motors and compressors.

Which Fluke is best for testing HVAC capacitors?+

Either. The 116 and 115 share the same capacitance function, 1 nF to 9,999 µF with ±(1.9% + 2) accuracy, which covers run and start capacitors comfortably. A 45 µF reading carries about ±1.1 µF of allowed error. Always discharge the capacitor safely and isolate it from the circuit before you measure it.

Why does my meter show voltage on a thermostat wire that should be off?+

Often it is ghost voltage: an idle conductor beside powered ones in the same cable picks up a coupled voltage that a high-impedance meter displays. A low-impedance input such as the 116's AutoVolt position loads the wire enough to collapse it. The 115 has no LoZ mode, so add a load across the leads to check.

Is the Fluke 116 a good general-purpose multimeter?+

Only if you carry a clamp meter. Its volts, resistance, continuity and capacitance match the Fluke 115, and it adds LoZ and temperature, but its single current range stops at 600.0 µA. That rules out battery-drain, appliance and small-motor current through the leads. For all-round home, car and shop use, the 115 or 117 is the better fit.

Can the Fluke 116 measure amps on a blower motor?+

No. The 116's only current range is 600.0 µA, so it cannot read any motor current. Use a clamp meter around one conductor instead. The 115 can read up to 10 A in series, but breaking into a running blower circuit is awkward and many motors exceed that, so a clamp is the better tool there too.
