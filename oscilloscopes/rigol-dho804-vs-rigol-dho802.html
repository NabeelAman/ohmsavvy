Rigol DHO804 vs DHO802: Four Channels or Two?

Rigol DHO804 vs DHO802 is a channel-count decision and almost nothing else. Rigol's DHO800 data sheet gives both the same 70 MHz bandwidth, 12-bit resolution, 7-inch 1024 × 600 touchscreen, 1.78 kg body and USB-C power. The DHO804 has four analog inputs. The DHO802 has two plus an external trigger input, and with two channels in use both sample at 625 MSa/s into 10 Mpts.

Spec by spec

The charts show the one place the two models differ in performance: what happens to sample rate and memory as you switch channels on. Up to two channels they are the same instrument.

The last chart is the quiet surprise. Halving both sample rate and memory leaves the record length unchanged, so the DHO804 with four channels on still holds 16 ms at full rate, the same window the DHO802 holds on two. What you lose is time resolution: 3.2 ns between samples instead of 1.6 ns. At 312.5 MSa/s that is still about 4.5 samples per cycle at the full 70 MHz bandwidth, which is thin for a sine wave and fine for the 5 to 20 MHz signals most hobby benches see. What each one can and can't measure

Rigol DHO804: four views of one circuit

Four channels change how you debug. On a switching regulator you can hold the PWM input, the gate, the switch node and the output on one screen and see the order of events directly. On an SPI bus the chip-select line frames each transfer, so having it on screen tells you which bytes belong to which transaction. Rigol's standard decoders (parallel, UART, I2C, SPI and CAN) work from analog channels, so four inputs means four lines available to them. The cost is that with three or four channels on, each one samples at 312.5 MSa/s and records 5 Mpts.

Rigol DHO802: two channels and a trigger input

Most measurement is a comparison of two points: what goes in and what comes out. The DHO802 does that at its best sample rate, 625 MSa/s per channel, because it never has to split four ways. Its EXT input is the part buyers overlook. Rigol lists it as "2 + EXT": a third connector that can start a capture from a reference such as a sync pulse or a microcontroller's debug pin, without spending one of the two displayed channels. It can't show that signal, decode it or measure it.

Count your signals before you count your money

List the last five things you probed. If any of them needed three signals in view at once, such as a gate, a switch node and an output, buy the DHO804. If every one was a pair, the DHO802 gives you the same 12-bit screen and the same per-channel numbers for less.

Mains and ground clips

Rigol rates both inputs CAT I, 300 Vrms and 400 Vpk. Neither is meant for direct mains measurements, and every probe ground clip on a bench scope is tied to the same ground. Use a differential probe for anything mains referenced, and don't clip a ground lead to a point that isn't ground.

The question with the DHO802: will two be enough next year?

The DHO802 is not the weaker scope per channel; on two channels Rigol gives it the same figures as the DHO804. Its risk is that you can't add channels later. Hobby work tends to grow toward motor drives, power supplies and microcontroller buses, and those are the jobs where a third trace stops being a luxury. The DHO804 is the model we have hands-on experience with, which is why it carries our Tested badge; nothing on this page relies on bench measurements, and every number comes from Rigol's data sheet.

Choose the Rigol DHO804 if…

  • ✓You debug SPI, where clock, MOSI, MISO and chip select want four traces
  • ✓You compare a gate drive, a switch node and a current probe at the same time
  • ✓You want four probes in the box rather than buying two more later
  • ✓You can live with 312.5 MSa/s per channel when three or four are on

Choose the Rigol DHO802 if…

  • ✓Your work is input against output: amplifiers, filters, regulators
  • ✓You want an external trigger input to sync on a reference signal without spending a channel
  • ✓You never expected to look at more than two signals at once
  • ✓You want the 12-bit DHO800 platform at the lowest entry point

Neither if…

Your signals have edges faster than about 5 ns or clocks above roughly 20 MHz. Both are 70 MHz scopes with a 5 ns typical rise time; the DHO814 or DHO914 adds bandwidth on the same platform.

The bottom line

Buy the Rigol DHO804 if any job you do needs three or four signals in view, such as SPI with chip select or a switching regulator's gate, switch node and output. Buy the Rigol DHO802 if your work is two-point comparisons: it has the same 70 MHz, 12-bit body and, per Rigol, the same 625 MSa/s and 10 Mpts per channel with two channels on, plus an external trigger input.

Frequently asked questions

Is the DHO802 just a DHO804 with two channels disabled?+

On paper the two share bandwidth, resolution, display, size and power, and Rigol lists identical sample rate and memory with two channels on. The differences Rigol publishes are the channel count, the DHO802's external trigger input and the number of probes in the box. Rigol's data sheet lists no option that adds channels, so buy for the channel count you expect to need.

Does the DHO804 have an external trigger input?+

Rigol's data sheet lists the DHO804 as four channels and the DHO802 as "2 + EXT", and the external-trigger specs are footnoted to the two-channel models. On the DHO804 you would trigger from one of the four analog channels instead, which is usually fine because you have more of them.

Is 312.5 MSa/s enough on the DHO804 with four channels on?+

For most hobby signals, yes. It gives about 31 samples per cycle at 10 MHz and 4.5 at the full 70 MHz bandwidth. Record length doesn't suffer, because memory halves with the sample rate: 16 ms at full rate either way. Switch off channels you don't need when you want 625 MSa/s or 1.25 GSa/s back.

Can both scopes decode CAN and SPI?+

Yes. Rigol lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C, SPI and CAN decoding as standard on the DHO800 series, with up to four protocols decoded at once. The practical limit on the DHO802 is inputs: SPI with chip select needs three lines, and it has two displayed channels.

Do they run from a USB-C power bank?+

Both take power through USB Type-C at 15 V and 3 A per Rigol, with a 45 W maximum. A source has to supply 15 V at that current; a phone charger that only offers 5 V or 9 V won't run them. Check your power bank's USB-C PD output profiles before relying on it in the field.
