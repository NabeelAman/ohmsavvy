Soldering Iron Temperature by Metal

The right iron temperature depends on two things at once: the solder alloy you're using and how much metal mass the joint has to heat. The same 300°C setting that works perfectly on a small signal wire can genuinely struggle on a thick ground connection, not because the temperature is wrong, but because more mass pulls heat away faster than the iron can replace it at that setting.

The two variables: solder alloy and thermal mass

"What temperature should my iron be?" actually depends on two separate things. First, the solder alloy itself has a melting point the tip needs to clear with some margin — leaded solders melt lower than lead-free ones. Second, the joint's thermal mass (how much surrounding metal is pulling heat away) determines how much hotter than the melting point the tip needs to run, and how long you need to hold it there, to actually bring the joint itself up to temperature rather than just the solder touching the tip.

Setpoint by solder alloy

Iron temperature needs to clear the solder's melting point with enough margin to melt it quickly rather than slowly — running only a few degrees above the melting point means weak, slow heat transfer into the joint.

60/40 leaded (melts 183–191°C)
~300–330°C typical setpoint
63/37 leaded (melts 183°C)
~300–330°C typical setpoint
Lead-free Sn-Ag-Cu (melts ~217–220°C)
~340–370°C typical setpoint

Base metals: copper, brass, nickel, steel, aluminum

The metal being joined matters too, mainly through how well solder wets it and how fast it conducts heat away. Copper and tinned copper wet easily but conduct heat very well, so heavy copper needs a larger tip rather than a dramatically hotter one. Brass wets well with ordinary rosin flux once it is clean. Nickel-plated battery tabs and connector shells wet more slowly and often need extra flux. Steel conducts heat poorly, so it is easy to heat locally, but plain and stainless steel usually need an aggressive acid-type flux that must never be used on electronics. Aluminum forms a tough oxide layer that ordinary solder and flux won't break through; it needs a dedicated aluminum solder and flux system.

BrassGood once cleanedNormal to upper setpoint; brass terminals are often bulky

Plain / stainless steelPoor; needs acid fluxNot for electronics work; follow the flux maker's guidance
AluminumVery poor with standard solderSpecial aluminum solder and flux only

Why thermal mass changes the picture

The same tip, at the same setpoint, delivers heat into very different surroundings depending on the joint. A small, isolated pad has almost nowhere for that heat to escape to, so it comes up to temperature quickly. A pad connected to a wide copper pour or ground plane has a huge amount of surrounding metal pulling heat away in every direction, so the same tip at the same temperature takes noticeably longer to bring that specific joint up to melting point.

Why hotter isn't simply better

Running the iron hotter than a joint needs doesn't make soldering faster or easier — it accelerates tip oxidation (see our tip care guide), increases the risk of heat damage to components and board traces, and can scorch flux before it has a chance to do its job cleaning the joint. Match the setting to the joint instead of defaulting to the highest setting "to be safe."

Signs your temperature setting needs adjusting

Rather than guessing a number, watch for these signs during normal soldering:

What you're seeingLikely adjustment
Solder takes 3+ seconds to melt on a normal jointRaise the temperature, or check for a dirty tip first
Flux is smoking heavily or scorching dark before solder flowsLower the temperature
Joints cool and solidify oddly slowly after the iron is removedTemperature may be too high; excess heat soaked into the board
Large joints never fully melt despite a high settingSwitch to a larger tip or higher-wattage iron rather than raising temperature further

Preheating for extreme thermal mass

For a board with many large ground-plane joints, or dense rework where several big pads sit close together, some people preheat the whole board with an external heat source — a hot plate, or hot air held well back and moved continuously — before touching it with the iron. Bringing the board partway up to temperature first means the iron only has to supply the last stretch of heat at each joint, rather than fighting the full thermal mass of the board from room temperature alone. This is a step beyond what a single joint usually needs, worth reaching for only when the quick reference below genuinely isn't enough.

Quick reference: where to start

  • General through-hole electronics, leaded solder: 300–330°C
  • Fine SMD work, leaded solder: 280–310°C with a fine tip
  • Lead-free solder, general work: 340–370°C
  • Large ground planes or thick wire: upper end of range, plus a larger tip and longer dwell

These are reasonable starting points, not fixed rules — adjust based on what you actually see at the joint, as covered above.

1Start at the alloy's typical setpoint

Pick the starting figure from the chart above for your solder: around 300–330°C for leaded alloys and 340–370°C for lead-free. Let the station settle on that number before you judge anything.

2Time a test joint

On a clean, freshly tinned tip, solder one representative joint and count. Wetting within about one to three seconds means the setting suits that joint. Much longer means too little heat is reaching it; much quicker, with flux smoking hard, suggests you can come down.

3Change the tip before the temperature

If a heavy joint is slow, fit a broader tip and retest at the same setting. Raise the temperature in small steps of about 10–20°C only if a bigger tip isn't practical, and drop it back when you return to small joints.

Heat and fume safety

Tips at 300–370°C cause serious burns instantly. Always return the iron to its stand, keep the cord clear of the hot barrel, and work with ventilation or fume extraction, especially at the higher lead-free settings where flux smokes more. Acid fluxes for steel give off corrosive fumes and belong in a separate, well-ventilated workspace.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should I start at if I'm not sure?

Around 300-330°C (570-630°F) for leaded solder on typical through-hole electronics work is a reasonable, widely-used starting point. Adjust upward if solder is melting slowly or joints are taking more than two to three seconds to wet, and downward if you're seeing scorched flux or damaged components.

Does wire gauge affect the right temperature setting?

It affects how long you need to hold the joint at temperature more than the setpoint itself. Thicker wire has more thermal mass and pulls heat away from the tip faster, so it benefits from either a slightly higher setpoint, a larger tip for better contact area, or simply a touch more dwell time — not a fundamentally different temperature range.

Can I just leave my iron at one temperature for everything?

For a lot of general hobby work, yes — many hobbyists pick one setpoint in the 300-350°C range for leaded solder and rarely change it. Where it matters is at the extremes: very fine SMD work benefits from a slightly lower, more controlled setting, and large ground-plane joints benefit from going higher or switching to a bigger tip rather than fighting a joint that won't heat through.

Is it true that a lower temperature is always gentler on components?

Not quite — a temperature too low for the joint often means holding the iron there much longer to compensate, and that extended dwell time can do more heat damage to a component than a correctly higher setting applied briefly. Matching temperature to the joint, rather than always running cool, usually protects components better.

What temperature is 350°C in Fahrenheit, and which unit should I set?

350°C is 662°F, using °F = °C × 9 ÷ 5 + 32. Likewise 300°C is 572°F and 370°C is 698°F. Use whichever unit your station displays, but stick to one: most solder datasheets and tutorials quote Celsius, so many people switch their station to °C to avoid converting every figure they read.
