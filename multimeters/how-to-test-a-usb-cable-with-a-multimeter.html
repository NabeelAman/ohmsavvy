How to Test a USB Cable With a Multimeter

To test a USB cable with a multimeter, set the meter to continuity and check each pin from one plug to the matching pin on the other (VBUS, D+, D− and ground), then measure the resistance or the voltage drop of the power wires to see whether the cable can carry charging current. A cable can pass continuity on every pin and still charge slowly, because thin power wires drop too much voltage under load. This guide covers both checks, the USB-C CC resistor that decides whether a charger turns on at all, and how to spot a charge-only cable.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 15, 2026

A palm-sized meter like the Fluke 107 handles continuity and resistance on small connectors. See our Fluke 107 review.

Unplug the cable before resistance and continuity tests

  • Continuity and resistance modes must only be used on an unpowered cable. Disconnect both ends from any charger, computer or device before probing.
  • USB-C pins are 0.5 mm apart. A probe tip that bridges VBUS and an adjacent pin in a live port can damage the charger or device, so do live voltage checks only through a breakout board.
  • A cable with melted plastic, a scorched plug or exposed wires should be thrown away, not tested. A discolored port on the charger or device needs attention too.

What a USB cable has to do

A basic USB 2.0 cable has four wires: VBUS (5 V power), ground, and a data pair, D+ and D−. USB-C adds a configuration channel (CC) wire that lets the charger and device agree on current and voltage, plus extra SuperSpeed pairs in full-featured cables. For charging, three things matter: the power wires must be intact and thick enough, the data pair must be present if the device uses it to detect a fast charger, and on USB-C the CC wiring must be correct or the charger may never switch on.

The current demands are significant. USB 2.0 ports are specified for 500 mA and USB 3.x ports for 900 mA, dedicated chargers under the Battery Charging 1.2 specification supply up to 1.5 A, USB-C current advertisement allows 1.5 or 3 A at 5 V, and USB Power Delivery reaches 3 A, or 5 A with an e-marked cable, at up to 20 V in its standard power range. A cable that is fine at 500 mA can drop a phone out of fast charging at 3 A.

1Get access to the pins

USB-A plug pins are big enough to touch with fine probe tips. USB-C, micro-USB and Lightning pins are not. The reliable method is a pair of breakout boards, one for each plug type, that turn every pin into a labeled pad or screw terminal. Plug each end of the cable into a breakout and probe the pads. Don't push probe tips into a USB-C plug; you'll bend the contacts and may short neighbors. If you must cut a cable to test it, it has already failed: cut cables can't be safely repaired for charging use.

2Check continuity, pin by pin

Set the meter to continuity, touch the probes together to confirm the beep, then probe each pin at one end and its partner at the other end: VBUS to VBUS, ground to ground, D+ to D+, D− to D−. On USB-A the pins are numbered 1 to 4 (VBUS, D−, D+, GND). On USB-C, VBUS sits on A4, B4, A9 and B9 and ground on A1, B1, A12 and B12; any one of each is enough to probe. On micro-USB, VBUS is pin 1, D− pin 2, D+ pin 3, ID pin 4 and ground pin 5.

While the meter is beeping on VBUS, flex the cable firmly near each plug. A beep that stutters means a broken strand. Many meters have a MIN MAX or a fast continuity response that catches brief breaks better than your ear does; our broken wire guide covers the technique. Open circuit (OL) on D+ and D− with VBUS and ground intact means a charge-only cable.

3Check for shorts between conductors

With the far end unplugged, measure between VBUS and ground at one end. You should see OL. On a USB-A to USB-C cable, also expect OL from D+ to D− and from each data line to VBUS and ground. A reading of a few ohms or a beep between VBUS and ground is a short, which is why some chargers refuse to start or shut off immediately with that cable. The one deliberate exception is the CC resistor in step 6, which reads tens of kilohms, not a short.

4Measure the power-wire resistance

Switch to the lowest ohms range and short the probes together. Note the reading, often 0.1 to 0.3 Ω, or press REL to zero it. Then measure VBUS end to end and ground end to end and add them: the current flows out through one and back through the other. For a 1 m cable, the copper alone gives roughly 0.21 Ω per meter for 28 AWG, 0.084 Ω for 24 AWG and 0.033 Ω for 20 AWG, so the round trip is twice that. Plug contacts add a little more. For more on low-ohm readings, see how to measure resistance with a multimeter.

5Measure the voltage drop under load

A resistance reading near the meter's lead resistance is hard to trust, so the more decisive test is voltage drop. Plug the cable into a 5 V charger through a breakout at the charger end and a breakout plus a load (an electronic load or a power resistor) at the far end. Measure VBUS to ground at each end while the load draws a known current. The difference divided by the current is the round-trip resistance. With a 5 Ω load at about 1 A, a drop of 0.15 V means 0.15 Ω, which is a good cable.

The USB 2.0 specification expects a port to deliver at least about 4.75 V at its connector, and many devices back off their charging current as their input voltage sags. A cable that loses half a volt or more at 2 A is the likely reason a phone charges at a crawl on one cable and quickly on another. The same check on a power supply's own output is covered in how to test a DC power supply.

6Check the USB-C CC line

USB-C chargers don't switch on VBUS until they detect a device through the CC pin, so the CC wiring matters as much as the power wires. What you should find depends on the cable type, according to the USB Type-C specification:

Cable typeWhat to measureExpected reading
USB-A to USB-CVBUS to CC (A5) on the C plugAbout 56 kΩ (pull-up, Rp)
USB-C to USB-CCC (A5) end to endContinuity, near 0 Ω
USB-C to micro-BCC (A5) to ground on the C plugAbout 5.1 kΩ (pull-down, Rd)
Any cableVBUS to groundOpen circuit (OL)

A USB-C to USB-C cable with no CC continuity will usually charge nothing. An A-to-C cable with a low resistor value instead of 56 kΩ (some early cables used 10 kΩ) tells the phone it may draw up to 3 A from an old USB-A port that may only be designed for 500 mA to 1.5 A. A multimeter can't read the e-marker chip that 5 A cables need; if a 100 W charger tops out at 60 W, that is the likely reason, as our guide on a USB-C charger not delivering full power explains.

Continuity says connected; voltage drop says capable

A cable can beep on every pin and still starve a phone. Check continuity for broken and missing wires, then measure the voltage drop under load to see whether the power wires can actually carry charging current.

Which multimeter to use to test a USB cable

You need a fast continuity beep, a low ohms range you can zero, and enough resolution to see a 0.1 V difference. A 6,000-count meter resolves 1 mV on its 6 V range, which is plenty. If a cable checks out but your power bank still won't fill, see why a power bank isn't charging.

Frequently asked questions

Can you test a USB cable with a multimeter?

Yes. Continuity mode confirms each wire connects end to end and that no two wires are shorted. Resistance or a voltage-drop test under load shows whether the power wires are thick enough for fast charging. A multimeter can't confirm data speed or USB Power Delivery capability, which need a protocol tester or the device itself, but it finds most broken and weak cables.

How do I tell if a USB cable is charge-only?

Check continuity on the D+ and D− pins from one end to the other. A charge-only cable has VBUS and ground but no data wires, so D+ and D− show open circuit, OL on the meter. Phones often show 'charging' but never appear on a computer with these cables. They work for charging, though often only at default USB current.

What resistance should a good USB cable have?

It depends on length and wire gauge, but a good 1 m charging cable usually measures well under half an ohm for the VBUS and ground wires together, and quality cables with 20 to 24 AWG power wires measure around 0.07 to 0.2 Ω round trip. Your meter's leads add a tenth of an ohm or more, so zero them first or use the voltage-drop method.

Why does my USB-C cable charge slowly when continuity is fine?

Continuity only shows that a path exists, not how good it is. Thin power wires, a worn plug, or a missing e-marker chip can all limit charging. USB-C cables need an e-marker to carry more than 3 A, so a non-marked cable caps a 100 W charger at 60 W. A voltage drop of more than a few tenths of a volt at 2 A also slows charging.

What is the 56k resistor in a USB-C cable?

Cables with a USB-A plug on one end and USB-C on the other are required by the USB Type-C specification to include a 56 kΩ pull-up from VBUS to the CC pin. It tells the device that a default-power legacy source is attached, so it won't try to draw more current than an old USB-A port can supply. A wrong value can let devices overload old ports.
