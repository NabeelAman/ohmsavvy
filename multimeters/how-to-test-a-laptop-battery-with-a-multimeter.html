How to Test a Laptop Battery With a Multimeter

To test a laptop battery with a multimeter, take out the removable pack, set the meter to DC volts and read across the positive and negative power contacts, then compare with the pack's rating: a 3-cell (3S) 10.8–11.1 V pack reads about 9–12.6 V, and a 4-cell (4S) 14.4–14.8 V pack about 12–16.8 V, depending on charge. A reading of 0 V doesn't always mean the pack is dead, because many laptop batteries keep their output switched off until the laptop talks to them over the SMBus pins. This page shows how to find the right pins, what each voltage means, when the onboard battery report is the better test, and why you should almost never open the pack.

Laptop packs are lithium batteries: treat them that way

  • Stop if the pack is swollen, cracked, hot or smells sweet. A swollen pack can push the trackpad or keyboard up; unplug the laptop, don't charge it, and take it to a repair shop or battery recycler.
  • Don't pry open the pack or puncture its case. The cells inside are in series at 11–17 V and can deliver many amps into a short, and a punctured cell can catch fire.
  • Touch the probes only to the power pins. Bridging adjacent pins with a probe tip can short the pack or damage its controller.
  • For built-in batteries, disconnect the battery only by following the maker's service manual, or leave it to an authorized repair center. If a pack vents smoke, move away and call 911.

How a laptop battery pack works

A laptop battery is a group of lithium-ion cells wired in series to raise the voltage and sometimes in parallel to add capacity, plus a small circuit board. The series count sets the voltage: each Li-ion cell is about 3.6–3.7 V nominal and 4.2 V full, so three in series (3S) give 10.8–11.1 V nominal and 12.6 V full, and four in series (4S) give 14.4–14.8 V nominal and 16.8 V full. Older removable packs used 18650 cells; most newer thin laptops use flat lithium-polymer pouch cells, but the voltages are the same.

The circuit board is a battery management system (BMS) with a fuel-gauge chip. It measures each cell group, counts charge in and out, reports to the laptop over a two-wire SMBus link (the Smart Battery standard most laptops follow), and controls a pair of MOSFETs that connect or disconnect the cells from the output pins. That last part is why a multimeter test on a laptop battery is less straightforward than on a car battery or an AA cell: you are often reading the BMS's output switch, not the cells directly.

1Read the label on the pack

Power the laptop down, unplug it, and remove the battery. The label lists the nominal voltage (for example 11.1 V or 14.4 V), the capacity in mAh or Wh, and often the number of cells, such as "6-cell" (usually 3S2P, three in series and two in parallel) or "4-cell". Write down the nominal voltage and divide it by 3.6 or 3.7 to find the series count: 11.1 ÷ 3.7 = 3, so the full-charge voltage is 3 × 4.2 = 12.6 V. The capacity figure is what the battery report will compare against later.

2Find the positive and negative power pins

Most removable packs have a row of 6 to 9 contacts. The power pins are usually grouped at the two ends, often two or three in parallel for current capacity, and the signal pins sit in between: SMBus clock and data, a thermistor, and a battery-present or ID line. Some packs print + and − beside the contacts; if not, the maker's service manual or the pack's part number usually leads to a pinout.

If there is no pinout, set the meter to DC volts and read between the two end pins. The pair that gives the largest steady voltage, close to the label figure, is the power pair. Signal pins usually read a few volts or nothing relative to ground. Fine probe tips help; avoid pushing them into the contact sleeves, which can spread them.

3Measure the pack voltage

Put the red probe on a positive pin and the black probe on a negative pin and hold them steady. Compare the reading with the table further down. A 3S pack reading 11.4 V is roughly half charged; 12.5 V is nearly full; 9.5 V is close to empty.

A reading well below the empty figure, say 6 V on a 3S pack, means the cells have been over-discharged, typically after months in a drawer. The controller will usually refuse to charge it, and that is a deliberate safety feature: deeply discharged lithium cells can develop internal shorts. Recycle that pack.

4If the pack reads 0 V, work out why

Three different conditions produce 0 V at the power pins, and the multimeter alone can't always tell them apart. First, the pack may simply hold its output off until a host identifies it over SMBus. This is common on smart packs, and those cells are fine; the test is to put the pack back in the laptop and see whether it charges. Second, the protection circuit may have tripped on over-discharge or over-current and be waiting for a charge voltage to reset. Leave it in the laptop on the adapter for an hour or two. Third, the fuel-gauge chip may have set a permanent-failure flag after a cell fault and blown a chemical fuse. That pack is finished by design.

If the pack stays at 0 V and the laptop reports "no battery detected" or "plugged in, not charging" with a known-good adapter, replace the pack. Before you buy, rule out the charger: the steps in why a laptop charger isn't charging the battery take five minutes and catch a surprising number of "dead battery" cases. The general behavior of protection boards is covered in the battery protection circuit test.

5Check the laptop's battery report for capacity

Voltage tells you charge level, not health. The pack's fuel gauge already tracks capacity, and the laptop will show it. On Windows, open a command prompt and run powercfg /batteryreport, then open the HTML file it saves and compare Full Charge Capacity with Design Capacity. On a Mac, hold Option, click the Apple menu, choose System Information, then Power, for cycle count and condition. Built-in batteries on most modern laptops can only be tested this way without opening the machine.

Work out the percentage. A pack designed for 56 Wh that now reports 41 Wh full is at 41 ÷ 56 ≈ 73%. Many makers and battery warranties use 80% of design capacity as the point where a battery is considered worn, so this one runs about a quarter less time than it did new.

The meter checks charge; the report checks health

A multimeter on the pack contacts tells you whether the pack is awake and how full it is. Capacity, the thing that actually sets runtime, comes from the pack's own fuel gauge, which the laptop reports.

Which multimeter to use for laptop batteries

Laptop packs sit under 20 V DC, so any multimeter's DC voltage range covers them. The practical needs are fine probe tips for small contacts, a display you can read at 0.01 V, and a meter that also handles the charger and wall-outlet checks that usually come next. Frequently asked questions

What voltage should a laptop battery read with a multimeter?

It depends on how many lithium-ion cells are in series. A 3S pack labeled 10.8 or 11.1 V reads about 12.6 V full and around 9 V empty. A 4S pack labeled 14.4 or 14.8 V reads about 16.8 V full and around 12 V empty. A 2S pack labeled 7.2 or 7.4 V reads about 8.4 V full. Readings far below those suggest over-discharge or a fault.

Why does my laptop battery read 0 volts?

Many smart laptop batteries keep their output MOSFETs off until the laptop identifies the pack over SMBus, so a meter on the bare contacts sees 0 V even when the cells are fine. A pack can also read 0 V because its protection circuit tripped after deep discharge or a fault, or because a permanent-failure flag was set. Try charging it in the laptop first.

Can a dead laptop battery be revived?

Sometimes. If the pack only went to sleep, leaving it in the laptop on the charger for a few hours can wake it. If the cells were over-discharged and the controller has locked the pack, it is designed to stay locked for safety. Rebuilding packs with new cells requires resetting the controller and handling bare lithium cells, so a replacement pack is the safer choice.

How do I check laptop battery health without opening it?

Use the laptop's own report, because the pack's controller counts charge in and out. On Windows, run powercfg /batteryreport in a command prompt and compare full charge capacity with design capacity. On a Mac, hold Option and open System Information, then Power, to see cycle count and condition. Under about 80% of design capacity, expect noticeably shorter runtime.

Is it safe to test the cells inside a laptop battery?

It is risky. The plastic case is glued shut, prying it open can puncture or short a cell, and the cells are wired in series to more than 12 V with high available current. Swollen packs are especially dangerous. Unless you're experienced with lithium cells, test from the outside connector, use the battery report, and recycle or replace the pack instead.
