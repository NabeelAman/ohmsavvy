Fluke 283 FC vs Fluke 289: Wireless Testing or Data Logging?

Pick the Fluke 283 FC if your work is PV commissioning and DC systems up to 1500 V: its a283 FC clamp radios up to 60 A AC/DC to the meter, and Fluke Connect pushes live trends and reports to your phone. Pick the Fluke 289 if you want a self-contained logger for industrial and bench work: 50,000 counts, a documented 10,000-interval memory with graphs on its own screen, microamps, milliohms and a 10 A input. Both log; the difference is where the data lives and what it measures. On Amazon the 283 FC is sold only as the 283 FC/PV kit (meter, a283 FC clamp, MC4 and TL175-HV leads).

Key differences

  • →Voltage: the 283 FC is ranged and rated for 1500 V DC (CAT III 1500V). The 289 stops at 1000 V (CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V).
  • →Current: the 283 FC reads 0–60 A AC/DC only through its wireless clamp and has no current jacks. The 289 measures in series from 0.01 µA resolution up to 10 A, with no clamp included.
  • →Logging: both log with a real-time clock. The 289 documents up to 10,000 intervals and graphs them on its own screen; the 283 FC streams to Fluke Connect for live trends and cloud reports, and Fluke does not state its memory size.
  • →Resolution: the 289 shows 50,000 counts with ±(0.025% + 2) DC volts, a 50 Ω range at 0.001 Ω and frequency to 999.99 kHz. The 283 FC shows 6,000 counts.
  • →Solar and field extras: only the 283 FC has polarity alerts, a limit gauge, a readiness self-check, MC4 leads, IP52 and a 2 m drop rating. Only the 289 has LoZ, a low-pass filter and temperature.

Two meanings of "connected"

Both meters can record readings over time with a time stamp, so the real question is not "which one logs?" but "where does the data need to go, and how fast?" The 283 FC is built around radio links: the clamp talks to the meter, and the meter talks to a phone. That suits a solar crew moving from string to string, where a supervisor or remote engineer wants to see results the moment they are taken. The 289 is built around its own memory and screen: it sits clipped to a panel or on a bench for hours, draws the trend itself, and gives the data up through an optical cable when you get back to a computer.

Neither path is better in general. Wireless saves walking and lets you read a clamp closed inside an enclosure from a safe distance, which Fluke specifically calls out for the a283 FC. A stand-alone logger keeps working where there is no phone signal, no app login and no one watching, and the 289 tells you exactly how many intervals it can hold. For the general version of this trade-off between Fluke's wireless and logging meters, see Fluke 3000 FC vs Fluke 289; this page stays on PV commissioning against bench and industrial logging.

The two charts on current tell the story of each meter's customer. The 289 resolves a sleeping circuit's microamps or a contact's milliohms, which matters on a bench or in a control panel. The 283 FC ignores that end of the scale and reads tens of amps of DC from a string conductor with no interruption, which matters on an array. One spec gap you should know about: Fluke states the 289's memory size but not the 283 FC's, so if you need a fixed number of hours of unattended recording at a fixed interval, only the 289 lets you plan that from the datasheet.

PV commissioning: where the 283 FC earns its place

Commissioning a large array means repeating the same few checks on every string: polarity, open-circuit voltage, and current once the inverter is producing. The 283 FC/PV kit is shaped around that loop. The MC4 leads plug into the string connectors, the polarity indicator sounds if a pair is reversed, and the user limit gauge flags any Voc outside the window you set for that string length, so a missing module jumps out. The a283 FC then reads string current at the combiner while the meter computes VA, and each reading is time-stamped for the turnover package. Sending results straight to the Fluke Connect app means the commissioning report can be assembled as you go, and ShareLive video calls let an engineer off site look at the same reading.

The 289 cannot do this job on 1500 V systems at all, because its rating ends at 1000 V. On a residential array below 600 V it is safe to use, but every current reading needs the circuit opened, and it has no polarity alarm or pass/fail gauge. Our Fluke 283 FC vs Fluke 87V comparison digs further into string voltage, cold-weather Voc and DC clamp technique.

Bench and industrial logging: where the 289 wins

Picture an intermittent nuisance trip on a 480 V control panel, or a battery-powered device that drains overnight. You want a meter that records unattended, catches the event and shows you when it happened. The 289 does that on its own: set the interval, leave it, then scroll the TrendCapture graph on its screen to the dip or spike. Its 50,000-count display resolves small drifts the 283 FC's 6,000 counts would round away, its low-pass filter steadies readings on drive outputs, LoZ clears ghost voltage on long control runs, and its 500 µA range resolves 0.01 µA for sleep-current work. Frequency to 999.99 kHz and a thermocouple input round out a toolkit the 283 FC does not try to match.

The 289's data path is older: an IR optical cable to FlukeView Forms on a PC rather than a phone app. For a maintenance tech who files reports from a desk, that is no hardship. For a deeper look at the 289 against its siblings, read Fluke 289 vs Fluke 287 and Fluke 87V vs Fluke 289. Neither meter has been hands-on tested by us: the 289 is rated 4.7 and the 283 FC/PV kit 4.5, both from Fluke's published specs and their Amazon listings.

Wireless solves distance; logging solves time

Choose the 283 FC when the problem is getting a reading from somewhere awkward or dangerous to stand, and getting it to someone else quickly. Choose the 289 when the problem is catching what a circuit does over hours while nobody watches. Remember that the 283 FC comes only as the 283 FC/PV kit on Amazon, and its clamp carries a one-year warranty while the meter and the 289 carry lifetime coverage.

Safety: match the meter to the system voltage, and never break live DC

Check the highest voltage the system can produce before you pick up either meter. The 289 is rated CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V and must not be used on 1500 V PV strings; use the 283 FC with leads rated CAT III 1500V. PV modules produce voltage whenever light reaches them, so a string stays live in daylight even with the disconnect open. Never open an MC4 connector, fuse holder or conductor under load to insert a meter in series: a DC arc will not go out on its own. Read string current with a clamp. When you leave either meter logging inside an energized panel, route leads so the door closes without pinching them, keep the enclosure closed while it records, and follow your site's lockout and arc-flash rules. Call a qualified electrician or PV installer for anything damaged, scorched or tripping repeatedly.

Choose the Fluke 289 if…

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 283 FC, which Amazon sells as the 283 FC/PV kit, if you commission or service solar and other DC systems: CAT III 1500V, a wireless 60 A AC/DC clamp, MC4 leads, polarity alerts and live trending in Fluke Connect. Buy the Fluke 289 if you need a stand-alone logger for industrial and bench work: 50,000 counts, a documented 10,000-interval memory with on-meter graphs, microamp and milliohm resolution, LoZ and a 10 A input, all up to 1000 V.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Fluke 283 FC log data like the Fluke 289?+

Yes, but differently. Fluke says the 283 FC logs time- and date-stamped readings with a real-time clock and user-set duration and interval, and streams live trends to the Fluke Connect app. It does not state the memory size. The 289 documents up to 10,000 recording intervals and draws TrendCapture graphs on its own screen.

Can the Fluke 289 be used on solar panels?+

Only on systems that stay within its CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V rating, such as most residential arrays. It cannot be used on 1500 V strings. It also measures current only in series up to 10 A, so reading string current means opening the circuit, which is unsafe under load. A DC clamp is the safer method.

Does the Fluke 289 have Fluke Connect or Bluetooth?+

No. The 289 transfers stored data through an infrared optical cable to Fluke's FlukeView Forms software on a PC. The 283 FC uses Fluke Connect to send readings to a phone or tablet for live trending, cloud upload, shareable reports and ShareLive video calls, and its clamp links to the meter wirelessly too.

Why is the Fluke 283 FC only sold as a kit on Amazon?+

Amazon.com lists it only as the 283 FC/PV kit; we found no bare-meter listing. The kit makes sense because the 283 FC has no current jacks, so the included a283 FC clamp is its only way to read amps. The kit also adds Stäubli MC4 solar leads, TL175-HV leads, a case and a magnetic strap.

Which is more accurate, the Fluke 283 FC or the 289?+

The 289. It shows 50,000 counts and Fluke lists ±(0.025% + 2) DC volts on its lower ranges, against 6,000 counts and ±(0.09% + 3) on the 283 FC. The 289 also resolves 0.01 µA and 0.001 Ω. For PV string comparisons the 283 FC is precise enough; the 289's resolution matters for electronics and drift logging.

Which meter is tougher for outdoor work?+

Fluke rates the 283 FC meter and clamp IP52 with a 2 m drop rating and an operating range of -10 to 60 °C, which suits rooftops and solar fields. We did not find an IP or drop figure for the 289 in our Fluke sources. The 289 is better treated as a panel and bench logger than a field meter.
