PicoScope 2204A vs Hantek 1008C

PicoScope 2204A vs Hantek 1008C compares two screenless USB scopes built for opposite jobs. The 2204A is an electronics tool: two channels at 10 MHz, 100 MS/s, 40 serial decoders, a generator and AWG, and software for Windows, macOS and Linux. The 1008C is a car tool: eight 12-bit channels at 2.4 MSa/s, 400 V inputs, an eight-channel crank and cam simulator and more than 80 guided automotive tests. Pico itself says the 2204A doesn't work with its automotive software, so circuit boards point to the Pico and cars point to the Hantek.

Key differences

  • →Channels: two on the 2204A, eight on the 1008C.
  • →Speed: 10 MHz and 100 MS/s on the Pico against 100 kHz and 2.4 MSa/s on the Hantek, about 40 times the sample rate.
  • →Voltage: the Pico's ranges top out at ±20 V; the Hantek takes 400 V (DC + AC peak), twenty times more.
  • →Resolution: the Hantek is 12-bit in hardware; the Pico is 8-bit, enhanced to 12-bit in software.

Spec by spec

Every chart points the same way: the Pico is faster and smarter, the Hantek is wider and tougher. The speed charts use a log scale because the gaps are large.

The Pico also holds a 10,000-waveform circular buffer and updates up to 2000 waveforms per second, per its listing, so you can step back through earlier captures to find a glitch. Hantek gives neither figure for the 1008C.

PicoScope 2204A vs Hantek 1008C on a car

A car's 12 V system runs at about 14 V when charging, which fits inside the Pico's ±20 V. The trouble comes when an injector or ignition coil primary switches off: the collapsing field throws a spike well above the supply, commonly tens of volts on injectors and more on coil primaries. That is past the 2204A's range, while the Hantek's 400 V limit has room for it.

The software gap is just as large. The 1008C's package walks you through tests for ignition, sensors, bus activity, starting and charging, with reference waveforms and video help, and produces a one-key report for the customer. PicoScope 7 on the 2204A is a general instrument; Pico's own listing points car technicians to its automotive models instead. With eight channels the Hantek can also show crank, both cams and several injectors in one capture, which reveals timing relationships the Pico's two channels can't.

The one car job the Pico does better

Bus signals. High-speed CAN at 500 kbit/s has 2 µs bits. At 100 MS/s the Pico takes 200 samples per bit and can decode the frames; at 2.4 MSa/s the Hantek takes under five samples per bit through a 100 kHz front end, enough to see that the bus is active but not to judge edge shape or read the data. The Hantek 6022BE vs 1008C page works through the same CAN arithmetic against a cheaper USB scope.

12-bit hardware vs 12-bit enhanced

The Hantek's converter resolves 4,096 levels on every sample. The Pico's 12-bit figure comes from software resolution enhancement on an 8-bit converter, which works by averaging neighboring samples and so trades away bandwidth to gain detail. On slow sensor signals both get you fine vertical detail; on fast edges only the Pico's speed helps.

What each one can and can't measure

The 2204A covers signals up to about 2–3 MHz at close to true size: audio, PWM, I2C at 400 kHz, UART and slower SPI. Its 8 kS block memory is short, but streaming to the PC with 100 MS of buffer stretches long captures at lower rates. It can't take more than ±20 V on its ranges or watch more than two signals. The 1008C covers slow signals to about 20–30 kHz at full size: crank and cam sensors, injector pulse widths, alternator ripple and slow sensors over many minutes, with a timebase to 20000 s/div. It can't show fast logic, can't decode a bus and has only 4K points per capture.

Car electrics and laptops

Both scopes take their ground from the laptop's USB port. Run the laptop on battery when probing a vehicle, and never clip a ground lead to anything other than chassis or battery negative. Ignition secondary must be read through a capacitive pickup, not a direct lead. Keep cables away from fans and belts on a running engine, and run it outdoors or with exhaust extraction.

The trust question: software support

Pico publishes a full datasheet, sells through its own Amazon store, offers a five-year warranty and supports three operating systems. Hantek's 1008C page is thinner: it repeats the automotive function count two different ways ("over 80" and "78"), puts the 100 kHz bandwidth only in the manual, and lists Windows as far back as XP without a current macOS or Linux option. If the scope will be used for years, Pico's paperwork is the stronger bet; if the job is cars, the Hantek is the one built for it.

Choose the PicoScope 2204A if…

  • ✓You work on microcontrollers, audio and sensors under a few MHz
  • ✓You want serial decoding for I2C, SPI, UART and CAN from one USB device
  • ✓You use a Mac or Linux laptop
  • ✓You want Pico's five-year warranty and ongoing software updates

Choose the Hantek 1008C if…

  • ✓You diagnose engines and want guided tests with reference waveforms
  • ✓You need to watch crank, cam and several injectors at the same time
  • ✓Your signals swing past 20 V: injector and coil primary circuits
  • ✓You want to simulate crank and cam signals into an ECU on the bench

Neither if…

You want a scope with its own screen for the bench or the driveway. Both need a laptop; a benchtop or handheld scope fits that better.

The bottom line

Buy the PicoScope 2204A for electronics: 10 MHz, 100 MS/s, serial decoding, a sweep generator, macOS and Linux support and a five-year warranty. Buy the Hantek 1008C for cars: eight 12-bit channels, 400 V inputs, a crank and cam simulator and over 80 guided tests. Pico says the 2204A doesn't run its automotive software, so don't stretch it into a car scope.

Frequently asked questions

Can the PicoScope 2204A be used for car diagnostics?+

Only in a limited way. Its ranges stop at ±20 V, so injector and coil primary spikes are out of range, and Pico's Amazon listing says it isn't compatible with PicoScope 7 Automotive software. It can still decode CAN and look at 5 V sensor signals. Pico points car technicians to its automotive models instead.

Can the Hantek 1008C be used for electronics projects?+

For slow signals only. Its manual gives a 100 kHz bandwidth and it samples at 2.4 MSa/s, so audio and slow sensors work, but I2C, SPI and microcontroller clocks don't. It has edge triggering and no listed serial decoding. The PicoScope 2204A, at 10 MHz and 100 MS/s, suits that work far better.

Do these USB scopes work on a Mac?+

The PicoScope 2204A does: Pico lists PicoScope 7 for 64-bit Windows, macOS and Linux. Hantek lists only Windows XP, 7, 8 and 10 for the 1008C, so on a Mac you would need a Windows installation, and Hantek doesn't document driver support for that setup.

Which has better vertical resolution?+

The Hantek 1008C is 12-bit in hardware, 4,096 levels per sample. The PicoScope 2204A is 8-bit, 256 levels, with software enhancement up to 12 bits that works by averaging and reduces effective bandwidth. For slow sensor signals both give fine detail; the Hantek gets it without trading speed.

What comes in the box with each?+

Pico's listing names the scope, a USB 2.0 cable, two x1/x10 passive probes and a quick start guide. The 1008C listing names the scope, an instruction CD, an auto probe, four alligator-clip-to-coax cables and four coax connectors. Hantek's page also lists an ignition probe, but the Amazon box list doesn't name one.
