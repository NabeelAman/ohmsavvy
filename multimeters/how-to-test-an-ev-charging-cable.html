HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test an EV Charging Cable With a Multimeter

To test an EV charging cable, unplug it from the wall and the car so it is dead at both ends, inspect the pins and jacket, then use a multimeter on resistance to check the proximity resistor and the continuity of each pin. You never probe a live EVSE or a powered connector. On a J1772 (Type 1) plug, the proximity pin to ground typically reads about 150 Ω with the latch button released and about 480 Ω with it pressed. A reading far from that, an open conductor, or any burn marks means the cable goes back to the maker, the dealer or a qualified electrician.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 13, 2026

A cable test is a de-energized resistance check, but the meter you bring near EV circuits should be rated for them. The Fluke 117 is CAT III 600V with non-contact voltage detection. See our Fluke 117 review.

EV equipment can kill. Read this first

  • Never probe a live EVSE, wall outlet, charging station or connector. Level 2 chargers run at 208 to 240 V and up to 48 A or more. Everything in this guide is done with the cable dead at both ends.
  • Do not open the control box of a portable charger or a wall unit. There are no user-serviceable parts, and opening it usually voids the warranty.
  • If the plug, handle or cable shows melting, scorching, a burning smell, cracked insulation or water inside, stop using it. Contact the charger maker or your dealer, or have a licensed electrician inspect the circuit.
  • Use a meter rated at least CAT III 600V near EV circuits, with leads in good condition.

What you can and can't test on an EV charging cable

A J1772 plug has five contacts: two large AC power pins (L1 and L2 or neutral), a large ground pin (PE) and two small signal pins, the control pilot (CP) and the proximity pilot (PP). The power pins stay dead until the EVSE and the car finish a handshake over CP, so the plug is not live sitting in the holster. That is no reason to probe it while connected. The handshake can start at any moment, and a faulty contactor can leave pins live.

You can check

Visual damage, the proximity resistor and latch switch, and end-to-end continuity on a detachable cable, all unplugged.

Leave to a pro

Supply voltage, pilot signal, GFCI/CCID function, contactor operation and insulation resistance at test voltage.

Dealer territory

The car's charging inlet, onboard charger and any fault codes the car stores.

Which tests apply depends on the cable. A portable charger (the cord with a control box in the middle) and a hardwired wall unit have the J1772 handle permanently attached, so only the handle-end checks are practical. A detachable cable, common with Type 2 in Europe, is just a cable and can be checked end to end. If the problem is the breaker tripping rather than the cable, start with why an EV charger keeps tripping the breaker.

1Unplug the EV charging cable from everything

Unplug the connector from the car first, then unplug the portable charger from its outlet. For a hardwired wall unit, switch off its dedicated breaker and lock or tag it so nobody turns it back on, then confirm the unit's status lights are off. A detachable cable should be disconnected at both ends. Wait a minute before handling the pins. Only then set the meter to resistance and test the leads by touching them together: note the reading, typically 0.1 to 0.3 Ω, so you can subtract it later.

2Inspect the plug, pins and jacket

Use a flashlight. Look for brown or black discoloration around any pin, plastic that has softened or bubbled, pins that are bent, pushed back or loose, and debris or water in the face. Work along the whole cable for flattened sections where it was driven over, cuts, and kinks at the strain reliefs. Press and release the latch button: it should move freely and spring back. Heat damage is a stop sign. A meter cannot tell you a scorched pin is safe.

3Measure the J1772 proximity resistor

The proximity circuit tells the car a plug is inserted and whether the latch button is being pressed. In the J1772 design, a resistor of about 150 Ω connects PP to ground, with a second resistor of about 330 Ω in series. A switch (S3) on the latch button shorts out the 330 Ω while the button is released. Pressing the button opens S3, the total rises to about 480 Ω, and the car takes that as a signal to stop charging before the plug comes out.

Set the meter to the 2 kΩ range (or auto). Hold one probe tip against the PP pin and the other against the large PE ground pin. Use the pin labels molded into the plug face or the maker's diagram to identify them; do not guess. Read with the button released, then press and hold the button and read again.

OL in both positions means the proximity circuit is open, so the car will not see the plug. The same reading in both positions means the latch switch is stuck. Wildly different values point to a damaged resistor or corrosion. Type 2 detachable cables work differently: the resistor between PP and PE at each plug tells the charger the cable's current rating. 4Check pin continuity end to end on a detachable cable

This step only applies to a cable with a plug at each end. With both ends disconnected, measure from each pin at one end to the matching pin at the other: L1, L2 or N (and L2, L3 on a three-phase Type 2), PE and CP. A good conductor reads the same as your shorted leads, give or take a fraction of an ohm. The copper itself contributes almost nothing. A 5 m cable with 6 mm² conductors works out to 0.0172 × 5 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.014 Ω, far below what a handheld meter resolves.

A conductor that reads OL is broken. One that reads a few ohms, or jumps around while you flex the cable near the handles, has broken strands. Flex slowly along the length with the continuity beeper on and listen for drop-outs. Our long-wire continuity guide covers lead extensions for long cables.

5Check for shorts between pins

On the highest resistance range, measure between every pair of power and ground pins on one plug: L1 to N, L1 to PE, N to PE, and each power pin to CP. All should read OL. Do not expect OL from PP to PE, which is the proximity resistor from step 3. On a portable or wall charger, the power pins at the handle connect back to a contactor inside the control box, so they should also read OL to each other with the unit unplugged.

OL on a multimeter is not an insulation test

A multimeter tests with a few volts. Damaged insulation can read OL at 3 V and still break down at 240 V, especially when wet. A proper insulation resistance test at 500 V or more is the job of an electrician with an insulation tester. Your meter can find opens, shorts and a bad proximity circuit. It cannot certify a cable as safe.

When to stop and call someone

Your meter checks narrow the fault to one of three owners. If the cable fails a check, it goes back to the cable or charger maker. If the cable passes but the same car fails on several chargers, the car inlet or onboard charger needs the dealer. If the cable passes and the car charges fine elsewhere, the supply circuit, outlet or EVSE electronics need a licensed electrician or the charger's service line.

For a home that charges slowly rather than not at all, see why an EV charges slowly on Level 2, and for vehicle-to-home setups, testing a bidirectional EV charger.

Which meter to use near EV equipment

The cable tests here are low-voltage resistance checks, so almost any meter can read 150 Ω. The reason to choose carefully is that the same meter will end up near a 240 V panel and an EVSE outlet. Pick one rated at least CAT III 600V from a maker that publishes its safety certification.

Frequently asked questions

Can I test my EV charger with a multimeter while it is plugged in?

No. Do not put probes into a powered EVSE, its outlet or its connector. A Level 2 unit runs at 240 V and tens of amps, and the pins are not designed to be probed. Live checks on the supply, the pilot signal and the contactor belong to a qualified electrician or the charger maker's service team, using proper test adapters and PPE.

What resistance should the J1772 proximity pin read?

Under the SAE J1772 design, the proximity pin to ground reads about 150 Ω with the latch button released and about 480 Ω with it pressed, because pressing the button opens a switch and adds roughly 330 Ω. Small tolerances are normal. Your cable or EVSE maker's documentation is the final word, so check it if your reading is close but not exact.

Why does my car say the charger is not connected?

The car looks for the proximity resistor and the control pilot signal. If the proximity circuit is open, the latch switch is stuck or the pin is dirty or damaged, the car may not detect the plug. Try another charger first. If other chargers work, measure the proximity resistor on your cable with everything unplugged. If other chargers fail too, the car's inlet needs a dealer.

Can a damaged EV charging cable be repaired?

Treat a damaged EV cable as a replacement job. Cut or crushed jackets, melted handles and burnt pins on a high-current connector are not safe to tape or splice. Many makers sell replacement cables or handles for their units, and some portable chargers are covered by warranty. Fitting a new connector to a hardwired unit is work for a qualified electrician.

Why does my EV charging handle get hot?

A connector handle can feel warm during long sessions, but it should not be too hot to hold. Heat usually comes from a worn or loose pin, a dirty contact or a damaged car inlet, all of which add resistance at high current. Many chargers have temperature sensing and will cut power. Stop using it and have the cable and inlet inspected.

Is a Type 1 cable the same as J1772?

Yes, in practice. Type 1 is the IEC name for the SAE J1772 connector, the five-pin AC plug used on most North American and older Japanese EVs. Type 2 is the seven-pin connector used in Europe. Type 2 detachable cables use a different proximity scheme where the resistor value tells the charger the cable's current rating.
