Hantek DSO2C10 vs Hantek DSO2D10

Hantek DSO2C10 vs Hantek DSO2D10 is not really a contest between two scopes. Hantek lists both in one DSO2000 series table with the same 100 MHz bandwidth, two channels, 1 GSa/s, 8 Mpts of memory and serial decoding. The single row that differs is the waveform generator: the DSO2D10 has a one-channel 25 MHz output, the DSO2C10 has none. So the question is whether you will use a built-in signal source.

Spec by spec

Because Hantek publishes both models in a single column-by-column table, this is one of the cleanest comparisons we can make: any figure not listed per model applies to the whole series. The first two charts therefore describe both scopes at once. The last two show the one place they part company.

Do you actually need the generator?

A large share of scope work is passive observation. You clip onto a node and look at what is already there: the clock on a microcontroller, the data on an I2C line, the ripple on a 5 V rail, a PWM signal driving a motor. For that, a generator is idle hardware. The DSO2C10 is listed with the same decoding (UART/RS232, I2C, SPI, CAN, LIN) and the same 8 Mpts memory, which is what makes it the budget decoder in our roundup.

The generator earns its place when the circuit can't test itself. A filter, an amplifier, a comparator or an analog sensor input only reveals its behavior when you feed it a known signal. With the DSO2D10 you set a sine at, say, 1 kHz, connect the output to the circuit's input and channel 1, put channel 2 on the output, then step the frequency up and watch the ratio change. Swap to a square wave to see how a stage handles edges, or load one of the four arbitrary slots to mimic a sensor pulse your firmware has to recognize. Note that this uses both scope channels: the generator adds a source, not an input.

Why 25 MHz is a sensible ceiling

By the usual 3–5x rule, a 100 MHz scope measures signals up to roughly 20–33 MHz comfortably. The DSO2D10's 25 MHz generator sits inside that window, so the scope can display everything the generator can produce. A sine at the top of the range may read slightly low, as it would on any 100 MHz scope. Our bandwidth explainer covers the rule.

High impedance vs 50 Ω

Hantek quotes 10 mV–7 Vpp into a high-impedance load and 5 mV–3.5 Vpp into 50 Ω. The 50 Ω output impedance and a 50 Ω load form a 2:1 divider, which is why the figure halves. Most hobby circuits are high impedance, so expect the larger range there.

Sampling is shared the same way on both. With one channel on, 1 GSa/s gives ten samples per cycle of a 100 MHz sine; with both on, 500 MSa/s still gives five, well above the Nyquist minimum of two. That leaves both models comfortable on the generator's full range and on typical hobby buses. For how the GSa/s figure relates to what you see, read what GSa/s means.

One thing to watch for when buying the DSO2C10: the front panel is shared with the D model, so WAVE GEN, BURST and GEN OUT markings are still printed on it. Hantek's own table shows a dash in the generator row for this model. Treat any listing text that promises waveform output on the DSO2C10 with suspicion and go by Hantek's specification.

Mains-referenced circuits

Both are mains-powered bench scopes, and the probe ground clip on this kind of instrument is normally connected to mains earth. Never clip it onto a mains-referenced node such as the primary side of an off-line supply; use a differential probe or an isolation arrangement set up by someone who knows the hazards.

  • ✓You mainly look at signals a circuit already produces: buses, clocks, PWM and supply rails
  • ✓You already own a function generator, or a microcontroller board can make the test signals you need
  • ✓You want Hantek's serial decoding and 8 Mpts memory without paying for an output you won't use

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓You test filters, amplifiers or sensor inputs and need a known signal to feed in
  • ✓Your bench has room for one instrument, not a scope and a separate generator
  • ✓You want custom test shapes from the four arbitrary waveform slots

The bottom line

Hantek's own table shows these are the same two-channel, 100 MHz scope with the same memory and decoding; only the DSO2D10 adds a 25 MHz single-channel generator. Buy the DSO2D10 if you test filters, amplifiers or sensor inputs and have no separate signal source. Buy the DSO2C10 if your work is mostly watching and decoding signals a circuit already produces, or you already own a generator.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Hantek DSO2C10 the same oscilloscope as the DSO2D10?+

On the scope side, yes. Hantek's DSO2000 series table gives both 100 MHz, two channels, 1 GSa/s on one channel, 8 Mpts of memory and the same serial decoding. The only row that differs is the waveform generator, which the DSO2D10 has and the DSO2C10 does not.

Why does the DSO2C10 have WAVE GEN buttons if it has no generator?+

The two models share a front panel, so the DSO2C10 still carries WAVE GEN, BURST and GEN OUT markings. Hantek's specification table shows a dash in the generator row for the DSO2C10, so go by that and assume it has no signal output, whatever some listing text suggests.

What can the Hantek DSO2D10's built-in generator produce?+

One channel up to 25 MHz at 200 MSa/s and 12-bit, with sine, square, ramp, exponential, noise and DC waveforms plus four arbitrary waveform slots of 4 KSa each. Hantek quotes an output of 10 mV to 7 Vpp into high impedance, or 5 mV to 3.5 Vpp into 50 ohms.

Can the cheaper DSO2C10 decode I2C, SPI and CAN?+

Yes. Hantek lists UART/RS232, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding for the whole DSO2000 series, including the DSO2C10. Skipping the generator does not cost you any bus-debugging features, which is why the DSO2C10 is our budget two-channel decoder pick.

Does the generator on the DSO2D10 use up one of the scope channels?+

No, it is a separate output, but it doesn't add an input either. In a typical stimulus test you connect the generator to the circuit, watch the input on channel 1 and the output on channel 2, which leaves no spare channel for a third signal.
