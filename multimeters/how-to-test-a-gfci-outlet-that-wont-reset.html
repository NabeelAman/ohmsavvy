How to Test a GFCI Outlet That Won't Reset

To test a GFCI outlet that won't reset, work out which of four situations you are in: no power is reaching it, a ground fault is still present, it has been wired with line and load reversed, or it has reached the end of its life. Most GFCIs made in the last couple of decades will not latch unless they have power and pass an internal check, so a RESET button that won't stay in is often the device telling you something specific. A plug-in tester and a multimeter, used only at the outlet face, separate these cases without opening the box.

Before you start

  • If the GFCI or its cover plate is warm, discolored, cracked or smells of burning, switch off its breaker and call an electrician instead of testing it.
  • Every check here is done at the outlet face with the cover on. Removing the receptacle from the box, reconnecting wires or replacing it is electrical work for a licensed electrician if you are not qualified.
  • Use a CAT III-rated meter with intact leads, and keep wet hands and wet floors out of it.

Why a GFCI refuses to reset

A GFCI's RESET button doesn't simply close a switch. Since the UL 943 requirements were revised in the mid-2000s, most GFCIs have reset lockout: the contacts only latch if the device has line power and its electronics confirm they can detect a fault. Many newer models also self-test periodically and lock out permanently when that test fails. So a GFCI that won't reset is often behaving exactly as designed, either because it has nothing to work with or because it no longer trusts itself.

The same design catches one of the most common installation mistakes. The incoming supply belongs on the terminals marked LINE, and outlets further along the circuit connect to LOAD. If the supply is on the LOAD side, the protected outlets would otherwise be powered without protection, so most current GFCIs simply won't latch when wired that way.

1Using TEST and RESET properly

Unplug everything from the GFCI and from any outlets it might protect. Those include outlets in other rooms, garages and outside walls, often marked with a small "GFCI Protected Outlet" sticker. Then press RESET firmly and squarely until it clicks; a half-press is the most common reason a good device seems not to reset. If it latches, press TEST: the button should pop out and the outlet should go dead. Press RESET again to restore it.

If it latches with everything unplugged, plug items back in one at a time. The one that sends RESET out again is leaking current, and the troubleshooting in why a GFCI outlet keeps tripping takes over from here. If it won't latch even with every outlet empty, go on to the power check.

2Check whether power reaches it

A tripped GFCI shows 0 V at its own slots, so measuring there tells you nothing about its supply. Look upstream instead. Find the breaker for the circuit and push it fully OFF and back ON. Then check other outlets on the same breaker that come before the GFCI. If they are live, power is very probably reaching the GFCI's LINE terminals. If they are dead too, the problem is upstream: another GFCI further back, a breaker, or a failed connection.

With the meter on AC volts, place the probes in the hot (shorter slot) and neutral (taller slot) of an upstream outlet, keeping your fingers behind the guards. Expect roughly 114 to 126 V, the normal range for 120 V service under ANSI C84.1. A non-contact voltage tester held at the GFCI's cover plate may beep if the LINE wires in the box are live, but it can't say which terminal they are on, so treat it as a hint rather than proof.

When it does reset, measure at its own slots: hot to neutral and hot to ground should both read near 120 V, and neutral to ground should be close to zero. If the outlet still reads 0 V after a firm reset, the problem is inside the device or its connections. Our guide to testing electrical wires with a multimeter explains the continuity checks an electrician will make once the circuit is de-energized.

Plug-in testers and their limits

A three-light plug-in receptacle tester with a GFCI test button is a handy companion here. Its lights flag an open ground, an open neutral, reversed polarity and some other wiring faults, and its button deliberately leaks a little current from hot to ground to make the GFCI trip. Use it on the GFCI itself and on downstream outlets to confirm they are actually protected.

Two caveats. On a two-wire circuit without an equipment ground, which GFCIs are often used to protect in older homes, the tester's button has no ground path and won't trip the GFCI, even though the GFCI's own TEST button will. And a tester cannot detect a swapped line and load, a bootleg ground or several other faults. It is a quick screening tool, not a diagnosis.

When replacement or professional diagnosis is needed

Replace the GFCI if it shows an end-of-life indicator, won't reset with power confirmed upstream and nothing connected downstream, fails to trip when TEST is pressed, or is cracked, warm or scorched. Call a licensed electrician if you are not qualified to replace it, if a newly installed GFCI has never reset (likely line/load reversal), if upstream outlets are also dead after the breaker is reset, or if water has reached any outlet on the circuit. If several devices act up at once, the cause may be bigger than one outlet; see AFCI breakers that keep tripping and testing a whole-house surge protector.

Frequently asked questions

Why won't my GFCI reset after a power outage?

Some GFCIs need line power present to latch, and after an outage a different breaker or an upstream GFCI may also have tripped. Check the panel and every other GFCI first. If power is confirmed at the outlet's circuit and it still will not latch, the device may have failed its self-test.

Can a GFCI go bad without tripping?

Yes. Older GFCIs can fail in a way that leaves the outlet live but unprotected, which is why the monthly TEST matters. Many newer devices self-test and either lock out or show an end-of-life indicator instead.

What does a red light on a GFCI mean?

It depends on the maker. On many models a solid or flashing red light indicates the device has tripped or reached end of life, while others use it for a different status. The legend printed on the face or the instruction sheet tells you which.

Is this the same problem as a GFCI that keeps tripping?

Related but not the same. A GFCI that keeps tripping resets fine and then trips again, which points to leakage on the circuit. One that will not reset at all is more often missing power, miswired or worn out.

Is it safe to bypass a GFCI that won't reset?

No. Moving the feed wires to other terminals or swapping in an ordinary receptacle removes the shock protection that electrical codes require in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoors and other damp locations. Leave the outlet and anything downstream unused until a new GFCI is fitted, and have a licensed electrician do that if you are not qualified.
