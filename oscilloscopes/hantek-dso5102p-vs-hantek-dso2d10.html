Hantek DSO5102P vs DSO2D10

Hantek DSO5102P vs DSO2D10 pits Hantek's older 100 MHz bench scope against its newer one. On the headline row they match: two channels, 100 MHz, 1 GSa/s, a 3.5 ns rise time and a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen. The gap is underneath. The DSO2D10 records up to 8 Mpts against 40K points (or 24K, depending on which Hantek document you read), decodes five serial buses and has a 25 MHz generator. The DSO5102P has none of those, but it keeps a few trigger tricks of its own.

Same front page, different engine

A buyer skimming listings sees the same four numbers on both: 100 MHz, two channels, 1 GSa/s, 7 inches. The charts below show where the spec sheets split, starting with memory, which needs a log scale.

Why record length decides slow captures

A scope can only sample as fast as its memory lets it fill the screen. The rule is simple: effective sample rate = record length ÷ capture time, capped at the converter's maximum. On short timebases both scopes run at full speed. Stretch the window and the DSO5102P has to slow down long before the DSO2D10 does.

Short record isn't always a problem

If you set the timebase so the event fills the screen, the DSO5102P's 40K points are plenty: a 1 kHz square wave over a 1 ms window still gets 40 MSa/s, 40,000 samples per cycle. The short record hurts when you want a long window and fine detail at once, or when you capture once and zoom in afterwards. That is what serial decoding and power-sequencing work demand, and it's why the DSO2D10 suits them.

What each one can and can't measure

Hantek DSO5102P: a classic 100 MHz bench for repeating signals

The DSO5102P is at its best on repetitive signals that fit a screen: oscillators, audio, PWM, ripple, video lines (it triggers on NTSC, PAL and SECAM). Its 1024-point FFT, 6-digit trigger counter and 32 automatic measurements cover routine checks. Alternative trigger is a real advantage for a technician comparing two clocks that aren't related, since each channel gets its own stable trigger. It can't decode a bus, drive a circuit, or keep fine detail over long windows.

Hantek DSO2D10: the newer platform with memory to spare

The DSO2D10 adds what embedded work needs: deep memory for single captures you zoom into afterwards, five serial decoders with matching triggers, runt and pattern triggers for faults, and a generator for stimulus. Its lower 13 pF input capacitance also loads a circuit slightly less. It doesn't list SECAM video, an EXT/5 input or an AC line trigger, and its external trigger input works only on the generator models, at 0 to 3.3 V.

Mains-powered, earth-referenced inputs

Both scopes run from mains with grounded probe returns, and both inputs are limited to 300 Vrms through a 10X probe. Never clip a probe ground to a live mains conductor or the primary side of a switch-mode supply: the ground lead shorts it to earth. Use a differential probe or an isolation approach suited to the job, and call an electrician for anything on household wiring.

The trust question: Hantek's two record lengths

The DSO5102P's product page says 40K points; the DSO5000P datasheet our Pick was built from says 24K. We can't tell you which is right from Hantek's own documents, so this page uses 40K and flags 24K. It doesn't change the verdict: both figures are about 200 to 330 times shorter than the DSO2D10's 8 Mpts. Hantek also doesn't say how the DSO5102P's 1 GS/s splits with both channels on, beyond a 500 MS/s to 1 GS/s range, while the DSO2D10 states 500 MSa/s per channel plainly. Hantek publishes no waveform update rate for either model.

Choose the Hantek DSO5102P if…

  • ✓You look at repeating signals on short timebases, where a 40K record is enough
  • ✓You want Alternative trigger to lock two unrelated signals on one screen
  • ✓You trigger from the mains line or an external source through EXT or EXT/5
  • ✓You don't need serial decoding or a built-in signal source

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓You capture long, slow events such as power-up sequences and still want fine detail
  • ✓You debug UART, I2C, SPI, CAN or LIN and want the bytes decoded on screen
  • ✓You want a 25 MHz signal source in the same box
  • ✓You want runt (Under Amp), window and pattern triggers for intermittent faults

Neither if…

You need three or four signals at once, or 12-bit detail on small signals. Both are 8-bit, two-channel scopes; a four-channel Rigol DS1054Z or a 12-bit Rigol DHO802 fits those jobs better.

The bottom line

Buy the Hantek DSO2D10 unless you have a specific reason not to: same 100 MHz and screen, but 8 Mpts of memory, five serial decoders, 14 trigger types and a 25 MHz generator. The Hantek DSO5102P still makes sense for repeating-signal bench work where its Alternative trigger, EXT/5 input or mains-line trigger matter more than memory or decoding.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Hantek DSO2D10 the replacement for the DSO5102P?+

Hantek doesn't describe it that way, and it still lists the DSO5000P series on its site. On paper, though, the DSO2D10 covers the same 100 MHz, two-channel, 1 GSa/s ground with far deeper memory, serial decoding and a generator. The DSO5102P keeps a few trigger options the DSO2D10 doesn't list.

How much memory does the Hantek DSO5102P have?+

Hantek's DSO5000P product page states a record length of 40K points, while the DSO5000P datasheet our product data comes from states 24K. The sources conflict, and we haven't been able to resolve it from Hantek's documents. Either figure is short next to the 8 Mpts of the DSO2D10.

Can the DSO5102P decode I2C or UART?+

Hantek lists no serial triggering or decoding for the DSO5102P. You can still read a slow UART or I2C transfer by hand from the waveform, using cursors to time the bits, but its short record makes long transfers hard. The DSO2D10 triggers on and decodes UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN.

What is Alternative trigger on the DSO5102P?+

It lets each channel trigger on its own signal, so two waveforms that aren't synchronized can both sit still on screen. Hantek lists edge, pulse width, video and slope as the internal trigger types available per channel. It suits comparing two separate oscillators, though the timing relationship between the channels isn't meaningful in that mode.

Which is lighter to move between benches?+

The DSO2D10, at 1.9 kg per Hantek against 2.08 kg without packing for the DSO5102P. The difference is small; both are mains-powered bench scopes, not portable instruments. Hantek lists the DSO2D10 at 318 × 110 × 150 mm.
