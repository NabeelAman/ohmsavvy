Why Does a Multimeter Fuse Blow When Measuring Current?

A multimeter fuse blows when more current flows through the meter's current jack than the fuse is rated for, and by far the most common reason is connecting the meter across a voltage source (in parallel) instead of in series with the load. On the amps setting the meter is close to a dead short, so placing it across a battery or supply lets that source push as much current as it can through the fuse. The other usual causes are forgetting the leads are still in the A or mA jack when you go to measure volts, using the mA jack for a load that draws amps, and startup surges from motors and capacitors. The fuse did its job; the steps below show how to stop it happening again and how to replace it safely.

Safety: the current jack is the most dangerous input on the meter

  • Never put the meter's current jack across a mains outlet, a panel, a car battery or a lithium pack. On those sources a short can deliver hundreds or thousands of amps, and the result is an arc at the probe tips, not just a blown fuse.
  • Use a meter whose CAT rating matches the location (CAT III 600V or better for household panels and branch circuits), and never bypass, bridge or upsize the meter's fuse.
  • For mains current, use a clamp meter around a single conductor. Work inside a panel is for a licensed electrician.

Why an ammeter blows its fuse

A voltmeter and an ammeter are opposites. On the voltage function the meter presents a very high input resistance, typically around 10 MΩ, so it barely disturbs the circuit and you can safely connect it across anything within its rating. On the current function the meter must carry the whole circuit current through itself, so its designers make its resistance as low as possible: the 10 A path is usually a shunt of a few hundredths of an ohm plus the fuse and leads, and the mA path a few ohms at most. The meter works out the current from the small voltage dropped across that shunt.

That low resistance is only safe in series, where the load sets the current and the meter just rides along. Wire the same meter across the supply and nothing limits the current except the meter's own fraction of an ohm, the leads and the source's internal resistance. The two diagrams below show the difference. The circuit on the left is how current is measured; the one on the right is how meter fuses are blown.

You can see the same thing from the inside. Behind each current jack there is a fuse and a shunt leading straight to COM; behind the V jack there is a high-resistance input with no current fuse at all. Plug the red lead into a current jack and the only thing between your probe tips is the fuse and the shunt.

The arithmetic of a short

Ohm's law shows why there is no gentle version of this mistake. Take a 12 V battery and a meter on its 10 A jack. Suppose the whole path through the meter, fuse, shunt and both test leads adds up to roughly 0.1 Ω. The current the battery tries to drive is:

I = V ÷ R = 12 V ÷ 0.1 Ω = 120 A

That figure is illustrative. In reality the battery's own internal resistance and the contact resistance at the probe tips add to the total, so the current is lower, but a healthy car battery can still deliver well over 100 A for a moment, which is more than ten times the rating of a typical 10 A fuse. Compare that with the same meter in series with a 12 V, 24 W lamp: the lamp draws 24 W ÷ 12 V = 2 A, and the meter's 0.1 Ω drops only 2 A × 0.1 Ω = 0.2 V. Same meter, same battery; the only difference is where the load sits.

On the mA jack the path resistance is higher, often a few ohms, but its fuse is rated in hundreds of milliamps. A 9 V battery across a 2 Ω mA path would try to drive 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 A, more than ten times a typical 400 mA fuse. On a mains outlet the available fault current is set by the building wiring and can reach thousands of amps, which is why the type of fuse matters as much as its rating.

Jack ratings and surge currents

The chart puts the numbers from this article side by side on a log scale. The jack ratings are typical of many handheld meters, not a specific model; your meter's limits are printed beside its jacks and in the manual. The motor and short-circuit figures are illustrative.

Two rows deserve attention. Motors commonly draw several times their running current for a fraction of a second while they get up to speed, often quoted as five to eight times for induction motors, so a 2 A motor can briefly ask for more than the 10 A jack allows. Capacitor-input power supplies, LED drivers and anything with a large input capacitor do the same: at switch-on the empty capacitor looks almost like a short until it charges. A meter connected before power is applied sees that surge in full.

Time limits are the least-read line in most manuals. Many meters let you measure their full rated current only for a short period, often stated as a number of seconds on followed by minutes of rest, because the shunt heats and its resistance drifts. Exceeding that duty cycle can blow the fuse on a load that is technically within rating, so check your meter's figures rather than assuming 10 A means 10 A indefinitely.

1Confirm the fuse is blown

A blown current fuse rarely announces itself. The meter powers up, voltage and resistance still work, and the current range simply reads zero on a circuit that is obviously drawing power.

Many manuals describe a quick check: set the meter to ohms, plug the red lead into the V jack and touch its probe into the current jack, then compare the reading with the value the manual gives. OL means the fuse is open. Otherwise switch off, remove the battery, open the case as the manual describes and test the fuse itself; our guide to testing a fuse with a multimeter covers the continuity check, using a second meter if you have one.

2Work out what blew it

Replace the fuse without understanding the cause and the new one goes the same way. Think back to the moment it happened. If the probes were on two terminals of a battery or supply, it was a parallel connection. If you were measuring volts, the red lead was in a current jack. If it blew at switch-on, suspect inrush. If it blew on the mA jack, the load drew more than expected. And if the current meter went to zero on a car battery test the moment a door opened, the modules woke up. Each of those maps to one row of the causes table above.

3Fit the correct replacement

The replacement must match the original in four ways, all of which are printed on the fuse body and in the manual's specifications or maintenance section:

  • Current rating, the same amps or milliamps. Larger lets the shunt overheat.
  • Voltage rating, at least as high as the original, which on CAT-rated meters is often the meter's full voltage rating.
  • Physical size, so it seats firmly in the clips without stress or gaps.
  • Breaking capacity and type, usually a ceramic, sand-filled high-breaking-capacity (HRC) fuse of the type the maker specifies.

Buy the maker's part number, or an equivalent that matches every line of its specification. Never fit a glass automotive or electronics fuse because it has the right amp value, and never bridge the clips with wire or foil. Close the case fully before using the meter again.

Estimate the current first, from the device's wattage or a label. Turn the circuit off, break it at one point and connect the meter so the current must flow through it. Why does my multimeter fuse keep blowing?

When a multimeter fuse keeps blowing, the same mistake or the same mismatch is repeating, because a correctly specified fuse in a healthy meter does not fail on its own. One blown fuse is a lesson; the third one is a pattern, and the pattern tells you which habit to change. Compare your history with the table before buying another fuse. The current figures are worked from Ohm's law and power ratings, so treat them as illustrative orders of magnitude.

The lead-left-in-the-amps-jack habit. This is the classic repeat offender because it happens on the measurement after the current test, often minutes later, when you have forgotten the lead is there. Make returning the red lead to V part of disconnecting the meter, every time. Some meters sound a warning or light the active jacks when a lead sits in a current input while the dial is on another function; your manual says whether yours does.

Trying to measure a battery's amps. Many repeat failures come from a reasonable-sounding idea: checking how many amps a battery "has." A battery does not hold a current waiting to be read; it delivers whatever the connected resistance allows, and the current jack is almost zero ohms. That is the parallel-connection short worked through above, and it will blow every fuse you fit. To judge a battery, measure its voltage, then its voltage under a known load, as in testing a 12 V battery under load.

Inrush and loads near the limit. If the fuse only goes when a motor, compressor, power supply or LED driver switches on, the start-up surge is the cause even when the running current looks safe. Loads that run close to the jack rating for long periods are a related problem: the shunt warms, and repeated stress near the rating can weaken a fuse element so it later opens below its marked value. A clamp meter avoids both; our clamp meter buying guide explains which type reads DC as well as AC.

The wrong replacement. After the first blown fuse, many people fit whatever matches the amp number and the physical size. An underrated fuse, a slow-blow where a fast-acting type is specified (or the reverse), or a glass fuse in place of a ceramic one can open early, open late, or fail to clear a high-energy fault at all. If your second fuse is not the same part as the first, the replacement may be the reason the problem keeps coming back.

Safety: replace only with the fuse the maker specifies

  • Match every line of the manual's fuse specification: current rating, voltage rating, breaking (interrupting) capacity, speed and physical size. Most CAT-rated meters specify a ceramic, high-breaking-capacity (HRC) fuse rated at the meter's full voltage.
  • Never fit a larger fuse, a glass or automotive fuse, or a wire or foil bridge to stop it blowing. A fuse that keeps blowing is warning you about a technique problem, and removing the warning leaves an arc at your hands on the next mistake.
  • If a correct new fuse blows with nothing connected, or the fuse clips are discolored or loose, stop using the current ranges and have the meter serviced or replaced.

Why breaking capacity matters

Every fuse has two ratings that sound alike. The current rating is the point at which it opens. The breaking capacity (or interrupting rating) is the largest fault current it can safely stop. When a fuse element melts under a heavy fault, an arc forms across the gap and keeps conducting. A small glass fuse can only quench a modest fault current; above that, the arc can persist, the glass can shatter and the current keeps flowing.

Meter fuses intended for electrical work are typically ceramic bodies packed with sand, which absorbs the energy and smothers the arc, and they are commonly rated to interrupt thousands of amps rather than tens. That is what lets a CAT-rated meter survive a lead in the amps jack touching a mains outlet: the fuse clears before the arc can grow into an arc flash at the probe tips. A cheap substitute with the right amp number but the wrong breaking capacity quietly removes that margin, and a CAT rating on the front of the meter assumes the correct fuse is inside.

Series, not across

On its current function a multimeter is nearly a short circuit. Put it in series, where the load limits the current, start on the 10 A jack, keep within the manual's time limits, and move the red lead back to V the moment you finish. When the fuse does blow, replace it with the exact type the maker specifies.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my multimeter fuse keep blowing?

A fuse that blows again and again points to a habit or a mismatch, not bad luck. The usual culprits are a red lead left in the A or mA jack before measuring volts, trying to read a battery's amps by touching its terminals, motor or capacitor inrush, a load above the jack rating, or a replacement fuse that is underrated or the wrong type. Fit only the maker's specified fuse.

Can I put a bigger fuse in my multimeter so it stops blowing?

No. The current rating protects the shunt and the circuit board, and the breaking capacity protects you. A larger fuse lets the shunt overheat and burn before anything opens, and a fuse with the right amps but a lower breaking capacity may fail to clear a mains fault. Fit the exact rating, voltage, size and breaking capacity in the manual, and fix the habit that blew the original.

Can I use a regular glass fuse to replace my multimeter fuse?

No, unless the manual specifies one. Most meters designed for electrical work specify a ceramic, high-breaking-capacity fuse with a particular current and voltage rating and physical size. A glass automotive or electronics fuse of the same amp value may interrupt only a small fault current, so a short on a mains circuit can arc across it instead of clearing. Buy the exact part number from the manual.

Why did my multimeter fuse blow when I measured voltage?

Almost certainly because the red lead was still plugged into the A or mA jack from an earlier current measurement. That jack connects the probes through a fuse and a shunt of a fraction of an ohm, so touching them across a battery or outlet is a short circuit. The fuse blowing is the protection working. Always move the red lead back to the V jack after measuring current.

How do I know if my multimeter's current fuse is blown?

The usual symptom is a current range that reads zero on a circuit that is clearly drawing power, while voltage and resistance still work. Many meters let you check the fuse by measuring resistance from the V jack into the current jack; the manual gives the expected value. Otherwise, remove the fuse and test it for continuity with a second meter or on the meter's own ohms range.

Is it safe to bypass a multimeter fuse with wire or foil?

No. The fuse is the only thing that stops a mistake on the current jack from turning into a dead short across the source. Without it, the shunt and leads carry the full fault current, which can melt them, burn the meter and cause an arc flash at your hands on a mains or high-energy battery circuit. Replace it with the specified fuse or do not use the current range.

How long can I measure 10 amps with a multimeter?

That depends on the meter, so check its manual. Many handheld meters allow their highest current only for a limited time, often stated as a number of seconds on followed by a longer rest period, because the shunt heats up. Some allow a little over 10 A briefly. For continuous monitoring at several amps, a clamp meter is the better tool because nothing heats in series with the load.

Why does my multimeter fuse keep blowing when measuring a car's parasitic draw?

Opening a door, waking the modules or turning the key can draw several amps for a moment, which exceeds the mA jack's rating instantly. Start on the 10 A jack, keep doors closed and the ignition off, wait for the modules to sleep, and only then move to the mA range if the reading is small enough. A clamp meter designed for low DC current avoids breaking the circuit at all.
