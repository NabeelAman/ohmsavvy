Why Is My MOSFET Getting Hot? Gate Drive, Switching Loss and Heatsinks

A MOSFET gets hot because it is turning electrical power into heat, and the usual reason is that it is not switching hard enough: the gate voltage is too low for the part, the switching edges are slow, or the current is too high for its on-resistance. Ringing, a missing flyback diode and an undersized heatsink make it worse. So when you ask why your MOSFET is getting hot, the fastest path is to measure the gate voltage, look at the edges on a scope and do the I² × Rds(on) arithmetic, which this guide walks through with worked numbers.

Heat, high voltage and scope grounds

  • A MOSFET case and heatsink can exceed 100 °C. Do not test temperature with a fingertip. Use a thermocouple or an infrared thermometer, and switch off before touching anything.
  • A bench scope's probe ground clip is tied to mains earth. Clip it only to circuit ground. On a mains-powered circuit, a high-side switch or the primary of an offline supply, use differential probes or an isolated setup, or leave it to someone trained for it.
  • Large bulk capacitors in motor drives and power supplies hold charge after power-off. Discharge and verify with a meter before reworking the board.

Where MOSFET heat comes from

A MOSFET has two loss mechanisms that matter in practice. Conduction loss happens while it is on: the drain current flows through the on-resistance, Rds(on), and the power is I² × Rds(on). Switching loss happens during each transition, when the device briefly carries current and blocks voltage at the same time. That overlap is short, but it repeats every cycle, so at tens of kilohertz it adds up. Both losses end up as heat that has to escape through the case and heatsink into the air.

A MOSFET running hot is telling you one of those losses is larger than the design assumed, or the heat path is worse than assumed. The diagnostics below separate the two. Most of the time the cause is the gate drive, because weak gate drive raises both losses at once.

1Measure the gate voltage against the datasheet

Open the MOSFET's datasheet and find the Rds(on) line. It always states the gate voltage it was measured at. Standard MOSFETs are specified at Vgs = 10 V. Logic-level parts are specified at 4.5 V and sometimes 2.5 V as well. If the datasheet only gives Rds(on) at 10 V and your microcontroller drives the gate at 3.3 V, the MOSFET is barely on, even though it switches the load.

With power on and the output held steadily on, measure DC volts from gate to source at the MOSFET pins. Compare it with the Rds(on) test voltage. Do not compare it with Vgs(th), the gate threshold. Threshold is typically 1 to 4 V and is the point where the part passes only about 250 µA. A MOSFET held near its threshold is a resistor carrying load current, and it will overheat in seconds.

While you are there, read Vds with the MOSFET on. Divide by the load current to get the real on-resistance. A 10 A load with 0.2 V across the MOSFET means 0.2 ÷ 10 = 20 mΩ. If you read 2 V, the MOSFET is at 200 mΩ and dissipating ten times more than it should. If you suspect the part is already damaged, pull it and run the checks in how to test a MOSFET with a digital multimeter.

2Work out the conduction loss: I² × Rds(on)

Conduction loss is the easy one to calculate, and it often explains the heat on its own. Take a MOSFET rated at 20 mΩ at Vgs = 10 V, carrying 10 A at 100% duty:

  • At 25 °C: P = 10² × 0.020 = 100 × 0.020 = 2.0 W.
  • Rds(on) rises with temperature, typically to about 1.5 to 1.8 times its 25 °C value near 125 °C (the datasheet has a curve). Hot: P = 100 × 0.020 × 1.6 = 3.2 W.
  • At 50% PWM duty, conduction loss scales with duty: 3.2 W × 0.5 = 1.6 W.
  • The same part underdriven at a Vgs where its effective Rds(on) is 200 mΩ: P = 100 × 0.2 = 20 W. No small heatsink survives that.

The current is squared, so it matters more than anything else. Doubling the load from 10 A to 20 A quadruples the conduction loss, from 2 W to 8 W at 25 °C. If your load draws more than you think (a stalled motor, an LED string with no current limit), the MOSFET heats up even with perfect gate drive. Measure the current, do not assume it.

3Look at the switching edges on a scope

Put channel 1 on gate to source and channel 2 on drain to source, both probes grounded at the source pin for a low-side switch. Trigger on the rising edge of the gate. A healthy edge shows Vgs rising, pausing on a flat shelf (the Miller plateau, where the drain voltage falls), then climbing to the full drive level. Measure the Vds fall and rise time between the 10% and 90% points; our guide to measuring rise time with cursors shows the method.

Switching loss can be estimated with a standard approximation: P ≈ ½ × V × I × (tr + tf) × f. For a 24 V, 10 A load switched at 20 kHz:

  • With a gate driver giving 100 ns edges: ½ × 24 × 10 × 200 ns × 20,000 = 120 × 0.004 = 0.48 W.
  • Driven from a weak logic pin with 1 µs edges: ½ × 24 × 10 × 2 µs × 20,000 = 120 × 0.04 = 4.8 W.
  • The same weak drive at 40 kHz: 9.6 W. Frequency multiplies the problem.

4Check for ringing and turn-off spikes

Zoom in on the turn-off edge of Vds. With an inductive load (a motor, solenoid or relay coil) and no flyback diode, the drain voltage shoots far above the supply until the MOSFET's body breaks down in avalanche, which dumps the coil's stored energy into the MOSFET as heat on every cycle. A properly placed flyback diode clamps that spike to about one diode drop above the supply rail.

Ringing on the gate is a separate issue. Stray inductance in the gate loop resonates with the gate capacitance, and if the ring dips back through the threshold the MOSFET partly turns on again, adding loss. If it overshoots the gate's maximum rating, often ±20 V, it damages the oxide. The method for measuring and damping it, including the ground-spring probe trick, is in our guide to measuring ringing on a MOSFET gate signal.

5Check the heatsink with a thermal calculation

Once you know the power, the temperature rise is power × thermal resistance. The datasheet gives junction-to-case (RθJC) and junction-to-ambient with no heatsink (RθJA). For a typical TO-220 part, RθJA is often around 60 °C/W. At 2 W with no heatsink, that is 2 × 60 = 120 °C above ambient, so 145 °C at a 25 °C room: right at the limit.

A dry, uneven mount or an insulating pad that is too thick can easily add a few °C/W. Also check the PCB: a surface-mount MOSFET depends on copper area for cooling, and a small pad turns a 1 W loss into a hot spot.

Fix the loss before you add aluminum

A bigger heatsink treats the symptom. If the gate voltage is below the Rds(on) test voltage, or the edges take a microsecond, fixing the drive can cut the heat by a factor of five or ten. Size the heatsink for the loss that remains after the drive is right.

Which oscilloscope to use for MOSFET switching

You need at least two channels (Vgs and Vds together), and enough bandwidth to see edges in the tens of nanoseconds. Four channels let you add the PWM input and a current probe. For the bandwidth reasoning, read oscilloscope bandwidth for switching power supplies, or compare models in our best oscilloscopes for power supply testing.

Frequently asked questions

How hot can a MOSFET get before it fails?

Most power MOSFETs are rated for a maximum junction temperature of 150 °C or 175 °C, stated in the datasheet. The case runs cooler than the junction, so a case you cannot keep a finger on is already a warning. Aim to keep the junction well below the limit, since on-resistance rises with temperature and reliability drops quickly near the maximum.

Can I drive a MOSFET directly from an Arduino pin?

Only a logic-level MOSFET whose datasheet specifies Rds(on) at 4.5 V or lower, and only at low switching frequencies. A 5 V pin has limited current, so it charges the gate slowly and the edges are slow, adding switching loss. For PWM above a few kilohertz or currents of several amps, use a gate driver chip between the pin and the MOSFET.

Why is my MOSFET hot when it is supposed to be off?

Check the gate. With no pull-down resistor, a floating gate picks up charge and the MOSFET sits partly on, dissipating heavily. A damaged MOSFET with a leaky gate or a partial drain-to-source short does the same. Measure Vgs with the circuit commanded off; it should read close to 0 V. A 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ resistor from gate to source fixes a floating gate.

Does a higher PWM frequency make a MOSFET hotter?

Yes. Switching loss is paid once per edge, so doubling the frequency roughly doubles it, while conduction loss stays about the same. Motor and LED dimming circuits often run far higher frequencies than they need. If your MOSFET runs hot at 50 kHz and the edges are slow, dropping to 20 kHz or speeding up the gate drive can cut the heat substantially.

What is the difference between Vgs(th) and the gate voltage I need?

Vgs(th) is where the MOSFET only just starts to conduct, typically at 250 µA in the datasheet test. It is not a turn-on voltage. Rds(on) is specified at a much higher Vgs, usually 10 V for standard parts and 4.5 V or 2.5 V for logic-level parts. Drive the gate to at least the voltage the Rds(on) figure is quoted at.

Can a multimeter tell me why my MOSFET is hot?

Partly. A meter can confirm the DC gate voltage in a static on state and measure the drain-source voltage to estimate Rds(on). It cannot show switching edges, ringing or a gate that is partly on during PWM, because it averages. For PWM circuits a scope is the tool that finds the cause. A meter is still the right tool to check whether the MOSFET is already damaged.
