How to Test an Electric Bike Hub Motor With a Multimeter

To test an electric bike hub motor with a multimeter, unplug it from the controller, measure the resistance between each pair of the three thick phase wires, and check that all three readings are low and nearly equal. Then confirm every phase wire reads OL to the axle and shell, check that the three hall signals switch between about 0 V and 5 V as the wheel turns, and finish with a spin test by hand. A motor that passes all four is almost never the reason the bike won't go.

Safety before you start

  • Switch off and unplug the battery before you separate the motor connectors. The phase wires carry pack current, up to about 58.8 V on a 52 V system, and a shorted phase plug can arc.
  • A hub motor is also a generator. Spinning the wheel fast puts real voltage on the phase pins, so turn it slowly for these tests and keep the pins from touching anything.
  • Motors get hot after a long climb. Let the hub cool before handling it, and treat a smell of burnt varnish as a sign to stop riding until the motor is checked.
  • Integrated mid-drive systems (Bosch, Shimano, Yamaha and similar) are sealed units diagnosed by the brand's dealer software. These tests are for hub-motor kits and bikes with standard three-phase connectors.

How a brushless hub motor is wired

Almost every ebike hub motor is a three-phase brushless DC motor. Inside the shell are three sets of copper windings, usually joined at one end in a star (also called wye), with the other ends coming out of the axle as three thick wires, typically yellow, green and blue. Next to them is a thinner five-wire hall cable: 5 V, ground and three signals from hall sensors that tell the controller where the magnets are. Some motors add a speed sensor or temperature wire.

Because the windings meet at a star point, any two phase wires read two windings in series. That is why the three phase-to-phase readings should match: each one includes two of the three windings, and a fault in one winding shows up in the two readings that include it. The winding insulation separates all of that copper from the steel stator, axle and shell, which is the phase-to-shell test.

1Disconnect the motor and inspect the cable

With the battery off, unplug the phase connector and hall connector between motor and controller. Look closely where the cable leaves the axle. This is where most hub motor faults start: if the axle has ever spun in the dropouts (common without a torque arm on a powerful front motor), the cable gets twisted and cut. Check the phase connector pins for brown, melted or loose contacts too. A heat-damaged bullet connector raises one phase's resistance enough to make the motor stutter under load, and it will show up in the next step.

2Measure phase-to-phase resistance and compare

Set the meter to its lowest ohms range. Touch the probes together and note the lead resistance, often 0.1 to 0.3 Ω; if your meter has relative or REL mode, press it now so the meter subtracts the leads. Then measure yellow to green, green to blue and blue to yellow, holding the probes firmly on the bare pins. Typical hub motors read a few tenths of an ohm per pair. Direct-drive motors tend to sit lower than geared motors, but the important number is the spread between the three.

Do the arithmetic on the spread. In the connector example, 0.46 ÷ 0.21 ≈ 2.2, so those pairs read more than twice the healthy one. Because both readings that include green are high and the one without green is normal, the problem is on the green side. A spread within about 10% is normal variation and probe pressure. Readings this low push any handheld meter to its limit, so repeat each one twice; our list of meters for measuring low resistance explains why.

3Test each phase for a short to the shell

Switch to the highest ohms range. Put one probe on a clean, bare part of the axle (scrape paint off the flat if needed) and touch the other to each phase pin in turn, then each hall wire. Every reading should be OL. Anything that settles at a definite value, even several megohms, means the insulation is leaking somewhere, and a reading in ohms or a few kilohms is a hard short. Repeat against the bare metal of the shell if the axle reading is clean.

The usual culprit is the cable inside the axle channel, where a spun axle or a crash crushes the insulation. A multimeter tests at only a few volts, so it can pass a winding with weak insulation that breaks down at pack voltage. The limits of a meter for this job are covered in checking a motor winding for a short.

4Check the hall sensors toggle between 0 V and 5 V

Hall sensors need power, so reconnect the hall plug (phases still unplugged) and switch the battery on, or power the hall cable from a 5 V bench supply. Many ebike hall sensors are open-collector types, so on a bench supply each signal needs a pull-up resistor (a few kilohms) to 5 V or it will read nothing. First confirm about 5 V between the red and black hall wires. Then put the black probe on hall ground and the red on each signal in turn while you turn the wheel slowly by hand.

Each signal should snap between close to 0 V and close to 5 V, with nothing in between except during the switch. A direct-drive motor has many magnet poles, so the signals change every few centimeters of tire. On most geared hubs, turn the wheel backward, since turning it forward just freewheels past the motor. A signal stuck at 0 V or 5 V through a full turn is a dead sensor or a broken wire. Sensor-level detail is in how to test a Hall effect sensor.

Three sensors, six states

On the common 120° hall layout, the three signals step through six combinations as the rotor turns, and never sit all high or all low together. If you ever see all three at 0 V or all three at 5 V, one of them has failed or the motor uses the less common 60° layout, which your controller must be set to match.

5Do the spin-resistance test

This one needs no meter, and it confirms what the meter suggests. With the phase wires unplugged and kept apart, spin the wheel by hand. A direct-drive hub should turn freely with a faint, even cogging from the magnets; a geared hub should freewheel. A wheel that feels hard to turn with nothing connected points to a winding shorted inside the motor, or to a mechanical problem such as a failed bearing.

Now short two phase wires together (a clip lead works) and spin again. The motor becomes a generator feeding a short, so a direct-drive wheel becomes noticeably harder to turn with a smooth, even drag. Repeat for all three pairs: they should all feel the same. A pair that turns freely has an open in one of those two phases, matching the OL from step 2. If a hub feels draggy only when connected to the controller with the power off, suspect a shorted MOSFET in the controller instead; the diode-mode test in our ebike controller guide confirms it.

For a measured version, set the meter to AC volts and spin the wheel at a steady, slow pace while reading each phase pair. The three readings should be close to each other. A low or missing reading on one pair matches an open or shorted winding.

Which multimeter to use for hub motors

Phase resistance is the demanding test. You want a stable low ohms range and a relative mode to null the leads, and good sharp probes that bite through oxide on the pins. For hall checks any meter with DC volts works, and a frequency mode lets you see the hall switching rate rise as the wheel turns faster. If the battery is in doubt as well, start with testing the ebike battery, since a sagging pack causes many of the same stop-start symptoms.

Frequently asked questions

What resistance should an ebike hub motor have?

Phase-to-phase resistance on most hub motors is well under 1 ohm, often a few tenths of an ohm, with direct-drive motors usually lower than geared ones. The exact figure depends on the winding, so the number matters less than the balance. All three pairs should agree within roughly 10 percent once you have subtracted the resistance of your test leads.

How do I know if my ebike motor is burnt out?

Look for a burnt smell from the hub, discolored or melted phase wire insulation at the axle, uneven phase-to-phase readings, or one pair that reads noticeably higher than the other two. A motor that feels rough or notchy in the spin test with nothing connected, or that drags with only one pair of phases shorted, has a damaged winding.

Why does my ebike motor make a grinding noise but not turn?

On a direct-drive motor, grinding or stuttering with no rotation usually means the controller has lost a phase or a hall signal, so it is pushing current in the wrong order. On a geared hub, it can also mean stripped nylon planetary gears, which spin the motor without moving the wheel. Run the phase and hall tests first, since those are free.

Can an ebike hub motor run without hall sensors?

Some controllers are sensorless or dual-mode and can run a motor from back-EMF alone, usually with a weaker or jerkier start from a standstill. A sensor-only controller will not run the motor properly with a failed hall. If one hall sensor is dead and your controller supports sensorless mode, unplugging the hall connector is a common way to ride home.

How do I test a geared ebike hub motor?

Use the same phase resistance, phase-to-shell and hall checks as a direct-drive hub. The difference is the clutch: on most geared hubs, turning the wheel forward freewheels past the motor, so turn it backward for the hall and spin tests. Shorting two phases makes the motor itself stiffer, which you feel through the gears rather than as a strong brake.
