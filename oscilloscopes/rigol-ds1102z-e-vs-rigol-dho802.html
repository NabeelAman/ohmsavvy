Rigol DS1102Z-E vs DHO802: Bandwidth or Bits?

Rigol DS1102Z-E vs DHO802 is Rigol's own two-channel trade-off. Both have two analog channels and an external trigger input. The DS1102Z-E gives you 100 MHz with an 8-bit converter in a 2.9 kg mains-powered case. The DHO802 gives you 70 MHz with a 12-bit converter, a touchscreen and USB-C power in a 1.78 kg case. Fast signals point to the first, small ones to the second.

Spec by spec

Each scope wins two of these four charts. That is the honest summary of the pair.

The DHO802 has the faster sample rate despite the lower bandwidth, so at 70 MHz it has about 8.9 samples per cycle with both channels on against 5 for the DS1102Z-E at 100 MHz. Bandwidth decides how fast a signal can be before the scope shrinks it; sample rate decides how finely it is drawn. Both matter, in different jobs. What each one can and can't measure

Rigol DS1102Z-E: two fast channels on the older platform

The DS1102Z-E is the DS1000Z platform with two channels and 100 MHz. That bandwidth is comfortable to roughly 20 to 33 MHz, which takes in a 25 MHz crystal and SPI clocks most two-channel buyers will meet. Rigol lists 1 mV/div as its most sensitive scale and an 8-bit converter, so at 10 mV/div each step is about 0.31 mV. Ripple of a millivolt or two is visible but drawn in a handful of levels. It runs from mains and weighs 2.9 kg.

Rigol DHO802: slower ceiling, finer floor

At 70 MHz the DHO802 is comfortable to about 14 to 23 MHz. A 16 MHz microcontroller clock fits with its harmonics softened; a 25 MHz one is past the comfortable range. Below that ceiling it is the better instrument: 12-bit steps of about 0.02 mV at 10 mV/div, a faster two-channel sample rate, 500,000-frame recording and CAN decoding. The touchscreen and HDMI output help when you share a screen with a class or a client.

The 70 to 100 MHz gap is narrower than it looks

The bandwidth difference only decides cases where your fastest signal sits between about 14 and 33 MHz. Below that, both scopes show the shape; the DHO802 shows it with more detail. Above that, both struggle and a 200 MHz scope is the real answer.

Grounding on a mains-powered scope

The DS1102Z-E's probe grounds connect to mains earth through its power cord. Connecting a probe ground to a live or neutral conductor causes a fault through the scope. For anything mains referenced, use a differential probe on either model.

The question with the DS1102Z-E: how much memory you get

Rigol's DS1000Z-E data sheet lists 24 Mpts with one channel and 12 Mpts with two as standard. Rigol's series web page lists 12 Mpts as standard. We use the data sheet figures in the charts and flag the conflict here; if deep memory is the reason you are buying, check what the unit shows in its acquisition menu on arrival. Rigol publishes one set of DHO802 memory figures, so there is nothing to reconcile on that side.

The DS1102Z-E against its four-channel sibling is on Rigol DS1102Z-E vs DS1054Z, and against a Siglent two-channel scope on Rigol DS1102Z-E vs Siglent SDS1102CML+. The DHO802 against its four-channel sibling is on Rigol DHO804 vs DHO802, and against the classic four-channel Rigol on Rigol DHO802 vs DS1054Z.

Choose the Rigol DS1102Z-E if…

  • ✓Your clocks reach 20 to 30 MHz
  • ✓You want more memory with both channels on (12 against 10 Mpts)
  • ✓You prefer buttons and a mains-powered bench scope
  • ✓You follow course material built around the DS1000Z family

Choose the Rigol DHO802 if…

  • ✓You measure ripple, noise, audio or sensor signals
  • ✓You want CAN decoding included
  • ✓You want a lighter, shallower scope with USB-C power and HDMI
  • ✓Your fastest clocks stay under about 15 MHz

Neither if…

You need three or four signals at once. Both are two-channel scopes with an external trigger input; the Rigol DHO804 or DS1054Z gives you four channels.

The bottom line

Buy the Rigol DS1102Z-E if your fastest signals sit between about 15 and 30 MHz, such as a 25 MHz reference clock or fast SPI: its 100 MHz bandwidth shows those edges more faithfully. Buy the Rigol DHO802 if your work is ripple, audio, sensors or buses under about 15 MHz: its 12-bit converter, faster two-channel sample rate, CAN decoding, touchscreen and 1.78 kg USB-C case make it the better everyday two-channel scope.

Frequently asked questions

Is 70 MHz enough for Arduino work?+

Yes. A 16 MHz AVR clock sits inside the DHO802's comfortable range by the three-to-five-times rule, and UART, I2C, PWM and servo signals are far slower. The edges of the clock will look a little rounded, which is true on most hobby scopes.

Do both scopes have an external trigger input?+

Yes. Rigol lists the DS1102Z-E with two analog channels and an external trigger input channel, and the DHO802 as "2 + EXT". On both, EXT can start a capture from a third signal, such as a sync pulse, but isn't drawn on screen or decoded.

Which one decodes more protocols?+

The DHO802. Rigol lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C, SPI and CAN decoding as standard on the DHO800 series. The DS1000Z-E data sheet lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C and SPI. If you work on CAN, the DHO802 is the only one of the two that decodes it.

Is the DS1102Z-E's 8-bit converter a problem?+

For timing, logic and most general work, no. It shows up on small signals: at 10 mV/div each step is about 0.31 mV, so millivolt ripple is drawn in a few levels. Rigol lists a High Resolution acquisition mode for the DS1000Z-E that reaches up to 12 bits; it is an acquisition mode, not the native converter resolution.

Which scope is easier to carry between home and a lab?+

The DHO802. Rigol lists it at 1.78 kg and 77 mm deep, powered by a USB-C 15 V supply, against 2.9 kg and 122 mm deep with a mains cord for the DS1102Z-E. Heights and widths are close. Both still need a bench and a power source; neither is a handheld.
