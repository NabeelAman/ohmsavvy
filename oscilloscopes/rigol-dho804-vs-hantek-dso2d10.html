Rigol DHO804 vs Hantek DSO2D10

Rigol DHO804 vs Hantek DSO2D10 is a choice between two different ideas of a compact scope. The Rigol puts four channels, 12-bit vertical resolution and a 1024 × 600 touchscreen into a USB-C powered body, with no signal generator. The Hantek has two channels and more bandwidth (100 MHz against 70 MHz), plus a built-in one-channel waveform generator up to 25 MHz.

Spec by spec

On a spec sheet these two trade blows. The Hantek wins on bandwidth, the Rigol on single-channel sample rate and channel count. The figure that matters most depends on whether you spend your time on one fast signal or on several related ones, so the charts show both.

Four channels and 12 bits, or two channels and a generator

Start with bandwidth, since it sets the ceiling for both. Rated bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine wave is shown about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its true amplitude, so a scope is accurate only well below that figure. The usual 3–5x rule says the scope should have three to five times the bandwidth of the fastest signal you care about. That puts the DHO804 at roughly 14–23 MHz and the DSO2D10 at roughly 20–33 MHz. Both cover UART, I2C, most SPI, PWM, audio and the switching frequency of typical hobby supplies. Square waves need more margin than sines because their edges carry higher harmonics, which is where the Hantek's extra 30 MHz shows up: a common estimate of the fastest rise time a scope can display is 0.35 divided by bandwidth, about 5 ns for 70 MHz and 3.5 ns for 100 MHz. Our guide to oscilloscope bandwidth explains the rule in more detail.

Sample rate tells a different story. With one channel on, the DHO804's 1.25 GSa/s is about 18 samples per cycle of a 70 MHz sine, and the Hantek's 1 GSa/s is 10 per cycle at 100 MHz; both are far above the Nyquist minimum of two. Turn on all four of the Rigol's channels and each gets 312.5 MSa/s, about four and a half samples per cycle at 70 MHz. That is enough to see the signal but leaves less detail on fast edges, so for edge work keep fewer channels on. Memory follows the same pattern: 25 Mpts with one channel, 5 Mpts with four. If GSa/s is new to you, read what GSa/s means.

The real reason to choose the DHO804 is its four channels. Timing faults rarely involve just two signals. An SPI transaction has a clock, a chip select and two data lines; a board with several regulators has rails that must come up in order. The DHO804 shows all four on one trigger and decodes UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and parallel buses as standard. Its 12-bit converter gives 4,096 vertical levels against the 256 of an 8-bit design, so millivolt ripple on a supply rail or a small sensor output stays smooth when you zoom in instead of breaking into steps. Our data doesn't state the Hantek's resolution, so check Hantek's datasheet if that matters to you.

The real reason to choose the DSO2D10 is its generator. Testing a filter, amplifier or sensor input needs a known signal going in. The Hantek's single output (up to 25 MHz) drives the circuit while channel 1 watches the input and channel 2 the output. The DHO804 can do the same measurement, with two channels spare, but only if you buy a separate signal source. Note that the generator doesn't add channels: once it is driving a circuit, both Hantek inputs are usually spoken for.

The trade in one line

The DHO804 sees more signals in more vertical detail; the DSO2D10 sees faster edges on two signals and can make its own test signal. Neither does both.

Mains and high-voltage circuits

On a mains-powered bench scope the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth. The DHO804 runs from USB-C, but its ground can still reach earth through the supply or anything plugged into it, so never assume it is floating. Use a differential probe for mains-referenced nodes.

Choose the Rigol DHO804 if…

  • ✓You need to watch three or four signals at once, such as a clock, chip select and two data lines
  • ✓You want 12-bit resolution to look at small ripple, noise or sensor signals
  • ✓You want standard UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and parallel decoding, HDMI out and USB-C power

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓You need a test-signal source as well as a scope, with room for only one instrument
  • ✓Two channels cover what you measure at the same time
  • ✓You want 100 MHz of bandwidth headroom over the DHO804's 70 MHz

The bottom line

For most hobbyists who already own a signal source, or rarely need one, the Rigol DHO804 is the stronger scope: four channels, 12-bit resolution, standard serial decoding and a sharper touchscreen. The Hantek DSO2D10 is the better buy if you have room for one instrument and your work is mostly feeding a test signal into a circuit and watching what comes out, where its generator and 100 MHz bandwidth earn their place.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Rigol DHO804 better than the Hantek DSO2D10?+

For multi-signal work, yes. The DHO804 has four channels, 12-bit resolution, standard serial decoding and a 1024 × 600 touchscreen, and rates 4.8 in our roundup against 4.3. The Hantek is better if you need a built-in generator or the extra headroom of 100 MHz on two channels.

Does the Rigol DHO804 have a built-in signal generator?+

No. Rigol lists no built-in generator and no logic channels for the DHO800 series. If you need to inject test signals, you would add a separate generator, or choose the Hantek DSO2D10, which has a one-channel generator up to 25 MHz built in.

Is 70 MHz enough, or do I need the Hantek's 100 MHz?+

By the 3-5x rule, 70 MHz covers signals up to roughly 14-23 MHz, which includes most microcontroller, serial bus, PWM and audio work. The Hantek's 100 MHz stretches that to roughly 20-33 MHz and shows faster edges more faithfully, which matters mostly for fast digital rise times.

What happens to the DHO804's sample rate with all four channels on?+

It shares its sample-rate budget, falling from 1.25 GSa/s with one channel to 312.5 MSa/s per channel with four, and memory drops from 25 Mpts to 5 Mpts. That is still above the Nyquist minimum at 70 MHz, but turn off unused channels when edge detail matters.

What does 12-bit resolution do that an 8-bit scope can't?+

A 12-bit converter divides the screen into 4,096 vertical levels against 256 for 8-bit, sixteen times finer. Small signals such as supply ripple or sensor outputs stay smooth when zoomed instead of showing steps. Our data doesn't state the Hantek's resolution, so check its datasheet before comparing directly.
