Why Won't My Soldering Iron Melt Solder?

A soldering iron that won't melt solder is almost never actually broken. In the large majority of cases the cause is a dirty tip, a temperature set too low for the solder you're using, or an iron that simply doesn't have enough wattage for the joint you're asking it to heat. This guide works through the causes in the order worth checking them, from the quickest fix to the least likely.

Don't force it

If solder isn't melting, pressing harder or holding the tip against the joint far longer than usual doesn't help — it risks heat-damaging the component or lifting a PCB pad, since heat is being applied for much longer than a properly functioning joint requires. Work through the causes below instead of pushing past the normal dwell time.

Check these in order

The causes below are ordered from most common and quickest to rule out, to least common. Work through them in order rather than jumping straight to "the iron is broken," which is actually the least likely explanation.

1Oxidised or dirty tip — the most common cause by far

A tip covered in oxide or old, burnt flux barely transfers heat into the joint even when the iron itself is at full temperature — the oxide layer insulates the actual metal from whatever it touches. This is the first thing to rule out, every time.

Fix: wipe the tip on a damp sponge or brass wool and re-tin it with fresh solder before testing again. Full details in our tip care guide.

2Temperature set too low for your solder

Leaded solders (60/40 and 63/37) fully melt in the 183–191°C range, so an iron set below roughly 280–300°C at the tip often struggles against normal heat loss into the joint, even though that's well above solder's melting point on paper. Lead-free solders typically need higher iron settings still — see cause 4 below. If your iron has an adjustable temperature dial or digital setpoint, confirm it's actually set where you think it is, not accidentally turned down.

3Not enough wattage for the specific joint

Wattage determines how fast an iron can replace the heat a joint pulls away from the tip. A joint with a lot of connected copper — a ground plane, a thick gauge wire, a large connector pin — draws heat away faster than a small isolated pad does. A 15–30W iron that handles small signal joints fine can genuinely fail to bring a ground-plane joint up to melting temperature at all, not because anything is faulty, but because it's underpowered for that specific joint's thermal mass.

Fix: for general electronics work, 60–75W gives enough headroom for most ground-plane and larger joints — see our buying guide for wattage guidance by use case.

A quick test to tell wattage apart from tip condition

With a freshly cleaned, re-tinned tip, try a small isolated pad first. If that melts solder normally within a second or two but a ground-plane or thick-wire joint next to it still won't, the tip itself is ruled out and the problem is almost certainly thermal mass outrunning the iron's wattage, not a dirty or cold tip.

4Lead-free solder needs more heat than you expect

Common lead-free alloys (tin-silver-copper blends) melt at roughly 217–220°C — noticeably higher than leaded solder's 183–191°C range, and the practical iron tip temperature needed to work with it comfortably is correspondingly higher too. If an iron setting that always worked with leaded solder suddenly seems too cold, confirm you haven't switched to a lead-free spool without raising the temperature to match. See our solder alloy comparison for the exact melting ranges.

5A genuine power or heating-element fault

Only once the four causes above are ruled out does an actual hardware fault become the likely explanation — a failed heating element, a damaged power cord or plug, or (on a two-part station) a bad connection between the base unit and the handpiece. The telltale sign is an iron that stays close to room temperature, or heats only slightly, no matter how long it's left plugged in with a known-clean tip installed.

A 2-in-1 station adds one more variable

On a combined soldering iron and hot air rework station such as the YIHUA 862BD+, the iron and hot air gun have independent heating elements and temperature controls. If only the iron side won't melt solder while the hot air side works normally, that narrows the fault specifically to the iron handpiece or its own setpoint, not the unit as a whole.

Frequently asked questions

My iron melts solder fine on the tip but not on the actual joint — what's wrong?

This points specifically at a thermal mass problem, not the iron itself: the joint (often a ground plane, thick wire, or large connector pin) is pulling heat away faster than the iron tip can supply it. The tip alone has far less thermal mass than a real joint. Increase dwell time slightly, use a larger tip for better contact area, or move to a higher-wattage iron for that class of joint.

Is it normal for solder to take a few seconds to start melting?

A brief delay while the tip and joint come up to temperature is normal, generally under two seconds on a properly heated, clean tip touching a typical through-hole joint. If you're routinely waiting five or more seconds, something on this page's list is the cause rather than normal behavior.

Can old solder itself be the problem, not the iron?

Yes, in two ways. Very old solder wire can have degraded, less-active flux, making it harder to get a clean melt and wet. Separately, mixing lead-free and leaded solder on the same joint raises the effective melting point of the mix, which can make an iron set for leaded solder seem suddenly underpowered.

How do I know if the heating element itself has failed, not just the tip?

If the iron no longer reaches a noticeably hot temperature at all — the tip stays close to room temperature, or barely warms after several minutes powered on, even with a known-good, freshly cleaned tip installed — that points to the heating element or its control circuit rather than tip condition. At that point the fix is repair or replacement, not cleaning.

Can more than one of these causes be happening at the same time?

Yes, and it's one reason the problem can seem to persist after a single fix — a dirty tip combined with a slightly-too-low temperature setting is a common pairing, since both make heat transfer worse together. If cleaning the tip improves things but doesn't fully resolve it, move on to the next cause on the list rather than assuming the cleaning didn't work.
