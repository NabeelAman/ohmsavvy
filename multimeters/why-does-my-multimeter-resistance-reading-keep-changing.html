Why Does My Multimeter Resistance Reading Keep Changing?

A multimeter resistance reading keeps changing when something other than the part itself is in the measurement: your fingers on the probe tips, a capacitor that is still charging, a part that is warming up, a probe that isn't making solid contact, or a value sitting on the edge between two ranges. Hold the part by its body (not the leads), press the tips firmly onto clean metal, give the reading a few seconds and, if it still moves, the steps below find which of those is to blame. This page is about a single ohms reading that won't settle; if you get different but steady values on a circuit board, or your voltage readings jump around, see the linked guides instead.

Safety: resistance is a power-off test

  • Only measure resistance on parts and circuits that are switched off, unplugged or disconnected from their battery. Check with the voltage function first, using a meter rated for the circuit.
  • Discharge capacitors before probing. A charged power-supply or motor-run capacitor can damage the meter's ohms input and deliver a painful or dangerous shock.
  • Heating elements, thermal fuses and motor windings can be hot right after use. Let them cool, both for your fingers and so the reading isn't drifting as they do. For fixed household wiring, call a licensed electrician.

Settling vs wandering

The way the number moves tells you most of what you need. A reading that climbs steadily in one direction and then stops is settling: something in the measurement is changing in a predictable way, usually a capacitor charging or a part warming or cooling to a steady temperature. A reading that jumps around at random, or changes every time you shift your grip, is wandering: the measurement path itself is unstable, because of poor contact, your body, electrical noise or a live circuit.

The ohms function works by driving a small, known test current through the part and measuring the voltage that appears. On high ranges that current is tiny, so anything that adds or steals even a little current, like a fingertip, a damp board or noise picked up by long leads, shifts the result. On low ranges the current is larger but the resistance being measured is so small that a few hundredths of an ohm of contact change matter. Both ends of the scale are where readings misbehave.

Why fingers matter on high ohms

Pinching a resistor lead and the probe tip together with each hand feels natural, and it's the most common reason a high-value reading won't sit still. Your body is a conductor. Hand-to-hand resistance at the low voltages an ohmmeter uses varies enormously with skin moisture, grip area and pressure, from roughly a hundred kilohms to a few megohms. That path sits directly in parallel with the part, so the meter reports the combination, and it changes every time your grip does.

The error depends on the ratio between the part and your body. Using the same illustrative 500 kΩ body path, the parallel formula (R × 500 ÷ (R + 500), in kΩ) gives the readings below. A 1 kΩ resistor reads 998 Ω, an error you'd never notice; a 1 MΩ resistor reads about 333 kΩ, a third of its real value.

Find out which way it moves

A reading that creeps and stops is the part or a capacitor settling: wait it out. A reading that jumps when you move is the connection: fix the contact before you trust any number.

1Take your hands off the metal

Lay the part on a dry, non-conductive surface and touch the probes to its leads without touching any metal yourself, or clip one lead on with an alligator clip so only one hand is involved. One hand on one probe doesn't create a parallel path; two hands, one on each side, does. If the reading jumps up and holds steady the moment you let go, you've found the cause.

The same idea applies to anything that bridges the two probe points: a damp workbench, flux residue on a board, or a component lying on an anti-static mat, which is deliberately slightly conductive. Above a few hundred kilohms, measure on clean, dry plastic or wood.

2Fix the probe contact

A tip resting lightly on a tarnished lead, a solder joint coated in flux or a plated terminal makes a contact whose resistance changes as you breathe. Press firmly, twist the tip a few degrees to cut through the surface film, and try a fresh spot. Clean old component leads with fine abrasive or a fiberglass pen. Worn or bent probe tips and leads with a cracked conductor inside cause the same symptoms; if wiggling the lead near its plug moves the reading, the leads are the problem. See how to tell if multimeter test leads are bad.

Contact matters most on low values, which is where step 5 picks up. On high values it matters less than leakage and noise, covered in the range section further down.

3Wait out a charging capacitor

If the part is a capacitor, or a circuit with one across it, the meter's test current has to charge it before the reading means anything. The value starts low and climbs; for a capacitor on its own it keeps climbing to OL, and in a circuit it levels off at the resistance of whatever else is connected. Bigger capacitors and higher ohms ranges (which use smaller test currents) take longer, from a blink for a small ceramic to many seconds for a large electrolytic. Reverse the probes and the climb starts again, which is a quick way to confirm capacitance is the cause.

Discharge the capacitor safely, measure, and wait until the reading stops moving. If you want to know whether the capacitor itself is good, measure it properly with how to test a capacitor with a multimeter. Climbing readings caused by capacitors on a populated board, along with the parallel-path errors that come with in-circuit testing, are covered in why resistance values differ on a circuit board.

4Rule out temperature

Some parts are supposed to change. An NTC thermistor's resistance falls as it warms, typically by roughly 3 to 5% per °C near room temperature. As an illustration, a 10 kΩ (at 25 °C) thermistor that drops about 4% per degree reads around 8.2 kΩ after warming 5 °C between your fingertips (10 × 0.96⁵). Photoresistors change with light, so a hand shadow moves the reading too. Metal elements and copper windings move the other way: copper's resistance rises by about 0.4% per °C, so a motor winding measured straight after running reads high and falls as it cools.

Let the part sit at room temperature, handle it with clip leads, and compare the stable value with the maker's table at that temperature. Sensor-specific procedures are in how to test a thermistor and how to test a photoresistor.

5Zero the leads on low values

Below about 10 Ω, the leads and contacts are a real fraction of the answer. A typical pair of test leads plus contacts adds something like 0.1 to 0.3 Ω, and that figure moves with probe pressure. Measuring a 0.5 Ω shunt, a lead total of 0.2 Ω makes the meter show 0.7 Ω, a 40% error, and a change in pressure of 0.05 Ω shifts the reading by another 10%. That's why low readings seem to drift as you hold the probes.

Short the tips firmly, note the value, and subtract it, or press REL (relative or zero) if the meter has it so the display starts from zero. Then use the same firm pressure on the part. The UNI-T UT61E+'s manual specifically tells you to use REL for low-ohm readings. A meter's resolution sets the floor too: the Fluke 117's lowest range is 600.0 Ω with 0.1 Ω resolution, so a value near 0.2 Ω can only ever flick between a couple of digits.

6Stop the range hunting

Autoranging meters switch range when a reading crosses a limit, and a value sitting right on that limit can bounce between two ranges. On the Fluke 117, for example, the two lowest ohms ranges are 600.0 Ω and 6.000 kΩ, so a part near 600 Ω can alternate between something like 599.8 Ω and 0.601 kΩ, and each switch takes a moment to settle. Press RANGE to lock the higher range and the hunting stops.

High ranges are noisy for a different reason. Measuring tens of megohms means a very small test current, so mains hum picked up by long, looped leads, and leakage across a dusty or humid board, are a big share of the signal. Fluke's own spec reflects this: the 117 is rated ±(0.9% + 1) up to 6 MΩ but ±(5% + 2) on its 40.00 MΩ range. Keep leads short and close together, stay clear of power cords, and expect the least stability at the top of the scale.

7Confirm the part is unpowered

Any voltage across the part, from a battery still connected, a charged capacitor or an induced voltage on a long cable, adds to or fights against the meter's test voltage. The result can be a value that drifts with the stray voltage, a negative number or OL. Switch to DC and then AC volts and measure across the same points. If anything more than a few millivolts shows, disconnect and discharge before measuring ohms again.

If the voltage reading itself is what's jumping around, that is a separate problem with its own causes; see what causes unstable multimeter readings.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my resistance reading go up slowly and then stop?

A slow, steady climb that levels off is the signature of capacitance. The meter's test current charges any capacitor across the probes, and the reading only becomes the true resistance once it is full. Large electrolytics on high ohms ranges can take several seconds or longer. Wait for the number to stop moving, and discharge the capacitor before and after measuring.

Does touching the probes affect resistance readings?

Yes, mainly on high values. If you hold both metal tips, or both leads of the part, your body becomes a resistor in parallel with it. Against a 100 Ω part that barely matters; against 1 MΩ it can cut the reading by more than half, and it drifts as your grip changes. Hold the part by its body, use clip leads, or rest it on the bench.

Why does my multimeter's last digit keep changing on ohms?

A flicker of one or two counts in the last digit is normal. It sits inside the meter's specified accuracy, which includes a count allowance, plus tiny changes in probe contact. On a Fluke 117 measuring 4.700 kΩ, the spec allows about ±0.043 kΩ. Movement much larger than the accuracy limit points to contact, temperature or a part that genuinely varies.

Why does resistance change when I measure a thermistor or sensor?

Because that is its job. An NTC thermistor's resistance falls as it warms, commonly by a few percent per degree Celsius near room temperature, so warmth from your fingers or breath moves the reading within seconds. Hold it by its leads with clips, let it reach room temperature, and compare the stable value with the maker's resistance-versus-temperature table.

How do I get a stable reading on a very low resistance?

Short the probes, note the lead resistance and subtract it, or use a relative (REL or zero) mode if the meter has one. Press the tips hard onto clean metal and keep the pressure the same, because contact resistance of a tenth of an ohm or so swamps a milliohm-level part. For serious low-ohm work, a meter with 0.01 Ω resolution or a four-wire method helps.
