Rigol DS1054Z vs OWON VDS1022I

Rigol DS1054Z vs OWON VDS1022I is a choice between a bench scope and a laptop scope. The Rigol is a self-contained 4-channel, 50MHz, 1 GSa/s instrument with a 7-inch display. The OWON is a 0.26 kg USB box with two channels, 25MHz and 100 MSa/s, no screen of its own and an isolated USB link: it shows waveforms through OWON's PC software.

Spec by spec

The Rigol leads on every headline number, by a factor of two on bandwidth and channels and ten on sample rate. One caution on sources: the Amazon listing for the VDS1022I quotes figures for OWON's whole VDS series, which don't apply to this model. The numbers below are the ones OWON publishes for the VDS1022I itself: 25MHz, 100 MSa/s and 5K points.

Four samples per cycle at 25MHz is above the Nyquist minimum, but a waveform near the OWON's limit is drawn from very few points, so its shape relies heavily on the software's interpolation. OWON doesn't state in the specs we verified whether the 100 MSa/s rate is shared when both channels run. For more on these figures, see what oscilloscope bandwidth is and what GSa/s means.

Rigol DS1054Z vs OWON VDS1022I for electronics testing

For electronics testing the real question isn't only bandwidth, it's how you work. A bench scope sits beside the circuit with its own screen and controls: you turn a knob and the trace moves, with nothing else to boot or connect. A PC scope like the VDS1022I turns your laptop into the display and control panel. That makes it small enough to carry to a project, and a laptop screen is usually larger than a 7-inch panel, but every session starts with plugging in, opening OWON's software and working through a mouse rather than dedicated knobs.

What the OWON VDS1022I handles

With 25MHz the VDS1022I is comfortable to roughly 5–8MHz. That covers audio circuits, sensor outputs, PWM for motors and LEDs, UART at common baud rates and 100kHz or 400kHz I2C. Its two channels let you compare an amplifier's input with its output, or watch clock and data on an I2C bus. OWON lists edge, pulse, video, slope and alternate triggers plus math and FFT, so a pulse trigger is available for catching a short glitch. The limits are the 5K-point record, which at 100 MSa/s fills in 50 µs, and the fact that it can't be used without a computer.

What the Rigol DS1054Z adds

At 50MHz the DS1054Z stays comfortable to roughly 10–17MHz, so faster SPI clocks, sharper logic edges and many switching regulators look closer to their real shape. Four channels mean you can watch chip select, clock, data and an output in one capture. And because it is self-contained, it is always ready on the bench. Its review notes that menus take some learning and that 50MHz is modest for RF; at that point both scopes are out of their depth.

Rise time sets the fastest edge you can trust

A common approximation puts a scope's own fastest displayable rise time at about 0.35 divided by its bandwidth: roughly 7 ns for the 50MHz Rigol and 14 ns for the 25MHz OWON, which matches the 14 ns OWON lists for the VDS1022I. If the edge you are measuring isn't several times slower than that figure, the screen shows the scope's rise time, not your circuit's. The twofold gap holds.

Record length: 50 µs against 24 ms

Memory decides how much time one capture holds at full speed. The VDS1022I's 5K points at 100 MSa/s cover 5,000 ÷ 100,000,000 = 50 µs. That is enough for a few cycles of a 100kHz signal, but a single 115,200-baud UART byte lasts about 87 µs (10 bits × 8.7 µs), so catching a whole byte means slowing the timebase and the sample rate with it. Rigol's datasheet gives the DS1054Z 24 Mpts, which at 1 GSa/s spans 24 ms; its product page lists 12M as standard, which halves that. Either figure holds an entire burst of serial traffic at a rate that still resolves every edge.

Where the probe ground goes

The two designs also differ in a way the spec sheet barely mentions: what the probe's ground clip is connected to. On the Rigol it is the scope chassis, and through the power cord, mains earth. On the OWON it is the scope's own front end, which talks to your laptop across an isolation barrier in the USB link.

In practice the isolation helps most on battery and bench-supply circuits whose ground isn't at earth potential, such as a USB-powered gadget plugged into a different computer: with a non-isolated scope, the two grounds meet through the probe and can cause a ground loop or worse. With the Rigol, that same situation is handled by making sure the circuit's ground is the point you clip to, or by floating the circuit from a battery.

Grounds, isolation and mains

On a mains-powered bench scope like the Rigol, the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth through the power cord, so clipping it to a live or non-earthed point creates a short. A non-isolated USB scope ties its probe ground to your computer's ground instead. The VDS1022I's isolated USB link is meant to break that path, but its isolation voltage rating isn't in our verified data, so don't treat it as permission to probe mains. Use a differential probe for mains work on either.

The bottom line

For a home bench and general electronics testing, the Rigol DS1054Z is the stronger tool: twice the bandwidth, four channels instead of two, a much higher sample rate and its own screen, so it is ready the moment you switch it on. The OWON VDS1022I suits people who already work at a laptop and want a light, USB-powered two-channel scope with an isolated USB link for signals below about 5MHz.

Frequently asked questions

Does the OWON VDS1022I have a screen?+

No. It is a PC oscilloscope: the box holds the two input channels and connects to your computer over isolated USB 2.0, and you view and control the waveforms in OWON's PC software. Without a computer it can't be used, which is the main practical difference from the Rigol DS1054Z.

Why do the VDS1022I's specs on Amazon look different?+

The Amazon listing's bullets quote figures for OWON's whole VDS series, and they don't all apply to this model. OWON's own product page and datasheet list 25MHz, 100 MSa/s and 5K points for the VDS1022I, which are the figures we use on this page.

Is the OWON VDS1022I enough for Arduino projects?+

For many of them, yes. At 25MHz it is comfortable to roughly 5–8MHz, which covers PWM, UART and standard I2C, and two channels let you watch clock and data together. Faster SPI clocks and sharp edges are better served by the 50MHz Rigol DS1054Z, which also adds two more channels.

What does isolated USB mean on the VDS1022I?+

It means the USB connection to your computer is electrically isolated, so the scope's probe ground isn't tied directly to the computer's ground. That reduces ground-loop and shorting risk on bench circuits. OWON's isolation voltage rating isn't in our verified data, so it isn't a substitute for a differential probe on mains.

Which is better for a beginner, the Rigol DS1054Z or the OWON VDS1022I?+

A beginner with a desk to dedicate to electronics will get more from the Rigol DS1054Z: a real front panel to learn on, four channels and 50MHz. The VDS1022I is the better fit if you don't have bench space, always work at a laptop, or want a light second scope to carry between places.

How much does the OWON VDS1022I record in one capture?+

OWON lists a 5K-point record length. At the full 100 MSa/s that is 50 µs of signal, a few cycles of a 100kHz waveform. Slower timebases stretch the window by lowering the sample rate. The Rigol DS1054Z's 24 Mpts per its datasheet, or 12M standard per its product page, holds milliseconds at full speed.
