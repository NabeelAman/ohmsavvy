HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test a Drill Battery With a Multimeter

To test a drill battery with a multimeter, set the meter to DC volts, put the red probe on the pack's positive terminal and the black probe on the negative, and compare the reading with the pack's rating: a charged 18V or 20V MAX lithium pack should read about 20–21 V, and anything under about 15 V means it is flat or locked out. Because voltage alone can look fine on a worn pack, finish with a short load test, either in the drill or with a lamp, and watch how far the voltage falls. This guide covers terminal identification on slide-on packs, the voltages for 12V, 18V/20V and older NiCd packs, why a pack that reads 0 V or 10 V won't charge, and what is and isn't safe to try.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 15, 2026

Any meter with a 20 V-plus DC range, such as the budget UNI-T UT139C, reads a tool pack. See our UNI-T UT139C review.

Tool packs can deliver huge currents

  • A cordless tool pack is built to supply tens of amps. Never bridge the + and − terminals with a probe tip, screwdriver or loose wire, and don't leave the meter on its amps setting.
  • Don't open the pack or connect packs together to force a charge. Over-discharged lithium cells can have internal damage, and opening a pack voids its warranty.
  • A pack that is cracked, swollen, wet, hot, smoking or smells sweet goes outdoors on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can burn. If it catches fire, leave it and call 911.
  • Recycle dead lithium tool packs at a battery drop-off, such as a home-improvement store collection bin, with tape over the terminals.

18V vs 20V MAX: same pack, different label

Almost every mainstream lithium drill battery in the 18–20 V class is five lithium-ion cells in series, usually 18650 or 21700 cylinders. Each cell is about 3.6 V nominal and 4.2 V when fully charged, so the pack is 5 × 3.6 = 18 V nominal and up to 5 × 4.2 = 21 V full. Some brands label by nominal voltage (18V); others label by maximum voltage (20V MAX). DeWalt's own fine print says 20 V is the maximum initial voltage without a load and 18 V is nominal. The 12V MAX packs follow the same logic with three cells: 10.8 V nominal, about 12.6 V full.

Higher-capacity packs (say 5 Ah instead of 2 Ah) add cells in parallel rather than raising the voltage, so a 2 Ah and a 5 Ah pack of the same brand read the same voltage at the same charge level. The pack also contains a protection board or a link to the tool's and charger's electronics that watches temperature and cuts off at low voltage. That protection explains most "my battery reads fine but won't work" puzzles.

1Find the positive and negative terminals

Look at the terminal block where the pack slides into the tool. Most brands mold a + and − beside the two power terminals, which are usually the largest blades and sit at the outer edges. The smaller terminals between them carry the thermistor and, on newer packs, a communication line to the tool and charger. On older stick-type packs that push into the handle, the + and − contacts are at the tip and side of the stem.

If nothing is marked, set the meter to DC volts and try pairs of terminals. The pair that gives the largest steady reading near the pack rating is + and −; a minus sign on the display means the probes are reversed. Use the probe tips gently; the slots are narrow and the blades can short against each other if you lever the tip sideways.

2Read the pack voltage at rest

Let the pack sit off the charger and out of the tool for at least 30 minutes, then read it. Compare the number with the table below. Many packs have a fuel-gauge button with three or four LEDs; the meter reading should roughly agree with it. A big mismatch, such as four LEDs and 16 V, points to a fault in the pack's electronics.

3Do a short load test

A drill battery can show 20 V with no load and still be worn out, so put a known load on it for a few seconds while you read the voltage. A simple load is two identical 12 V car headlight bulbs wired in series, so each sees about half the pack voltage. A 55 W, 12 V bulb has a hot filament resistance of roughly R = V² ÷ P = 144 ÷ 55 ≈ 2.6 Ω, so two in series on a 20 V pack draw about 20 ÷ 5.2 ≈ 4 A (a slightly cooler filament draws a little more). Connect the bulbs with clip leads to the + and − terminals, read the voltage across the terminals after two or three seconds, then disconnect.

Work out the sag. Say a pack reads 20.2 V at rest and 19.7 V on the 4 A load (illustrative). The drop is 0.5 V, so the pack resistance is about 0.5 ÷ 4 = 0.125 Ω, or about 25 mΩ per cell across five cells including terminals and wiring, which is in the normal range for power-tool cells. A pack that falls to 17.4 V on the same load has about 2.8 ÷ 4 = 0.7 Ω; under a drill's 20 A it would lose about 14 V and hit the cutoff almost instantly.

4If the pack reads low or 0 V, think lockout

Lithium tool packs stop the tool at roughly 3.0 V per cell, about 15 V for a five-cell pack, to protect the cells. A pack that then sits for months keeps slowly self-discharging, and the electronics draw a trickle too. Once it falls well below about 2.5 V per cell (12.5 V for 5S, 7.5 V for 3S), many chargers treat the pack as damaged and flash a fault instead of charging. Some chargers first try a slow pre-charge; give it 30 minutes before deciding.

A pack that reads 0 V at the terminals may have its output switched off by its protection electronics, or a blown internal fuse, rather than truly empty cells. Either way, the multimeter tells you the charger's refusal is consistent with what the pack is showing. That is a warranty question for the maker, not a reason to open the case. For how protection boards behave, see the battery protection circuit guide.

5Check the charger before you buy a pack

If every pack fails in the same charger, the charger is the suspect. Many tool chargers only switch on their output when they detect a pack through the thermistor or data terminal, so an empty charger often reads 0 V or a few volts at its contacts; that is normal. The better test is to charge a known-good pack on a friend's charger, or read a pack before and after 20 minutes on your charger. A rise of a volt or more means it is charging.

Voltage says full; sag says healthy

A charged drill battery reads about 20 V whether it is new or worn out. What separates them is how much the voltage falls when the pack has to deliver real current.

Which multimeter to use for drill batteries

Any digital meter with a DC voltage range above 21 V works, and a 6,000-count display shows 0.01 V steps on a 20 V pack, enough to see sag clearly. Probe tips need to be slim enough to reach terminals in narrow slots. Don't try to measure a tool's running current through the meter's 10 A jack: a drill can pull more than that at stall.

Frequently asked questions

What voltage should a fully charged 20V drill battery read?

About 20 V, sometimes up to around 20.5 V just off the charger. A 20V MAX pack is five lithium-ion cells in series. DeWalt, for example, states that 20 V is the maximum initial voltage without a load and 18 V is the nominal voltage. So a 20V MAX and an 18V pack from another brand use the same five-cell arrangement and read about the same.

At what voltage is a drill battery dead?

A five-cell lithium pack is effectively empty around 15 to 16 V resting, about 3.0 to 3.2 V per cell, and the tool usually cuts out before that. Below about 12.5 V, 2.5 V per cell, the cells are over-discharged, and many chargers refuse to charge them. A 12V MAX three-cell pack is empty around 9 V and over-discharged below about 7.5 V.

Why does my drill battery read full but die quickly?

High internal resistance or one weak cell. With no load, all five cells may still read near full, but under the 10 to 30 amps a drill pulls, a worn pack sags until the protection circuit cuts out. A load test shows it: if the voltage drops several volts the moment you run the drill, the pack is worn even though it read 20 V on the bench.

Can I jump-start a drill battery that won't charge?

Videos show people connecting a good pack to a dead one to raise its voltage until the charger accepts it. Tool makers don't recommend this. It bypasses the protection that stops charging cells that have been over-discharged, which can have internal damage. If the charger rejects a pack, it is safer to replace it under warranty or recycle it at a battery drop-off.

How do I test an old NiCd drill battery?

The same way, with different numbers. An 18 V NiCd pack has fifteen 1.2 V cells, so it reads about 19 to 20 V just after charging and around 18 V when settled, and it is flat near 15 V. NiCd packs have no internal protection board, so a near-zero reading usually means a shorted or reversed cell. Most cordless tools sold today use lithium packs instead.
