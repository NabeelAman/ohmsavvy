Rigol DS1054Z vs FNIRSI DSO152

Rigol DS1054Z vs FNIRSI DSO152 is really a question about what kind of first oscilloscope you need. The Rigol is a 4-channel, 50MHz, 1 GSa/s bench scope with a 7-inch display. The FNIRSI is a pocket-sized, single-channel scope with 200kHz of bandwidth and 2.5 MSa/s. They are not rivals at the same job: one is a full bench instrument, the other a low-cost way to see slow waveforms.

Spec by spec

Most comparisons between scopes argue over a factor of two. Here the gaps are measured in hundreds, so the bandwidth and sample rate charts use a log scale; on a linear scale the FNIRSI's bar would be too thin to see. Every number comes from each maker's published specs.

Sample rate and bandwidth line up sensibly on both. At its 200kHz limit the FNIRSI still takes 12.5 samples per cycle (2.5 MSa/s ÷ 200kHz), and the Rigol takes 20 per cycle at 50MHz on one channel, or 5 with all four channels on. Both stay well clear of the Nyquist minimum of two samples per cycle. For what these figures mean, see what oscilloscope bandwidth is and what GSa/s means.

What each one can and can't measure

A scope's bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine wave is shown about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its true amplitude. To see a signal's shape accurately, the usual rule of thumb is a scope with 3–5 times the signal's frequency, and more for square waves, because their sharp edges are built from harmonics well above the fundamental. Applied to these two scopes, that rule draws very different boundaries.

FNIRSI DSO152: audio and slower

With 200kHz of bandwidth, the DSO152 is comfortable up to roughly 40–65kHz. That covers the whole audio band (up to about 20kHz), so you can check whether an amplifier output is clipping, see the ripple on a linear supply at mains-related frequencies, watch a slow sensor output change, or confirm a low-frequency PWM pin is actually toggling. Its limits arrive quickly after that. A 100kHz I2C clock is already past its comfortable range and its edges will look rounded; SPI clocks in the megahertz and most switching regulators are out of reach. And with one channel, it can't show cause and effect side by side, which is how much real debugging gets done.

Rigol DS1054Z: general electronics

At 50MHz the DS1054Z is comfortable on signals up to roughly 10–17MHz. That takes in microcontroller GPIO, UART, I2C and SPI at typical hobby speeds, PWM motor drives, most switching supplies and everything the FNIRSI can show. Four channels let you watch a clock, data line, chip select and output together. Where it runs short is RF and very fast digital edges, the cases its own review flags as modest; for those, a 100MHz scope such as the Siglent SDS1104X-E gives more headroom.

Why a slow scope rounds a fast edge

A common approximation puts a scope's own fastest displayable rise time at about 0.35 divided by its bandwidth. For the FNIRSI that is roughly 1.75 µs; for the Rigol, roughly 7 ns. A 50kHz square wave has a 20 µs period, so on the FNIRSI each edge takes up a visible slice of the cycle, while on the Rigol it looks essentially vertical. The exact figure depends on the front end's response, but the 250-fold gap doesn't.

Mains and high voltage

On a mains-powered bench scope like the Rigol, the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth through the power cord. Clipping it to a live or non-earthed point creates a short through the scope. Use a differential probe or proper isolation for mains work, and don't use the FNIRSI on mains unless its input rating covers it; that rating isn't in our verified data.

Buying both, or one after the other

These two don't replace each other, so owning both is sensible. A common path is to start with the FNIRSI, learn what a waveform looks like and how timebase and volts-per-division work, and step up to a bench scope once you run into its single channel or 200kHz ceiling. If you already know you'll be debugging microcontroller projects, skipping straight to the Rigol avoids buying a tool you'll outgrow within weeks.   • ✓You want one scope that covers microcontroller, digital and audio work for years
  • ✓You need to compare signals: input against output, or clock against data
  • ✓Your signals run above a few tens of kilohertz, such as I2C, SPI or switching supplies
  • ✓You'd rather buy the hands-on tested pick in this pairing

Choose the FNIRSI DSO152 if…

  • ✓Your signals are audio-range or slower and one channel is enough
  • ✓You want a scope small enough to carry in a pocket or tool bag

The bottom line

For most people buying a first oscilloscope to build and debug circuits, the Rigol DS1054Z is the better buy: 250 times the bandwidth, four channels instead of one, and enough headroom for microcontroller and power-supply work for years. The FNIRSI DSO152 earns its place as a pocket-sized, very cheap way to learn what waveforms look like and check audio-range signals, or as a grab-and-go companion to a bench scope, not as a substitute for one.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FNIRSI DSO152 good enough as a first oscilloscope?+

For learning waveform basics, yes. Its 200kHz bandwidth and 2.5 MSa/s handle audio-range and other slow signals, and the low price makes it a cheap way to find out whether you will use a scope. If you plan to debug microcontroller or digital circuits, its single channel and bandwidth limit will stop you quickly, and the Rigol DS1054Z is the better first buy.

Can the FNIRSI DSO152 check Arduino signals?+

Only the slow ones. It can confirm a low-frequency PWM output or a slow sensor signal is present. A 100kHz I2C clock is past its comfortable range of roughly 40–65kHz, SPI clocks are far beyond it, and with one channel it cannot show clock and data together. For Arduino debugging, the 50MHz, 4-channel Rigol DS1054Z is the practical tool.

How much faster is the Rigol DS1054Z than the FNIRSI DSO152?+

Its bandwidth is 250 times higher: 50MHz against 200kHz. Its sample rate is 400 times higher with one channel on, 1 GSa/s against 2.5 MSa/s, and still 100 times higher at 250 MSa/s per channel with three or four channels running. It also has four input channels to the FNIRSI's one.

Should I buy the FNIRSI DSO152 first and upgrade to the Rigol later?+

It is a reasonable path if you are unsure you will use a scope; the FNIRSI costs little and teaches the basics. If you already know you will work on microcontroller, digital or power-supply projects, buy the Rigol DS1054Z directly. You would hit the FNIRSI's single channel and 200kHz limit almost immediately, and it would end up as a backup tool.

Does the Rigol DS1054Z lose sample rate with four channels on?+

Yes. Rigol's datasheet gives 1 GSa/s with a single channel and 250 MSa/s per channel with three or four channels on. That is still 5 samples per cycle at the 50MHz bandwidth limit, comfortably above the Nyquist minimum, and 100 times the FNIRSI DSO152's 2.5 MSa/s.
