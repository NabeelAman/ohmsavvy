Rigol DS1054Z vs Siglent SDS1202X-E

Rigol DS1054Z vs Siglent SDS1202X-E is the cleanest channels-or-bandwidth decision in the beginner bench market. The Rigol gives you four inputs at 50 MHz; the Siglent gives you two inputs at 200 MHz, four times the bandwidth, plus standard serial decoding and 100,000 waveforms per second against the Rigol's 30,000. Both peak at 1 GSa/s and both have a 7-inch screen, so the choice rests on how many signals you watch at once and how fast they are.

  • →Channels: the DS1054Z has four analog inputs; the SDS1202X-E has two.

Spec by spec

Put the two side by side and they pull in opposite directions. The Siglent wins every speed figure, the Rigol wins on inputs. These charts use only the bandwidth, sample-rate and channel figures from each maker's published specs.

The last chart is the trade nobody prints on the box. Run the Siglent with both inputs on and look at a signal right at 200 MHz, and you get two and a half points per cycle, just over the theoretical minimum; scopes usually fill the gaps with sin(x)/x interpolation, which works for clean sine waves and less well for anything else. With one channel on it has five points per cycle. The Rigol, slower in absolute terms, never has fewer than five points per cycle at its own 50 MHz limit. In practice that means the Siglent's extra bandwidth is most trustworthy on a single channel or on signals well below 200 MHz. The explainers on oscilloscope bandwidth and what GSa/s means cover the theory behind these numbers.

Channels or bandwidth? Matching the scope to the signal

Bandwidth is the frequency where a sine wave is displayed about 3 dB low, around 70% of its real height. The usual guidance is to buy three to five times the highest frequency you care about, and more for square waves, because a sharp edge is built from harmonics far above the repetition rate. That last point is what catches people out: a 5 MHz clock from a fast logic chip can have edges of a few nanoseconds, and those edges, not the 5 MHz, decide what you need.

Where four inputs at 50 MHz win

Plenty of real debugging is about timing between several slow signals rather than the detail of one fast one. Checking that a 3.3 V rail comes up before a 5 V rail and that a reset line releases after both is a three-trace job at millisecond speeds, which the DS1054Z handles easily and a two-input scope can only do in pieces. The same goes for watching an SPI clock, data and chip select together, comparing a motor driver's PWM inputs against its outputs, or lining up audio left, right and a reference. Every one of those signals sits well under the Rigol's comfortable ceiling of roughly 10 to 17 MHz, and with three or four inputs active it still samples each at 250 MSa/s. Our DS1054Z review covers its limits, including the menu learning curve.

Where two inputs at 200 MHz win

Edges are where the SDS1202X-E earns its place. A scope's own rise time adds to the signal's roughly as the root of the sum of squares, so a 5 ns edge viewed on a 50 MHz scope (about 7 ns) shows up near 8.6 ns, while the 200 MHz Siglent (about 1.75 ns) shows it near 5.3 ns. Those are approximations, but the direction is reliable: on MOSFET gate drives, switch nodes, fast logic and clock ringing, the Siglent shows overshoot and ringing that the Rigol smooths away. Its standard I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN decoding and its 100,000 wfm/s update rate (against the DS1054Z's 30,000) also suit two-wire bus work and glitch hunting, and 14 Mpts of memory on one channel lets it hold a long capture at speed. What it cannot do is show a third signal: there are no more inputs, and Siglent offers its 16-channel MSO option only on the four-channel X-E models.

A quick way to decide

List the signals you expect to probe in the next year. If any of them need more than two traces at once, the extra inputs matter every session. If any have edges faster than about 10 ns, or frequencies above about 10 MHz, the extra bandwidth matters. If both are true, neither of these scopes covers everything, and a four-channel 100 MHz model such as the one in our DS1054Z vs SDS1104X-E comparison is the compromise.

Switching supplies and mains

A bench scope plugged into an earthed outlet normally ties every probe ground clip to mains earth. On low-voltage, battery or USB-powered boards that is harmless, but on a mains-input switching supply the ground clip can short a live node through the scope. Use a differential probe or an isolation method rated for the job.

Choose the Rigol DS1054Z if…

  • ✓You routinely need three or four traces together, such as several supply rails during power-up
  • ✓Your signals sit comfortably below about 10 MHz: hobby microcontroller buses, audio, PWM and slow logic
  • ✓You want the pick in this pair that carries our Hands-on Tested badge
  • ✓You value a large community and plenty of documentation while you learn the menus

Choose the Siglent SDS1202X-E if…

  • ✓You look at fast edges, ringing or overshoot, for example in switching supplies or gate drives
  • ✓Two traces at a time are enough: input against output, or clock against data
  • ✓You want I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN trigger and decode without buying options
  • ✓You hunt intermittent glitches and want a fast 100,000 wfm/s update rate

The bottom line

Buy the Rigol DS1054Z if you need to see three or four signals together and they stay below about 10 MHz; that describes most student and hobby benches, and it is the pick here with our Hands-on Tested badge. Buy the Siglent SDS1202X-E if your problems live in fast edges, ringing or two-wire buses, and you are certain two inputs will always be enough.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Siglent SDS1202X-E's 200 MHz worth giving up two channels?+

Only if your signals are fast. By the 3 to 5 times rule the SDS1202X-E is comfortable up to roughly 40 to 67 MHz and the DS1054Z up to roughly 10 to 17 MHz. If you mostly watch microcontroller buses, audio or power rails at hobby speeds, four inputs will help you more often than the extra bandwidth.

Does the Siglent SDS1202X-E decode I2C, SPI and UART?+

Yes. Siglent lists I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN trigger and decode as standard on the SDS1202X-E. With two analog inputs you can probe two lines at a time, which suits I2C's clock and data pair or a UART link better than a full SPI bus with a separate chip select.

Why does the DS1054Z's sample rate drop with more channels on?+

Rigol rates the DS1054Z at 1 GSa/s with one channel and 250 MSa/s per channel with three or four channels on, because the active inputs share the sampling capacity. At its 50 MHz limit, 250 MSa/s still gives five samples per cycle, comfortably above the Nyquist minimum of two.

Which is better for switching power supply work?+

The SDS1202X-E. Switching edges are far faster than the switching frequency suggests, and its 200 MHz bandwidth implies a scope rise time near 1.75 ns against about 7 ns for the DS1054Z, so ringing and overshoot are shown more faithfully. Two inputs cover a gate drive and a switch node; use a differential probe on mains-referenced circuits.

Is either of these scopes hands-on tested by OhmSavvy?+

The Rigol DS1054Z carries our Hands-on Tested badge. Our assessment of the Siglent SDS1202X-E is desk research based on Siglent's published specifications, and it has no full OhmSavvy review yet, so read its verdict as a spec-sheet comparison rather than a bench result.
