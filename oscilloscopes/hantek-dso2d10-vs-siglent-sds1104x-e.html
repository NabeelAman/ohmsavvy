Hantek DSO2D10 vs Siglent SDS1104X-E

The Hantek DSO2D10 vs Siglent SDS1104X-E choice is unusual because the headline numbers match: both are 100MHz bench scopes with a 1 GSa/s maximum sample rate, a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen and standard serial decoding. What differs is what surrounds that front end. The Hantek has two channels and a built-in one-channel waveform generator (to 25 MHz); the Siglent has four channels, keeps 500 MSa/s per channel with all of them on, and has deeper memory per channel.

Spec by spec

Most scope comparisons are decided by bandwidth. This one isn't. Both scopes are rated at 100MHz and both peak at 1 GSa/s, so on a single channel they are evenly matched. The charts below show where they part ways: how many signals you can watch together, how long each channel can record, and whether you also get a signal source. The record-length chart is simple arithmetic: 4 million points at 500 MSa/s fill in 8 ms, and 7 million fill in 14 ms. That matters when you want one capture to hold a whole event, such as a burst of serial packets or a power-up sequence, and still zoom into individual edges afterwards. Both are generous for a hobby bench; the Siglent simply holds a longer window per channel while running twice as many.

Two ways to use 100MHz: a test loop or a full bus

The diagram shows the job each scope is shaped for. The Hantek can feed its own circuit: the generator output drives a filter or amplifier, channel 1 watches the input and channel 2 the output. The Siglent can't make a signal, but it can put a probe on every line of a four-wire bus, such as SPI's chip select, clock, MOSI and MISO, and capture them all on one trigger.

Notice what the top setup costs: once the Hantek's generator is driving a circuit, both of its inputs are spoken for by input and output, so there is no spare channel for a third node such as a supply rail. The bottom setup shows the opposite limit: the Siglent can see the whole bus, but if the circuit needs an external stimulus you must bring one, either a separate generator or Siglent's optional USB AWG.

What each one can and can't measure

Start with what they share. Rated bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine wave is shown about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its true amplitude, so a scope reads a signal accurately only well below that figure. The common 3–5x rule says the scope's bandwidth should be three to five times the highest frequency you care about. At 100MHz, both are comfortable on signals up to roughly 20–33MHz: microcontroller clocks, PWM, UART, I2C and most SPI, audio, and the switching frequency of typical hobby power supplies. Square waves need more margin than sines because their edges carry harmonics well above the fundamental, so treat the lower end of that range as the safe limit for clean edges. Our guide to oscilloscope bandwidth explains the rule in more depth.

Sample rate is closer than it first looks. Both reach 1 GSa/s on one channel, which is ten samples per cycle of a 100MHz sine, and both drop to 500 MSa/s per channel with every input running: Hantek states this for its two channels, Siglent for its four. Five samples per cycle at 100MHz is still well above the Nyquist minimum of two. For what the GSa/s figure means in practice, see what GSa/s means.

The real dividing line is signal count. The Hantek can watch two things at once. That covers a lot: input against output of an amplifier, a PWM signal against its filtered result, a trigger pulse against a delayed response. It runs out when the problem involves three or more related signals, such as an SPI transaction or several supply rails coming up in sequence. Both scopes decode SPI, but the Hantek can only decode what its two probes reach, so a full four-wire transaction with chip select needs the Siglent.

The generator turns that around for analog work. To check a filter or amplifier you need a known input signal. With the Hantek, the built-in output (one channel, to 25 MHz) drives the circuit while the two channels compare input with output. A worked example: an RC low-pass with 1 kΩ and 10 nF has its corner at about 16 kHz (1 ÷ 2πRC). Step the generator from 1 kHz to 100 kHz and the output on channel 2 falls to about 70% of the input near 16 kHz, then keeps dropping. With the Siglent you would need a separate source to run the same test.

Waveform update rate is the one figure only one maker publishes. Siglent states up to 100,000 waveforms per second (400,000 in sequence mode), which helps a rare glitch show up on screen instead of falling into the dead time between captures. Hantek doesn't state an update rate for the DSO2D10, so we can't compare the two on intermittent-fault hunting.

Same bandwidth, different bottleneck

Because both are rated at 100MHz, neither scope will show a fast edge more faithfully than the other. Choose on the constraint you will hit first: running out of channels (Siglent) or lacking a signal source (Hantek).

Mains and high-voltage circuits

On a mains-powered bench scope the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth. Never clip it to a mains-referenced node; use a differential probe or an isolation transformer set up by someone who knows the hazards, and call a licensed electrician for building wiring.

The bottom line

With bandwidth, peak sample rate, screen and decoding tied, this comes down to channels against a built-in generator. For most buyers the Siglent SDS1104X-E is the safer pick: four channels for timing and multi-signal debugging, more memory per channel, a stated 100,000 wfm/s and our Hands-on Tested badge. The Hantek DSO2D10 is the better fit if your bench has room for only one instrument and your work is mostly feeding a test signal into a circuit and watching what comes out.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Hantek DSO2D10 as fast as the Siglent SDS1104X-E?+

On paper, yes. Both are rated at 100MHz bandwidth with a 1 GSa/s maximum sample rate, and both drop to 500 MSa/s per channel with every channel on: two channels on the Hantek, four on the Siglent. Siglent also states a 100,000 wfm/s update rate, which Hantek doesn't publish for the DSO2D10.

Are two channels enough, or do I need the Siglent's four?+

Two channels cover input-versus-output checks, a PWM signal against its result, or one signal against a trigger. You need four when three or more related signals must line up in time, such as an SPI bus with clock, chip select and two data lines, or several supply rails powering up in sequence.

Can the Hantek DSO2D10's generator replace a separate function generator?+

For many hobby tasks, yes. It is a single output up to 25 MHz, enough to drive audio filters, amplifiers and sensor inputs while the scope watches the result. It won't help if you need two independent test signals at once, and it doesn't add scope channels, so check Hantek's documentation for waveform options.

What signals can a 100MHz oscilloscope measure accurately?+

By the 3-5x rule of thumb, roughly 20-33MHz and below. That covers most microcontroller clocks, UART, I2C, much SPI, PWM, audio and typical switching supplies. Square waves need more headroom than sines because their edges contain higher harmonics, so stay toward the lower end for clean edges.

Which is better for a beginner, the Hantek DSO2D10 or the Siglent SDS1104X-E?+

Usually the Siglent. Its interface is widely regarded as intuitive, its four channels leave room to grow into timing work, and it rates higher in our roundup. The Hantek suits a beginner who mainly wants to inject test signals into circuits and has no space or budget for a separate generator.

Do both scopes decode I2C and SPI as standard?+

Yes. Hantek lists UART/RS232, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding as standard on the DSO2D10, and Siglent lists the same five protocols as standard on the SDS1104X-E. The practical difference is channel count: the Hantek can only decode lines its two probes reach, so four-wire SPI needs the Siglent.
