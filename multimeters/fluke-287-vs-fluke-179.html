Fluke 287 vs Fluke 179: Which Is Better for Electronics Work?

For electronics work the Fluke 287 is the better meter, by a wide margin on paper. It shows 50,000 counts against the 179's 6,000, resolves current down to 0.01 µA where the 179 stops at 0.01 mA, reads frequency to 999.99 kHz instead of 100 kHz, and records up to 10,000 timed readings that the 179 can only summarize as a minimum, maximum and average. The Fluke 179 still makes sense if your bench is one stop on a working day that also includes panels, HVAC units and motors: it's half the weight, runs on a 9V battery, and shares the 287's CAT IV 600V rating and lifetime warranty.

Key differences

  • →Resolution: the 287's 50,000-count ranges show one more digit than the 179's 6,000 counts, so a 3.3 V rail reads in 0.1 mV steps instead of 1 mV.
  • →DC accuracy: Fluke specifies ±(0.025% + 2) for the 287 and ±(0.09% + 2) for the 179, so the 287's error window on a 3.3 V rail is about five times narrower.
  • →Microamps: the 287 has a 500 µA range resolving 0.01 µA with a published 102 µV/µA burden. The 179 has no µA range; its finest step is 0.01 mA, which is 10 µA.
  • →Frequency and capacitance: the 287 reads to 999.99 kHz (the 179 to 100 kHz) and capacitance to 100 mF (the 179 to 10,000 µF, a tenth of that).
  • →Logging: the 287 stores up to 10,000 time-stamped intervals and graphs them on the meter. The 179's Min/Max-Average keeps the extremes and the average, not when they happened.
  • →Identical: True RMS, CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V, and a lifetime warranty. The 179 is lighter (420 g vs 871 g) and includes a thermocouple; neither has LoZ.

These are two different classes of meter that happen to share a brand, a safety rating and a warranty. The 179 is a 170 Series troubleshooting meter that also handles electronics; the 287 is a 280 Series instrument designed around electronics and logging. We haven't tested either on our bench, so every figure here is Fluke's, and the worked numbers below are arithmetic on those figures.

Resolution and DC accuracy on a logic rail

Counts decide the smallest change a meter can show; the accuracy line decides how far the display can be from the truth. Put both meters on a 3.300 V rail. The 287 measures it on its 5 V range (50,000 counts, so 0.1 mV per count) and Fluke allows 0.025% of reading plus 2 counts: 0.825 mV + 0.2 mV ≈ ±1.0 mV. The 179 uses its 6 V range (6,000 counts, 1 mV per count) with 0.09% plus 2 counts: 2.97 mV + 2 mV ≈ ±5.0 mV.

Does ±5 mV matter? For deciding whether a regulator is alive, no. It starts to matter when you compare two points on the same rail to find a voltage drop across a connector or trace, when you set a trimmer on a reference, or when you check an ADC input against its expected value. A 4 mV drop is inside the 179's window and well outside the 287's. Our explainer on multimeter accuracy for electronics repair walks through reading these spec lines for other ranges.

Microamps: the gap that decides it

Battery-powered electronics live and die by sleep current, and this is where the two meters stop being comparable. Take a sensor node that should sleep at 45 µA (an illustrative figure). On the 287's 500 µA range it reads to 0.01 µA, and Fluke's accuracy line allows 0.075% of reading plus 20 counts, about ±0.23 µA. The burden is 102 µV per µA, so the meter drops roughly 4.6 mV at 45 µA, which a 3 V coin-cell circuit tolerates.

The 179 has no microamp range. Its finest current step is 0.01 mA, so the same 45 µA shows as 0.04 or 0.05 mA. That can tell you the device is roughly asleep, but not whether firmware changes cut 45 µA to 38 µA, or whether a pull-up is leaking 3 µA. Our guide to measuring standby current covers the method, and best multimeters for measuring microamps ranks the meters that resolve it.

Capacitance, frequency and timing

Frequency reach matters for switching regulators, many of which run in the hundreds of kilohertz: the 287 can read them, the 179 runs out at 100 kHz. The 287 also lists duty cycle and pulse width, which help on PWM fan and LED lines; our sources don't list duty cycle for the 179, so treat it as not stated. On capacitance, both cover ordinary electrolytics and motor capacitors. The 287's 100 mF ceiling only pays off on supercapacitors and large bulk banks. For smaller parts, see how to test a capacitor with a multimeter.

Logging versus Min/Max-Average

Both meters can watch a signal while you're away, but they keep different records. The 179's Min/Max-Average holds three numbers: the lowest reading, the highest and the running average. If a 12 V supply sagged once overnight, you'd learn how low it went and nothing about when or how often. The 287 stores up to 10,000 sample intervals with a real-time clock, graphs them on its own screen, and moves them to a PC over its infrared link with FlukeView Forms. Fluke rates battery life at 100 hours minimum and 200 hours in logging mode on six AA cells.

Logging changes how you find intermittent faults

A fault you can watch, you can fix with any meter. A fault that shows up once every few hours needs a record of when it happened, so you can match it to temperature, load or a firmware event. That record is the 287's main advantage over the 179, more than its extra digit.

Where the 179 is the better buy

The 287 is a bench instrument that weighs 871 g and runs on six AA cells. The 179 is 420 g on a single 9V battery and ships with a thermocouple, so it travels better and answers temperature questions without extra parts. Its -40 to 400 °C range covers heat sinks, regulators and HVAC air and line temperatures. The 287's meter range is far wider, -200 to 1350 °C excluding the probe, but our sources don't say which probe, if any, comes with it.

If your electronics work is a few repairs a week between industrial or HVAC calls, the 179 covers volts, resistance, capacitance and frequency well enough, and its extra resolution would mostly sit unused on a 287. How the 179 stacks up for that field work is in Fluke 117 vs Fluke 179 and Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179.

Buying the 287 on Amazon

The 287 we link to is sold by a third-party seller, and its Amazon listing describes the safety rating as Category I/II. Fluke rates the 287 CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V, and we use Fluke's figure. Check the seller and the model printed on the meter when it arrives. On any mains-connected board, discharge capacitors before switching to resistance, capacitance or diode test, and confirm the meter on a known live source before trusting a zero reading.

The bottom line

For electronics work, buy the Fluke 287: 50,000 counts, ±(0.025% + 2) DC accuracy, a 500 µA range at 0.01 µA, frequency to 999.99 kHz and on-meter logging put it in a different class from the 179 at the bench. Buy the Fluke 179 if electronics is a side job to industrial or HVAC work and you want a lighter meter with a thermocouple, the same CAT IV 600V rating and the same lifetime warranty.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 287 better than the Fluke 179 for electronics?+

Yes, on every bench spec we have. It shows 50,000 counts against 6,000, has tighter DC accuracy, ±(0.025% + 2) against ±(0.09% + 2), a real microamp range, frequency to 999.99 kHz and logging. The 179 wins on weight, battery format and a thermocouple in the box, which matter more in the field than at a bench.

Can the Fluke 179 measure microamps?+

Not in any useful way. Fluke lists 0.01 mA as its finest current resolution, which is 10 µA, and there is no µA range. A device sleeping at tens of microamps reads as a handful of counts. The Fluke 287's 500 µA range resolves 0.01 µA, a thousand times finer, with a published burden of 102 µV per µA.

Does the Fluke 179 have data logging?+

No. It has Min/Max-Average recording, which keeps the lowest, highest and average readings while it runs, but no time stamps or stored series. The Fluke 287 records up to 10,000 sample intervals with a real-time clock, shows them as a graph on the meter and transfers them to a PC through its infrared link and FlukeView Forms.

Do the Fluke 287 and 179 have the same safety rating?+

Yes. Fluke rates both CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, so either can be used from the service entrance to the bench. Note that the 287's Amazon listing describes it as Category I/II, which conflicts with Fluke's own rating; we go by Fluke. Neither meter has a LoZ input, so neither drains ghost voltage on building wiring.

Which is better for measuring temperature, the 287 or 179?+

The 179 is ready out of the box, since Fluke supplies a thermocouple and it reads -40 to 400 °C. The 287's meter range is much wider, -200 to 1350 °C excluding the probe, but our sources don't confirm a probe in the box. For component and heat-sink temperatures either range is enough; check the probe's own rating before measuring anything hotter.

Is the Fluke 287 too heavy for field work?+

It's a bench-first meter. Fluke lists 871 g and 22.2 × 10.2 × 6.0 cm, powered by six AA cells, against 420 g and a single 9V battery for the 179. It's rated CAT IV 600V, so it can go on site for a logging job, but for all-day pouch carry the 179 is the easier meter to live with.
