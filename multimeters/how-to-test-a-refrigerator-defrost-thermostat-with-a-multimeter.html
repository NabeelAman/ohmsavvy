How to Test a Refrigerator Defrost Thermostat With a Multimeter

To test a refrigerator defrost thermostat with a multimeter, unplug the fridge, disconnect the thermostat and measure continuity across its two wires: at room temperature a good one reads open (OL), and after it has been chilled well below freezing it should read close to 0 Ω. A thermostat that stays open when properly cold stops the defrost heater from running, which is why the evaporator coil frosts over and the fresh-food section warms up. This guide covers the snap-disc bimetal thermostat used in many top-freezer and side-by-side refrigerators, how to chill it so the test is valid, and how to tell it apart from a failed heater or timer.

Any meter with a continuity beeper and an ohms range does this test; the Fluke 115 also handles the 120 V checks at the outlet. See our Fluke 115 review.

Unplug before you open the freezer panel

  • Unplug the refrigerator, don't just turn the dial to off. The defrost circuit runs at 120 V AC and a control in the off position can still leave parts live.
  • The evaporator fins are thin and sharp. Wear gloves, and never chip ice away with a knife or screwdriver: a punctured evaporator tube loses refrigerant and is a costly sealed-system repair.
  • Melt frost with time, a fan or a hair dryer kept moving and well away from plastic liners, which can warp.
  • Burnt wiring, melted connectors or a burning smell from the freezer mean an electrical fault beyond a simple part swap. Leave it unplugged and call an appliance technician.

What the defrost thermostat does

A frost-free refrigerator periodically melts the frost off its evaporator coil. A defrost timer or control board switches power from the compressor circuit to a defrost heater for a while. The defrost thermostat, a small disc clipped to the evaporator tubing, sits in series with that heater. It is closed while the coil is frozen, so the heater can run, and it snaps open once the coil warms above freezing, which ends heating even if the timer is still in defrost.

Because the heater, thermostat and timer all sit in one series loop, a failure in any of them gives the same result: frost keeps building. That is why testing the thermostat alone isn't the whole job; step 5 covers the other two. Some refrigerators add a one-shot thermal fuse in the same loop as a backup, which you can check the way how to test a thermal fuse describes.

1Unplug the refrigerator and empty the freezer

Unplug the refrigerator and move frozen food to a cooler. If the coil is heavily frosted, don't melt it yet: a frozen coil is the best cold test for a thermostat still in place. Find the model number on the label inside the fresh-food section and look up the tech sheet, often tucked behind the kick plate, which shows the defrost circuit and part locations for your model.

2Reach the evaporator

On most top-freezer and side-by-side models, the evaporator sits behind the rear panel inside the freezer, held by a few screws. On many bottom-freezer models it is behind the freezer drawer's rear panel. Remove the shelves and panel, then find the thermostat: a small round or oval disc, often with two wires, clipped to one of the evaporator tubes near the top of the coil. Note how it is clipped so the new one goes back in the same place.

3Test the thermostat warm

Disconnect the thermostat's two wires at their connectors. Set the meter to continuity or the lowest ohms range and touch a probe to each wire end. At room temperature a good bimetal defrost thermostat should read OL, with no beep. If it beeps or reads near 0 Ω when warm, the contacts are stuck closed. The general technique is the one in how to test continuity with a multimeter.

4Test the thermostat cold

A warm OL reading proves nothing on its own, because a failed-open thermostat reads the same. To check that it closes, either measure it still clipped to a frozen coil, or put the removed thermostat in a working freezer for 15 to 20 minutes and measure it immediately, before it warms in your hand. A good one reads close to 0 Ω, usually within a fraction of an ohm of your shorted test leads. If it stays OL after a thorough chill, it has failed open, and that alone explains a frosted coil.

5Check the heater and timer too

Because the parts are in series, test the defrost heater before you order anything. Measure across its two terminals: a good heater reads a definite resistance, often a few tens of ohms on 120 V models, and OL means an open element. Our defrost heater guide shows how to work out the expected value from the wattage on its label. On models with a mechanical timer, you can turn the timer's slot slowly with a screwdriver until it clicks into defrost; with the fridge plugged in, the compressor should stop and the heater should start to warm if the thermostat is cold and closed. Treat that live check with care, or leave it to a technician.

6Reinstall and watch the next defrost cycle

Clip the new thermostat onto the same tube and in the same spot, because its position decides when it senses the coil is clear. Connect the wires with the connectors supplied, refit the panel and plug the refrigerator back in. Let the coil melt fully before restarting if it was heavily iced. After a day, a working defrost system leaves the coil with only a light, even frost.

Open when warm is normal

A defrost thermostat is supposed to read open on the bench. The meaningful test is whether it closes when it is properly cold, so always chill it before you judge it.

Why a cup of ice water isn't cold enough

A common mistake is dropping the thermostat in ice water. Melting ice holds at 32 °F, and many defrost thermostats are designed to close only at a lower temperature, so a good part can stay open and be thrown away by mistake. The FDA recommends keeping a home freezer at 0 °F and the fresh-food section at or below 40 °F. A working freezer is therefore a far better cold source than ice water.

The exact close and open temperatures are set by the part maker and vary between models; they are sometimes stamped on the thermostat body. If your refrigerator uses a thermistor-type defrost sensor rather than a bimetal switch, the test changes to a resistance-versus-temperature check, the same idea as testing a heat pump defrost sensor, using the chart in the refrigerator's tech sheet.

Which multimeter to use on refrigerator parts

A continuity beeper and an ohms range are all this test needs. For appliance work in general, a meter rated CAT III 600V lets you also check the 120 V outlet safely. See our home-use multimeter guide for more.

Frequently asked questions

Should a defrost thermostat have continuity?

Only when it is cold. A bimetal defrost thermostat is normally open at room temperature, so a reading of OL on your bench is expected. It closes once chilled below its rated temperature, which is well below freezing on many parts, so test it after it has been in the freezer or still clipped to a frozen coil. Continuity when warm means it is stuck closed.

How cold does a defrost thermostat need to be to close?

It depends on the part. Many close only well below 32 °F, which is why the test is done on a frozen coil or after the part sits in a home freezer, which the FDA recommends keeping at 0 °F. The rating is often stamped on the thermostat body or listed in the parts catalog. Ice water at 32 °F may not be cold enough.

What happens when a defrost thermostat goes bad?

If it fails open, the defrost heater never turns on, so frost builds up on the evaporator coil until it blocks the airflow. The freezer may still feel cold while the fresh-food section warms up, because air can no longer pass the coil. If it fails closed, the heater may run longer than intended, which wastes energy and can trip a thermal fuse.

Can I test a defrost thermostat without removing it?

Often yes. With the fridge unplugged and the coil still frozen, you can measure across the thermostat at its wire connectors or across the harness pins while it remains clipped to the tubing. A frozen coil is the ideal cold test. Once you have taken readings, compare them with the warm reading after the frost melts to confirm it switches both ways.

Does my refrigerator use a defrost thermostat or a sensor?

Older and simpler refrigerators typically use a two-wire bimetal thermostat clipped to the evaporator tubing. Many newer models, including several Samsung and LG designs, use a thermistor sensor read by the control board instead, which is tested by resistance against a temperature chart in the tech sheet. Check the part number or tech sheet behind the kick plate.
