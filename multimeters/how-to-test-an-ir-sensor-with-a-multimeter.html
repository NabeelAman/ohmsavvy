How to Test an IR Sensor With a Multimeter

To test an IR sensor with a multimeter, first work out which kind you have: test a bare IR LED or photodiode in diode mode like any diode, and test a powered module, such as a three-pin IR receiver or an obstacle sensor, by measuring its output pin in DC volts while you trigger it. A working IR receiver idles near its supply voltage and the reading dips while you press a remote button at it. This guide covers IR LEDs, receivers, photodiodes and phototransistors, obstacle modules and PIR motion sensors, with what a good and a bad reading look like for each.

Low voltage, but easy to damage

  • Most hobby IR sensors run from 3.3 V or 5 V. Check the datasheet's supply range before powering a sensor on the bench; reversed polarity or overvoltage can destroy a receiver instantly.
  • Never power a bare IR LED straight from a supply without a current-limiting resistor. Use the meter's diode test, or a supply with a resistor sized for the LED's rated current.
  • Don't stare into high-power IR emitters at close range. Infrared light is invisible, so you get no warning from brightness.
  • IR sensors inside TVs, appliances and alarms sit on boards that may carry mains voltage. Unplug the appliance and let it discharge first, or leave live testing to a qualified technician.

Which IR sensor do you have?

"IR sensor" covers several different parts, and each needs a different test. Two-leg parts are bare components you test with the circuit off. Three- and four-pin parts and small boards are powered modules you test by watching their output. Work out which you have before you start.

1Test an IR LED in diode mode

With the circuit unpowered and the LED out of circuit or one leg lifted, set the meter to diode test. Put the red probe on the longer leg (anode) and black on the shorter (cathode). An IR LED should show a forward reading, and reversed, OL. IR emitters have a lower forward voltage than visible LEDs, so a lower reading than you'd expect from a red LED is normal. The chart shows typical datasheet figures at rated current; your meter's small test current usually reads somewhat lower.

You can't see an IR LED glow during the test, but a phone camera often can (step 6). For how diode mode works and why it reads in volts, see how to test a diode with a multimeter.

2Test a photodiode or phototransistor

A photodiode tests like a diode in diode mode, forward one way and OL the other. To prove it responds to light, switch to a high resistance range or measure its reverse leakage: cover it, note the reading, then shine a working IR remote or a bright lamp at it and watch the resistance fall. A two-leg phototransistor reads high resistance in both directions in the dark and drops sharply when IR hits it with the correct polarity. The principle is the same as a light-dependent resistor; our guide to testing a photoresistor shows the light-versus-dark method.

3Test a three-pin IR receiver

IR receivers such as the Vishay TSOP38238 contain a photodiode, amplifier and demodulator, and are designed to respond to IR bursts modulated at a carrier frequency, commonly 38 kHz. Vishay specifies a supply of 2.5 to 5.5 V for that family, and the output is active low. Check the pinout in your part's datasheet; many are OUT, GND, VS from left to right facing the dome. Power it from a 5 V or 3.3 V source, put the black probe on GND and the red on OUT, and set the meter to DC volts.

With no remote, the output should sit near the supply voltage. Press and hold any button on a TV remote a short distance away. The output pulses low too quickly for the meter to follow, so you see the average fall, typically by several tenths of a volt or more depending on the remote's code, then recover when you let go. No dip at all means the remote, the receiver or the carrier frequency is wrong: confirm the remote works with a phone camera first. To watch the actual pulse train, an oscilloscope set to trigger on a falling edge shows each burst; see how to trigger an oscilloscope on a falling edge.

4Test an IR obstacle avoidance module

Obstacle and line-follower modules pair an IR LED with a photodiode or phototransistor and a comparator, often an LM393, that switches a digital output. Power the module on VCC and GND, then measure DO to GND in DC volts. On most of these boards the output sits high with nothing in front and drops low, with an onboard LED lighting, when you hold your hand a few centimeters away; some are the other way round, so note the idle state first. If it never switches, turn the sensitivity trim pot slowly while holding your hand in place. A module that is always triggered is usually set too sensitive or facing a reflective surface. For board-level checks on microcontroller projects, our guide to measuring an Arduino's current consumption helps confirm the sensor isn't overloading the supply.

5Test a PIR motion sensor

A PIR (passive infrared) sensor detects changes in body heat rather than reflected IR light, but people search for it as an IR sensor too. Power common HC-SR501 style modules within the supply range on their documentation, wait up to a minute for the sensor to settle, then measure OUT to GND. Per that common module's documentation, the output is about 3.3 V when motion is detected and near 0 V when idle. Wave a hand across the lens and watch the reading jump, then drop after the time-delay pot setting. A mode jumper selects single or repeat triggering, so check it if the output behaves unexpectedly.

6Confirm the IR source with a phone camera

Many phone cameras pick up near-infrared light, often the front camera better than the rear one, which has a stronger IR filter. Point the remote or the module's IR LED at the camera and press a button: a working emitter shows as a flickering purple-white light on screen. It's the quickest way to tell whether the emitter or the receiver is at fault before you reach for the meter.

Bare parts in diode mode, modules on DC volts

A two-leg IR LED or photodiode is tested like any diode with the power off. A powered receiver or module is tested by watching its output voltage change when you trigger it. If the output moves, the sensor works.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my IR sensor is working?

For a powered module, measure the output pin in DC volts and trigger the sensor. An IR receiver idles near its supply and dips while a remote is pressed at it; an obstacle module's output changes state when you put your hand in front of it, usually with an onboard LED. If the output never moves while supply and ground are correct, the sensor is likely faulty.

What should an IR LED read on a multimeter?

In diode mode, an infrared LED usually reads lower than a visible LED, because IR emitters have a lower forward voltage, typically around 1.2 to 1.5 V at their rated current in datasheets and often near 1 V at a meter's small test current. Reversed, it should read OL. A reading near zero both ways means it is shorted; OL both ways means it is open.

Can I test an IR receiver without an oscilloscope?

Yes. Power it within its rated supply range, put the meter on DC volts from OUT to GND and press any button on a TV remote aimed at it. The output pulses low in bursts too fast for the meter to follow, but the average falls, so the reading dips while the button is held and returns when released. An oscilloscope shows the actual pulses if you need them.

Why does my IR receiver read 0 V at the output?

Check the pinout first: many three-pin receivers are OUT, GND, VS from the left when facing the dome, but not all, and reversed supply can destroy them. Confirm the supply voltage at the VS pin. If supply is correct and the output stays at 0 V with no remote nearby, the receiver is likely damaged. Some modules also include an onboard LED that loads the output.

How do I test a PIR motion sensor with a multimeter?

Power the module, give it a minute to settle, and measure the output pin to ground in DC volts. Common HC-SR501 style modules output about 3.3 V when they detect motion and near 0 V when idle. Wave a hand across the lens and watch the reading rise, then fall after the delay set by the trim pot. No change at all suggests a faulty sensor or wrong mode jumper.
