Fluke 1577 vs Fluke 1587 FC: Which Insulation Multimeter Should You Buy?

Buy the Fluke 1587 FC if you test anything besides motors and cables rated for 500 V or more, or if you want PI and DAR results logged to your phone. It applies 50, 100, 250, 500 or 1000 V and reads up to 2 GΩ, and it adds temperature, capacitance, frequency, Min/Max and a drive filter. The Fluke 1577 is the simpler tool: only 500 V and 1000 V, a 600 MΩ ceiling, no timed ratio tests and no wireless, with looser meter accuracy. Both share the same CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating, 30 V live-circuit lockout, auto discharge, remote probe, 400 mA current limit, IP40 case and 3-year warranty.

Everything that protects you is common to both: the live-circuit lockout, the automatic discharge, the category ratings and the remote probe. Where Fluke's documents disagree

The 1577 product page shows accuracy rows for 50, 100 and 250 V, but its own overview, the dial and the data sheet list only 500 V and 1000 V, so we follow those. A Q&A answer on the same page says 660 MΩ maximum, while the spec table and data sheet say 600 MΩ. Weight is 550 g in the shared data sheet but 624 g on the 1577 page, which may include the holster. One Amazon bullet says "550V"; Fluke says 500 V.

Picking a test voltage, and why the 1577 starts at 500 V

An insulation test is meant to stress the insulation a little beyond what it sees in service, without damaging it or anything connected to it. For low-voltage motors, cables and switchgear, 500 V DC is the usual starting point; guidance commonly cited from IEEE 43 suggests 500 V for windings rated below 1000 V. Both meters cover that. The gap is underneath it.

Control circuits, sensor wiring, telecom pairs and anything with surge suppressors or electronics still connected are often tested at 50, 100 or 250 V, because 500 V can break down protective components or stress parts that were never meant to see it. With a 1577 your only options there are to disconnect everything sensitive first or to skip the test. The 1587 FC lets you match the voltage to the circuit. On every range, Fluke specifies 1 mA of test current at 1 kΩ per volt, for example 1 mA into 500 kΩ at 500 V, and the test voltage may run up to 20% above the nominal setting but never below it.

The 600 MΩ ceiling and PI tests

A polarization index is the 10-minute reading divided by the 1-minute reading; a DAR is the 60-second reading divided by the 30-second one. Healthy, dry insulation keeps absorbing charge, so its resistance keeps climbing and the ratio comes out well above 1; a PI of 2.0 or more is a commonly cited target for many motor insulation classes. The 1587 FC runs these as timed tests and graphs them in the app. Fluke doesn't list a timed ratio function on the 1577, so you'd have to keep a test running and write down the readings yourself, if your procedure accepts a manual PI.

These readings are worked examples, not measurements. They show the practical problem with a low ceiling: the better the insulation, the sooner the 10-minute reading runs past 600 MΩ, and the PI can't be calculated. The 1587 FC keeps reading to 2 GΩ, though Fluke widens its accuracy to ±(10% + 3) above 600 MΩ. The other piece is record keeping. Insulation resistance swings with winding temperature, so trending only works if readings are corrected to a common base; the 1587 FC's app applies temperature compensation and stores results by asset, while 1577 readings go in a notebook.

The everyday meter side

Both meters share a 6000-count backlit display, True RMS AC, DC millivolts, 60 mA and 400 mA ranges, resistance to 50 MΩ and continuity. Neither has a 10 A input, so motor running current needs a clamp; the 1587 FC is sold in kits with an i400 clamp, and the bare meters on Amazon don't include one.

After that, the 1587 FC is the more complete meter. Its low-pass filter lets you read a drive's output voltage after you've tested the motor it feeds. Capacitance covers run and start capacitors, temperature (−40 to 537 °C with the included K-type probe) covers bearing and winding checks, and Min/Max and frequency round it out. If you only own one meter for motor work, those extras save carrying a second one.

Safety: an insulation tester makes high voltage on purpose

Both meters put up to 1000 V DC on the test leads. Only test circuits that are de-energized, locked out and tagged, and prove them dead with a voltmeter first; the 30 V lockout is a backstop, not a replacement for that step. Disconnect drives, surge protectors, electronics and meters from the circuit, because a 500 V or 1000 V test can destroy them. Keep hands off the probe tips and the conductor under test while the test runs, warn anyone nearby, and let the meter discharge long cables and windings before you disconnect; long runs and large motors store charge. Neither meter is rated above CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V. If you are not trained and authorized for insulation testing, leave it to a qualified electrician.

  • ✓You test control wiring, electronics-laden equipment or 120/240 V circuits that need 50, 100 or 250 V
  • ✓You run polarization index or DAR tests on motors and want them timed and graphed for you
  • ✓You keep insulation records by asset and want readings saved to the Fluke Connect app

Choose the Fluke 1577 if…

  • ✓Your insulation work is spot checks on motors, feeders and cables at 500 V or 1000 V
  • ✓A pass/fail reading below 600 MΩ is all your procedure asks for
  • ✓You don't want an app or radio in the meter and don't need temperature or capacitance

The bottom line

The Fluke 1587 FC is the better insulation multimeter for most buyers: five test voltages from 50 to 1000 V, readings to 2 GΩ, timed PI and DAR with results stored in the Fluke Connect app, tighter accuracy and a full meter with temperature and capacitance. Buy the Fluke 1577 only if your insulation work is 500 V or 1000 V spot checks under 600 MΩ and you don't want wireless; it shares the 1587 FC's safety features, ratings and warranty.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between the Fluke 1577 and 1587 FC?+

Test range and records. The 1587 FC tests at 50, 100, 250, 500 or 1000 V and reads to 2 GΩ, runs timed PI and DAR tests, and saves results through Fluke Connect. The 1577 tests only at 500 V and 1000 V up to 600 MΩ, with no timed tests or wireless, and it drops temperature, capacitance and frequency.

Can the Fluke 1577 do a polarization index test?+

Fluke doesn't list PI or DAR for the 1577; its data sheet marks timed ratio tests as 1587 FC only. You could time the readings and divide them by hand, but healthy insulation often climbs past the 1577's 600 MΩ ceiling before the 10-minute mark, and then the ratio can't be worked out.

Does the Fluke 1577 have Fluke Connect?+

No. Fluke Connect is the FC in 1587 FC. It adds app memory, TrendIt graphs during PI and DAR tests and temperature compensation for insulation readings, over a 2.4 GHz radio. The 1577 has no wireless, which some sites with radio restrictions actually prefer.

Can I test 120 V or 240 V wiring with a Fluke 1577?+

You can apply 500 V to it once everything sensitive is disconnected, which matches common practice for many low-voltage circuits. You can't choose a gentler 250 V or lower test, because the 1577 has only 500 V and 1000 V. The 1587 FC adds 50, 100 and 250 V for circuits that can't take 500 V.

Are the Fluke 1577 and 1587 FC equally safe?+

Their protection is the same: CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, a lockout that blocks the insulation test above 30 V, automatic discharge of capacitive voltage after the test, a 0.44 A 1000 V fuse on the mA input and the same remote probe. The differences are measurement features, not safety hardware.

Can either meter measure motor running current?+

Not directly at motor-load levels. Both have only 60 mA and 400 mA current ranges and no 10 A input. Use a clamp meter, or a 1587 FC kit that includes Fluke's i400 current clamp. The 1587 FC's low-pass filter does help when you read a drive's output voltage.
