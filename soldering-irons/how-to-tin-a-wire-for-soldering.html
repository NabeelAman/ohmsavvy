HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Tin a Wire for Soldering

To tin a wire for soldering, strip and twist the strands, add a little flux, hold a tinned iron tip under the bare wire for a second or two, and touch solder to the top of the wire so it melts and soaks between the strands. Stop before the solder reaches the insulation, and leave the strand texture visible under a thin, shiny coat. Tinning turns a loose bundle of strands into one solid end that solders to a pad, pin or solder cup in a second, instead of splaying or needing a long dwell. This guide covers the technique, iron settings and solder sizes, the faults that spoil tinned wire, and the places where you shouldn't tin at all.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 24, 2026

A small iron with fast heat recovery, like the Pinecil V2, is ideal for tinning hookup wire. For joining tinned wires to each other, see how to solder wires together.

Hot tip, hot wire

  • The tip runs at 300 to 400°C. A freshly tinned wire stays hot for several seconds, and copper conducts heat back toward your fingers: hold short wires with pliers or a helping-hands clip.
  • Work on unpowered wiring only. Tinning live leads risks shorts, sparks and damaged equipment.
  • Ventilate flux fumes, wear eye protection, and wash your hands after handling leaded solder.

Why tin a wire before soldering it?

Most people search for this after a frustrating joint: strands splaying out of a pad, a wire that won't take solder in a cup connector, or a joint that took so long the insulation shrank back. Tinning solves each of them. A tinned end is one solid piece instead of seven or nineteen loose strands, so it goes through a plated hole or sits in a solder cup without stray wires. It already has solder bonded to it, so the final joint only needs a second or two of heat to merge the two coats.

Tinning also separates two jobs that otherwise fight each other. Wetting a cold, slightly oxidized wire and making a neat joint on a delicate pad are both hard; doing them at once often means too much heat on the board. Tin the wire first, where you can use plenty of heat and flux, and the joint on the board becomes quick and gentle.

1Strip only the length the joint needs

Match the strip length to where the wire is going: the depth of a solder cup, the thickness of the board plus a little for a through-hole, or the pad length for a surface pad. That is often 3 to 6 mm (1/8 to 1/4 in) for electronics. Longer bare ends just give solder more room to wick and leave exposed copper after the joint is made. Use a stripper sized to the gauge so you don't cut strands; every cut strand reduces the wire's capacity and makes it weaker.

2Twist the strands tightly

Roll the stripped strands between your finger and thumb in the direction of their existing lay until they form a tight, round bundle with no strays. Clean fingers are best, since skin oil on the copper resists wetting; some people twist with a gloved hand or pliers. A tight twist lets solder wick evenly through the bundle by capillary action rather than clumping on the outside.

3Add a little flux

Rosin-core solder carries its own flux, and on fresh copper that is often enough. For older wire, tinned-copper wire that has dulled, or anything that fought you last time, brush a little liquid or paste flux onto the bare strands. Flux removes the thin oxide layer and lets solder flow into the bundle instead of sitting on top. Use a no-clean or rosin flux for electronics, never acid plumbing flux, which keeps corroding copper afterward.

4Heat from below and feed solder from above

Put a small bead of fresh solder on the tip to make a good thermal contact, then hold the tip against the underside of the bare wire near the insulation end. Touch the solder wire to the top of the strands, on the opposite side from the iron. When the copper is hot enough, the solder melts on contact and is pulled into and through the strands. Draw the iron slowly toward the end of the wire, feeding a little solder, and lift off cleanly.

Thin 24 to 22 AWG wire tins in about a second; 16 AWG may take two or three with a small tip. If it takes much longer, heat transfer is the problem: use a larger chisel tip or a little more temperature rather than holding the iron there and cooking the insulation. Our guide to soldering iron tip types explains which shapes move heat best.

5Check the result and trim if needed

A well-tinned wire is shiny (satin with lead-free), uniformly coated, and still shows the spiral texture of the strands. It should be only slightly thicker than the bare bundle and should slide into the hole or cup it was meant for. If it's lumpy or oversized, reheat it with a clean tip and let the excess run onto the tip, then wipe the tip. If solder ran under the insulation, cut the end off and redo it with a shorter dwell.

Thin coat, texture visible, flexible at the insulation

Those three signs mean the solder wetted the strands rather than sitting on them, there is no excess to bridge or block a hole, and the wire will still bend where it leaves the jacket.

A few wire types need extra care. Tinned-copper hookup wire, the silvery kind, already has a thin tin coating from the factory, so it takes solder quickly as long as the coating isn't dull with age. Enamelled magnet wire has an insulating varnish that must be burned or scraped off first, or solder will never wet it; see how to solder magnet wire. Silicone-insulated test-lead wire tolerates heat well, while PVC hookup wire softens and shrinks back quickly, which is another reason to tin fast with a hot, well-tinned tip rather than slowly with a cool one.

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean to tin a wire?

Tinning means coating the bare end of a wire with a thin layer of solder before you join it to anything. The solder soaks between the strands and bonds them into one solid, pre-soldered end. When you later hold it to a pad or terminal that is also tinned, the two coats melt together almost instantly, so the final joint needs less heat and time.

Should you tin wires before putting them in screw terminals?

Generally no. Solder slowly deforms under constant clamping pressure, a behavior called creep or cold flow, so a tinned end can loosen in a screw terminal over months and heat up. Many terminal makers say not to do it. Use the bare, twisted strands or a crimped ferrule instead, and tighten to the terminal's specified torque.

Should you tin a wire before crimping it?

No. A crimp works by squashing the strands and the barrel together into a cold-welded mass. Solder makes the end rigid, so the crimp can't compress it properly and may loosen, and the joint becomes stiff right where the wire leaves the crimp. Crimp bare wire with the right tool and die, and add heat-shrink for strain relief.

Why won't solder stick to my wire when I tin it?

Usually the copper is oxidized, coated, or not hot enough. Old or tarnished wire needs fresh stripping or extra flux; enamelled magnet wire needs its coating removed first. If the solder balls up and rolls off, the wire isn't at solder temperature yet: hold the iron under it a moment longer, use a larger tip, or add a small bead of solder to the tip to improve heat transfer.

How far should solder go when tinning stranded wire?

Stop a short distance before the insulation, about one wire diameter, so the strands right at the insulation edge stay flexible. Solder that wicks under the insulation creates a stiff section that ends abruptly, and repeated bending cracks the wire at that point. Tin only as much length as the joint needs, typically the depth of the solder cup, pad or hole.
