Fluke 177 vs Fluke 179: Features and Accuracy Compared

The Fluke 179 is a Fluke 177 with a thermometer: Fluke publishes the same accuracy line for both meters on DC volts (±0.09% + 2), AC volts, AC and DC current, resistance, capacitance and frequency, and only the 179 adds a temperature function, −40 °C to 400 °C at ±(1.0% + 10), with an 80BK probe in the box. Same 420 g body, same CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating, same backlight, battery and lifetime warranty. Buy the 179 if you will measure temperature; otherwise the 177 gives identical electrical readings.

OhmSavvy hasn't hands-on tested either meter, so our editorial scores (4.7 for the 179, 4.6 for the 177) rest on Fluke's published specifications. Everything below comes from the two Fluke product pages, and the tolerances are our arithmetic on Fluke's numbers, not measurements.

Worked tolerances for everyday readings

A spec of ±(a% + n) means a percent of the displayed value plus n steps of the last digit. On a 6,000-count meter the step depends on the range: 0.001 V on 6 V, 0.01 V on 60 V, 0.1 V on 600 V. The table below works that out for readings an electrician or technician takes every week. Because the tables match, each row holds for the 177 and the 179 alike.

Two things stand out. At low readings the count part can be as large as the percentage part: on 24 V, the two counts (20 mV) nearly equal 0.09% of the reading (21.6 mV). And AC volts are about ten times looser than DC on both meters, so a 120 V receptacle that reads anywhere from 118.5 V to 121.5 V on the display is inside the published tolerance if the true value is 120.0 V. For a 4–20 mA loop the ±0.23 mA allowance at full scale is just under 1.5% of the 16 mA span, which is fine for finding a dead transmitter but not for calibrating one. How the 179's temperature spec behaves

The temperature line, ±(1.0% + 10), has a large count term. At 0.1 °C resolution, ten counts are a fixed 1.0 °C, so near room temperature that fixed part is most of the allowance, and only at a few hundred degrees does the percentage part take over.

These are the meter's figures alone. Any thermocouple probe has its own tolerance, and the reading you see includes both, so the real uncertainty is wider than the bars. In practice that means the 179 is right for trend and difference work: supply versus return air, a bearing that runs warmer than its twin, a heat sink climbing under load. For a temperature you must certify to a fraction of a degree, use a dedicated thermometer. To check that a probe itself is sound, see how to check a thermocouple with a multimeter.

A difference reading cancels some of the error

When you measure supply air and return air with the same meter and probe a minute apart, a fixed offset in the probe tends to appear in both readings and largely cancels in the difference. That makes the 179 more useful for a temperature split than its absolute ±(1.0% + 10) figure suggests. It doesn't cancel the percentage part, which differs slightly between the two readings, and it assumes the probe settles fully each time.

The current row matters most. If the 179's finest step is 0.01 mA (10 µA), it can't resolve a furnace flame-sensor signal of a few microamps, whatever the comparison table says. For that check, look at the Fluke 116 or 87V, discussed in our Fluke 87V vs 179 comparison.

Safety: keep the thermocouple off live parts

A thermocouple probe plugs into the same input jacks as the test leads, so treat it as part of the measuring circuit. Don't touch a bare probe tip to anything energized, and remove the probe before switching the 179 back to volts. Respect the meter's CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V limits and the lower rating of any lead or probe in use. On hot surfaces, check the probe's own temperature rating first; a probe can be rated well below the meter's 400 °C. When mains equipment is involved and you're unsure, call a qualified technician.

The bottom line

The Fluke 177 and 179 share every published electrical accuracy figure, the same backlit display, CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating, 420 g body, battery life and lifetime warranty. The 179 adds one thing: temperature from −40 °C to 400 °C at ±(1.0% + 10), with an 80BK probe in the box. Buy the 179 for HVAC, motor and maintenance work where temperature comes up; buy the 177 if your readings are purely electrical.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Fluke 177 and 179?+

Temperature. The 179 reads −40 °C to 400 °C at ±(1.0% + 10) with 0.1 °C resolution and comes with an 80BK probe. Otherwise Fluke publishes the same accuracy for both meters on DC and AC volts, current, resistance, capacitance and frequency, and they share the same backlit display, CAT IV 600V rating and lifetime warranty.

Is the Fluke 179 more accurate than the 177?+

No. Fluke's product pages list identical best accuracies: ±(0.09% + 2) on DC volts, ±(1.0% + 3) on AC volts, ±(1.0% + 3) and ±(1.5% + 3) on DC and AC current, ±(0.9% + 1) on resistance, ±(1.2% + 2) on capacitance and ±(0.1% + 1) on frequency. The 179 only adds a temperature line.

Does the Fluke 179 come with a temperature probe?+

Yes. Fluke lists the 80BK temperature probe in the 179's box, along with test leads, an installed 9 V battery and a quick reference guide. Fluke's 179 page doesn't state the probe's own rating, so check its markings before measuring hot surfaces; a probe can be rated lower than the meter's 400 °C ceiling.

Can the Fluke 179 measure microamps for a flame sensor?+

Not usefully, by the spec table. Fluke gives 0.01 mA (10 µA) as the best current resolution, while flame-sensor signals are typically a few microamps. The 179 page's comparison table does list µA, which conflicts, so we follow the more detailed spec table. For flame sensors, use a meter with a 0.1 µA range such as the Fluke 116 or 87V.

How accurate is the Fluke 179 at room temperature?+

About ±1.25 °C at 25 °C from the meter alone, worked from Fluke's ±(1.0% + 10) spec: 1% of 25 °C is 0.25 °C, and ten counts at 0.1 °C add 1.0 °C. The probe's own tolerance adds to that. At 200 °C the meter allowance grows to ±3.0 °C, and at 400 °C to ±5.0 °C.

Is the Fluke 179 worth paying more for than the 177?+

Only if you will use temperature. The extra money buys one function, −40 °C to 400 °C at ±(1.0% + 10), plus an 80BK probe in the box. It does not buy better electrical accuracy, a higher safety rating, a longer warranty or finer current resolution, because Fluke lists those identically for both meters. Without temperature work, the 177 is the better value.

Should an HVAC technician buy the 177 or the 179?+

The 179, in most cases. HVAC work involves temperature splits, motor checks and capacitor tests, and the 179 handles all three with the probe included, at the same electrical accuracy as the 177. Pick the 177 only if you already carry a clamp meter or thermometer that covers temperature and want to avoid paying for it twice.
