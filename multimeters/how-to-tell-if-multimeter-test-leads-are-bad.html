HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Tell If Multimeter Test Leads Are Bad

To tell if multimeter test leads are bad, set the meter to its lowest ohms range and press the two probe tips together: good leads typically read a few tenths of an ohm and hold steady, while a reading above about 1 Ω, a number that wanders, or OL means the leads need a closer look. Then flex each lead along its length while watching the display, and inspect the insulation, plugs and tips. Leads fail far more often than meters, usually at the two strain points where the cable meets the plug and the probe, and a broken conductor hidden inside intact insulation is the most common fault of all. The steps below find it in a couple of minutes and tell you which lead is the problem.

Symptoms and causes

Bad leads rarely announce themselves. More often they look like a different problem: a meter that seems faulty, a circuit that seems dead, or a resistor that seems out of tolerance. If you see any symptom in the left column, test the leads before you suspect anything else.

1Short the probes on ohms

Plug black into COM and red into the VΩ jack, pushing each plug fully home. Select the lowest resistance range (on a manual-ranging meter, usually 200 Ω) rather than continuity. Press the tips firmly together, metal to metal. What you see is the combined resistance of both leads, both plugs and the meter's internal contacts. On a healthy set that is typically a few tenths of an ohm, and it should settle within a second or two. The exact figure depends on the lead length, wire gauge and meter, so the most useful reference is your own leads when they were new; note the figure down once and you will spot a change straight away.

Probes-together reading (typical)VerdictWhat to do
About 0.1–0.5 Ω, steadyGoodUse REL or Δ to subtract it on low-ohm work

If the display reads OL, first rule out the obvious: the dial on volts instead of ohms, or a plug sitting in the wrong jack. If the meter is blank rather than showing OL, the leads are not the cause; see why a multimeter won't turn on. And for the meaning of OL across other ranges, read what OL means on a multimeter.

2Test each lead end to end

The probes-together test tells you the pair has a problem but not which lead. To isolate one, you need a second, known-good set. Plug the good leads into the meter, short them and press REL or Δ if your meter has it, so the display starts from zero. Now lay the suspect lead on the bench, touch one good probe to the metal of its banana plug and the other to its probe tip. You are now measuring that one lead alone. A short lead in good condition typically adds only a tenth of an ohm or so; one that reads much higher than its partner, or OL, is the faulty one. No spare set? Swap the leads one at a time with any lead you trust: whichever swap cures the fault identifies the bad lead.

While a lead is isolated like this, also check the jack it lives in. Plug the good lead into the jack the suspect lead came from and repeat the probes-together test. If the high reading follows the old lead, the lead is at fault. If it stays with the jack, the meter's socket is worn or dirty, and replacing the leads will not help. A worn input jack on a meter you use for mains work is a job for the maker's service center, not a home repair.

3Do the flex test

This step finds the fault that every other check misses. Keep the lead connected as in step 2, or keep the probes shorted as in step 1, and slowly bend the cable back and forth where it enters the banana plug, then where it enters the probe body, then along its length. Watch the display, not the lead. Copper strands fatigue and break inside the insulation at those strain points, and a lead with only a few strands left can read perfectly when lying straight and open the moment it bends. Any flicker, jump or flash of OL while you flex means a broken conductor. That lead is finished; trimming and re-terminating it removes the factory-molded strain relief and plug that carried its safety rating. The same method works on any cable, as our guide to finding a broken wire with a multimeter explains for longer runs. If your continuity beeper chatters while you probe, that can be a lead too; see intermittent continuity beeps.

4Inspect insulation, plugs and tips

Run the leads through your fingers in good light. Look for cuts, nicks, crushed spots, shiny melted patches from a soldering iron, and stiff or cracked insulation on old PVC leads. Some leads have a contrasting colored inner layer that shows through when the outer jacket wears; if you can see it, the lead is due for replacement. Next, the plugs: a shrouded banana plug should push into the jack with some resistance and not wobble or fall out under its own weight. Dull, oxidized plugs can be cleaned with isopropyl alcohol, but a loose fit means the spring contacts are worn. Try a known-good plug in the same jack to tell a worn plug from a worn jack. Finally, the tips: a bent, blunt or loose tip makes poor contact and can slip on live terminals. Many leads come with snap-on tip caps that limit the exposed metal; keep them on for panel work, where a long bare tip can bridge two terminals.

Damaged leads have no place on mains

Test leads are the part of the meter your hands are on, and their insulation is all that stands between you and the circuit. Never use leads with cracked, cut or melted insulation on mains or any high-energy circuit, and don't repair them with tape or heat-shrink. Before testing that a circuit is dead, prove your meter on a known live source, test, then prove it again, so a lead that opened mid-job can't make live wiring look safe. For work in a service panel, your meter and leads both need a suitable CAT III or higher rating; if you are not trained for panel work, call a licensed electrician.

5Replace with the right CAT rating

A meter and its leads are a system, and the system is only as safe as its lowest rating. Replacement leads should carry a CAT rating and voltage at least equal to your meter's, and to the work you actually do. Fitting CAT II leads to a CAT III meter turns the whole setup into a CAT II tool. Fitting leads rated higher than the meter does not raise the meter's rating, but it costs nothing in safety. Look for the rating printed or molded on the lead and the probe body, not just on the packaging. Silicone insulation stays flexible in the cold and resists soldering-iron burns better than PVC, which makes it the comfortable choice for bench use.

CAT IIPlug-in appliances and wall outletsCAT II or higher, at the meter's voltage

How much a bad lead skews readings

Lead resistance adds directly to every resistance you measure, so its effect depends on what you are measuring. The arithmetic is simple: error equals lead resistance divided by the true value. With a good 0.3 Ω pair, a 10 Ω part reads 10.3 Ω, an error of 3%. With a damaged pair at 1.5 Ω, the same part reads 11.5 Ω, a 15% error that could make a good heating element or speaker coil look faulty. On a 1 kΩ resistor those same leads cause errors of only 0.03% and 0.15%, which is why worn leads go unnoticed on everyday work and then cause confusion the first time you measure something small.

The REL or Δ button subtracts a steady lead resistance, but it cannot correct a broken conductor whose resistance changes as you move, so null only leads that have passed the flex test. If your leads pass every check and readings still wander, the cause lies elsewhere; our guides to unstable multimeter readings and a multimeter showing the wrong voltage cover the next suspects. Short, isolate, flex, inspect

A steady few tenths of an ohm with the probes together means the conductors are sound. Anything higher, anything that moves when the cable bends, and any visible damage to the insulation means the leads come out of service. Replacements must match or beat the meter's CAT rating.

Frequently asked questions

What should my multimeter read when I touch the leads together?

On the lowest ohms range, a good pair of leads typically reads somewhere around 0.1 to 0.5 Ω, and the figure should settle quickly and stay put. That small value is the resistance of the leads, the plugs and the meter's own contacts. A steady reading above roughly 1 Ω, a value that keeps drifting, or OL points at the leads or the jacks.

Can bad test leads give wrong voltage readings?

Yes. A lead with a broken conductor can make an intermittent connection, so the meter shows zero, a low figure or a number that jumps around on a circuit that is really live. That is dangerous, because a broken lead can make live wiring look dead. Always prove the meter and leads on a known live source before and after testing a circuit you believe is off.

Can I repair a cut in my multimeter lead with electrical tape?

No. Tape does not restore the insulation rating the leads were certified to, and it can slide or peel off in use. Leads are a low-cost part compared with the risk of a shock or an arc flash at mains voltage. If the outer insulation is cut, cracked or melted, or a contrasting inner layer is showing through, retire the leads and buy a properly rated set.

Why does my continuity tester beep with bad leads?

Continuity mode only checks whether resistance is below a threshold, and on many meters that threshold is in the tens of ohms. A lead that has lost most of its strands may add a few ohms and still beep perfectly. Use the ohms range instead, which shows the actual figure, so you can see that a pair reading 4 Ω instead of 0.3 Ω has a problem.

Do I need test leads from the same brand as my meter?

No. Banana plugs on handheld meters follow a common 4 mm format, so most third-party leads fit. What matters is the rating: the leads should carry a CAT rating and voltage equal to or higher than your meter's and the work you do, from a maker that marks the rating on the lead itself. Check that the shrouded plugs seat fully in your meter's jacks.

How often should multimeter test leads be replaced?

There is no fixed interval; replace them on condition. Check them every time you pick up the meter for mains work: a quick probes-together reading and a look along the insulation take seconds. Replace them whenever the insulation is damaged, the reading wanders when you flex the cable, the plugs fit loosely, or the tips are bent or worn down. Heavy daily use wears leads out sooner.
