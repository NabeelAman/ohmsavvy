Rigol DHO914S vs Siglent SDS1104X-E for a Home Lab

Rigol DHO914S vs Siglent SDS1104X-E pits a one-box bench against an established four-channel bench scope. The DHO914S is a 125 MHz, 12-bit scope with a built-in 25 MHz signal generator, a Bode plot function, 16 digital channels (with a separately sold probe) and up to 50 Mpts. The SDS1104X-E has 100 MHz, keeps 500 MSa/s on all four channels, decodes I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN for free and has a widely praised, intuitive interface.

Rigol DHO914S vs Siglent SDS1104X-E for a Home Lab

A home lab rarely has room or budget for every instrument at once, so the real question is what you will plug into the scope. A scope only observes; to test a filter, an amplifier or a sensor input you also need something that produces a known signal. The DHO914S includes that source. With the SDS1104X-E you either already own a function generator, buy Siglent's USB generator module, or use whatever your circuit makes on its own.

Bode plot is the feature that turns the DHO914S generator from handy into useful. It sweeps the generator across a frequency range, from as low as 10 Hz up to 25 MHz, measures input and output at each step and draws the gain and phase curve. That is how you check the corner frequency of an RC filter, the response of a guitar pedal tone stack or the roll-off of an op-amp stage without plotting dozens of points by hand.

The Siglent earns its place on a different kind of bench: one where four signals move quickly at the same time. Holding 500 MSa/s per channel with all inputs active, it captures a clock, chip select, data line and interrupt together with more samples on each than the Rigol can give. Its 100,000 wfm/s normal update rate helps a glitch show up on screen without switching modes, and Siglent includes LIN, CAN, I2C, SPI and UART decoding at no charge. Its interface is also widely regarded as intuitive, which counts on a tool you reach for in short evening sessions.

What each scope can and can't measure

A bandwidth rating is the frequency at which a sine reads 3 dB low, so a scope is only accurate well below it. Keeping signals three to five times under the rating gives the DHO914S a useful range of roughly 25 to 42 MHz and the SDS1104X-E roughly 20 to 33 MHz. Both comfortably cover UART, I2C, PWM, audio and the switching frequency of small converters; the gap only shows on clocks or edges in the 30 MHz region. Read more in what oscilloscope bandwidth means.

That bandwidth edge reverses once you use every input. Dividing each scope's four-channel sample rate by its rated bandwidth, the Rigol gets 2.5 samples per cycle at 125 MHz and the Siglent 5 at 100 MHz. Both are above the Nyquist minimum of two, but the Siglent has more room for interpolation, so on four fast channels at once it draws edges more faithfully. On one or two channels the Rigol's 1.25 GSa/s and 625 MSa/s reverse that again. The GSa/s explainer covers why per-channel rate matters more than the headline.

Resolution decides how small a detail you can read. At 100 mV per division across eight divisions, an 8-bit converter divides the 800 mV screen into steps of about 3.1 mV, while a 12-bit converter gets about 0.2 mV. Real scopes add their own noise, so the practical gain is smaller than that ratio, but on a 20 mV ripple riding on a 5 V rail the Rigol shows a cleaner, steadier shape when you zoom in.

Instrument count versus channel speed

The DHO914S replaces a scope and a basic generator; the SDS1104X-E is the stronger pure scope when all four inputs are busy. Decide by whether a signal source is already on your bench.

Mains-connected circuits

On both scopes the probe ground is tied to the instrument's ground, which normally reaches mains earth through the power cord or adapter and anything else connected to it. Never clip it to a mains-referenced node; use a differential probe instead.

The bottom line

For a home lab starting from scratch, the Rigol DHO914S is the more complete bench: a 125 MHz 12-bit scope, a 25 MHz generator with Bode plot, up to 50 Mpts and a path to 16 logic channels, all in one USB-C powered box. Choose the Siglent SDS1104X-E if you already own a generator or mostly watch four fast signals together, where its 500 MSa/s per channel, 100,000 wfm/s update rate and free decoding including LIN make it the stronger pure oscilloscope.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Siglent SDS1104X-E have a built-in signal generator like the DHO914S?+

No. Siglent offers a generator for the four-channel SDS1000X-E models only as an optional USB AWG module. The DHO914S has a single-channel 25 MHz arbitrary/function generator built in, with sine to 25 MHz, square to 15 MHz and arbitrary waveforms to 10 MHz, plus a Bode plot function that uses it.

What can I do with the DHO914S Bode plot in a home lab?+

Measure frequency response. The scope sweeps its generator from as low as 10 Hz up to 25 MHz into your circuit and plots gain and phase at each point. That shows the cutoff of an RC or active filter, the bandwidth of an op-amp stage or the tone curve of an audio circuit without taking readings by hand.

Which is better for four-channel work, the DHO914S or the SDS1104X-E?+

The SDS1104X-E when all four signals are fast. It keeps 500 MSa/s per channel against the Rigol's 312.5 MSa/s, giving about 5 samples per cycle at its rated bandwidth versus 2.5. The Rigol counters with 10 Mpts per channel against 7 Mpts and 12-bit resolution, which help with slower, longer or smaller signals.

Is 12-bit resolution or a faster update rate more useful?+

It depends on the fault. The Rigol's 12 bits show small amplitude details such as rail ripple and sensor noise more clearly. The Siglent's 100,000 wfm/s normal update rate, against Rigol's 30,000, helps rare glitches appear sooner in normal mode, though Rigol's UltraAcquire mode reaches 1,000,000 wfm/s when you switch to it.

Do both scopes decode serial buses without extra licenses?+

Yes. Rigol lists parallel, UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN decoding as standard on the DHO914S, and Siglent lists I2C, SPI, UART/RS232, CAN and LIN as standard on the SDS1104X-E. For a home lab working with Arduino, ESP32 or automotive modules, neither scope needs a paid upgrade to read those buses.
