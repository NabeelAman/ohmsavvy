How to Use a Soldering Iron

Soldering an electronic joint well comes down to one idea: heat the metal parts until they are hot enough to melt solder on their own, then feed solder into that heat — never melt solder directly on the tip and drip it onto a cold joint. This guide walks through a complete first repair or build session, from tinning a brand-new tip to recognizing a joint that needs redoing.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 21, 2026

Basic safety before you plug in

  • A soldering iron tip commonly runs at 300–370°C (570–700°F) — hot enough to cause a serious burn instantly. Always return the iron to its stand between joints; never set it down on a bench.
  • Solder fumes are mostly flux vapor, which irritates eyes and airways with prolonged exposure. Work in a ventilated area or use a small fan/extractor to pull fumes away from your face, and avoid breathing directly over the joint.
  • Lead-based solder (60/40 or 63/37) is a health hazard if ingested — wash your hands after soldering and before eating, and don't eat at the bench.

What you need before you start

Beyond the iron itself, a short list of basics makes every joint easier and safer:

Stand & sponge/brass wool

Holds the hot iron safely and cleans the tip between joints.

Rosin-core solder

60/40 or 63/37 leaded, or a lead-free alloy — flux is built into the core.

Liquid or paste flux

For rework, larger pads, or oxidized surfaces core flux alone won't wet.

Wire cutters/strippers

To trim leads flush after soldering and prep wire ends.

Helping-hands or vise

Frees both hands for the iron and the solder spool.

Ventilation

A fan or extractor pulling flux fumes away from your face.

The session at a glance

Every joint, whether it's a single wire splice or a full board of through-hole parts, follows the same six-stage routine. Once this becomes automatic, soldering stops being intimidating.

1Tin a new or freshly-cleaned tip

A brand-new tip ships with a thin protective coating, not solder. Heat the iron to around 300°C, then as soon as it reaches temperature, melt a small amount of fresh solder directly onto the tip so it fully coats the working end in a thin, shiny layer. This is called tinning, and it's what actually transfers heat into a joint — a dry, untinned tip barely conducts heat at all, even red-hot. Re-tin after every cleaning, since wiping the tip removes the solder layer along with the oxide.

2Prep the joint before the iron touches it

Strip wire ends to bare, clean copper and twist stranded wire so it doesn't fray. On a PCB, make sure the component lead sits fully through the hole and is bent slightly to hold it in place. Both surfaces being soldered — not just one — need to be clean metal; solder won't wet oxidized, painted, or greasy surfaces no matter how long you apply heat.

3Heat the joint itself, not the solder

Touch the tip so it contacts both parts of the joint at once — for example, the component lead and the copper pad, or both wires being spliced — and hold it there for roughly one to two seconds before introducing any solder. The goal is for the metal itself to reach solder's melting point, so the solder melts because the joint is hot, not because the iron melted it separately. This single habit is the biggest difference between a strong joint and a cold one.

4Feed solder into the heat, away from the tip

With the tip still holding the joint at temperature, touch the solder wire to the joint on the side opposite the iron — not to the tip itself. Properly heated metal will pull the molten solder in and let it flow around the joint on its own (this flow is called wetting). Feed just enough solder to cover the joint with a thin, smooth fillet; too much pools into a blob, too little leaves exposed metal or a weak mechanical bond.

5Remove the solder, then the iron, then hold still

Pull the solder wire away first, then lift the iron a moment later. Keep the joint completely motionless for a few seconds while the solder solidifies — movement during this window is the single most common cause of a cracked or cold joint, since it disturbs the metal crystal structure as it sets.

Why 'don't move it' matters so much

Solder needs to cool through its melting range undisturbed to form one continuous crystal structure. Jostle it mid-cool and you get a fractured, dull-gray joint with poor conductivity and weak mechanical strength, even though it may look fine from a glance.

6Inspect the finished joint

A good joint is a smooth, shiny, slightly concave cone shape that clearly wets both the lead and the pad or wire. Compare it against the signs below before moving to the next joint.

LookWhat it usually means
Shiny, smooth, concave coneGood joint — move on
Dull, grainy, or cracked surfaceCold joint — the metal moved while cooling, or wasn't hot enough; reheat and redo
Ball-shaped blob, not wetted to the padJoint wasn't hot enough when solder was applied — reheat and add a touch more solder
Solder bridging two adjacent pads/pinsToo much solder, or pins too close — remove the bridge and reflow with less solder

What the fillet shape actually tells you

Looking at a joint from the side, in cross-section, says more than a quick glance from above. A wetted joint tapers from the pad up to the lead at a shallow, concave angle, because the molten solder was pulled thin across the whole surface. A cold or unwetted joint instead balls up at a much steeper angle, because the solder never spread properly in the first place — it just solidified roughly where it landed.

Common first-timer mistakes to watch for

  • Melting solder directly onto the tip, then wiping it onto the joint — this skips proper heating of the joint itself.
  • Pulling the iron away the instant solder starts to flow, before the joint is fully wetted.
  • Holding a part in loose tweezers that lets it shift during cooling.
  • Using a cold, oxidized tip and wondering why nothing melts — see the troubleshooting guide for this specifically.

Myth: blowing on a joint cools it faster, with no downside

It's a common habit, but moving air across a joint while it's still molten is just another form of disturbing it during the exact window it needs to stay still — the same problem as a nudged wire or a twitchy hand. If you want a joint to cool sooner, give it a moment longer under the iron so less heat has soaked into the surrounding board, then leave it alone in still air rather than blowing on it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I hold the iron on a joint?

Usually one to three seconds once the joint is up to temperature, for small through-hole connections on a typical PCB. If you are routinely holding the tip on a joint for five seconds or longer to get solder to flow, the iron is underpowered for the joint, the tip is dirty, or the temperature is set too low — not something to compensate for by simply waiting longer, since the extra time increases the risk of heat damage to the component or the board.

Do I need flux if my solder already has a flux core?

Core flux handles most clean, straightforward joints. Add extra liquid or paste flux when you are reworking an existing joint, soldering to a larger pad or ground plane that pulls heat away quickly, or working with an oxidized surface — the core flux in the wire alone often isn't enough once a joint needs rework rather than a first pass.

Why does my solder joint look dull and grainy instead of shiny?

That appearance is the classic sign of a cold solder joint: the solder cooled before it fully wetted the metal, usually because the joint was moved while cooling, the parts weren't hot enough when solder was applied, or the iron was removed too early. A good joint looks smooth and slightly concave (a shiny cone), not lumpy, grainy, or cracked.

Can I reuse solder that's already stuck to the tip?

Not for a new joint. Old solder sitting on the tip oxidizes and loses flux activity, so it won't wet a fresh joint properly even if it still looks silver. Wipe the tip clean on a damp sponge or brass wool, re-tin it, then feed new solder from the spool into the actual joint.

What temperature should I set my soldering iron to?

Common starting points are about 330 to 350 °C (625 to 660 °F) for leaded 63/37 or 60/40 solder and about 350 to 380 °C (660 to 715 °F) for lead-free alloys. Go higher for large pads and thick wires. Use the lowest setting that lets a joint flow within one to three seconds, because a hotter tip oxidizes faster and wears sooner.
