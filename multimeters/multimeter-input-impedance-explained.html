Multimeter Input Impedance Explained

A multimeter's input impedance is the resistance it presents to whatever you're measuring, and on most digital multimeters' voltage ranges that figure is a standard 10 MΩ. It matters because the meter itself always draws a tiny bit of current to take a reading, and on a circuit with high enough source resistance, that current pulls the reading down below the real voltage, an effect called loading. The same 10 MΩ input resistance is also why a digital multimeter can pick up a "ghost voltage" on a wire that isn't actually connected to anything live. This guide works through both effects with real numbers, and when a dedicated low-impedance (LoZ) mode is worth having.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 28, 2026

A standard 10 MΩ input is fine for almost all electronics and electrical work. The exception is chasing ghost voltages on disconnected wiring, where a dedicated LoZ mode helps. See how to measure resistance with a voltmeter for the same loading effect used on purpose.

Why input impedance matters

Every voltmeter, digital or analog, needs to draw at least a tiny current to produce a reading — there's no way to measure voltage with truly zero current flow. That current has to come from somewhere, and it flows through whatever resistance the circuit you're probing actually has. The meter's own input resistance sets how much current it draws for a given voltage, and that determines two very different-looking problems: an accuracy error on real circuits, and a false reading on circuits that aren't actually carrying any meaningful current at all.

The 10 MΩ standard, and what it replaced

Nearly every modern digital multimeter specifies a 10 MΩ input resistance on its DC and AC voltage ranges. It's high enough to be a non-issue for the vast majority of circuits, from household wiring to most electronics, and standardizing on one figure makes meters interchangeable for everyday work.

Older analog (needle-type) meters instead carry a sensitivity rating in ohms-per-volt, commonly 20,000 Ω/V. The actual input resistance on a given range is that figure multiplied by the full-scale voltage of the range in use — so a 20,000 Ω/V meter set to a 10 V range presents only 200 kΩ, fifty times lower than a 10 MΩ digital meter. That's a real, practically-sized difference, not a rounding error.

The loading effect, with the actual math

When the meter's input resistance (Rin) connects across a source that has its own internal resistance (Rs), the two form a voltage divider. The meter only sees the fraction of the true voltage that falls across its own input: reading = true voltage × Rin ÷ (Rs + Rin). The higher Rin is relative to Rs, the closer the reading gets to the truth.

Worked example: a source with 100 kΩ of internal resistance reads 5.000 V unloaded. On a 10 MΩ meter, the reading is 5.000 × 10,000 ÷ (100 + 10,000) = 4.95 V, a 1% error. On a 200 kΩ analog meter (20,000 Ω/V on a 10 V range), the same source reads 5.000 × 200 ÷ (100 + 200) = 3.33 V, a 33% error — enough to send you chasing a fault that doesn't exist.

High-resistance sources are where this bites

Loading error only becomes noticeable when the source resistance is a meaningful fraction of the meter's input resistance. A stiff, low-impedance source like a battery or a power supply rail is essentially immune. A high-resistance sensor output, a long signal line, or a circuit deliberately built with large resistors is where a low-impedance meter can mislead you.

Ghost voltages: the same effect in reverse

A disconnected wire sitting near a live one can pick up a small voltage through stray capacitive or inductive coupling, with no actual current path and essentially infinite source resistance. A standard 10 MΩ meter draws so little current that it can't discharge that stray voltage, so it displays a real-looking reading, sometimes close to full line voltage, on a wire that is genuinely safe to the touch and carries no usable power. This is the single most common cause of "my multimeter says the wire is live but it isn't."

What a LoZ mode actually does

A low-impedance (LoZ) mode deliberately switches in a much lower input resistance, often in the low thousands of ohms, for the duration of the reading. That low resistance draws enough current to collapse a stray, uncoupled voltage down to near zero, while a genuinely live wire — backed by a real low-impedance source like the utility supply — still reads its true voltage almost unaffected. It's the same loading effect from the sections above, deliberately engineered in as a feature rather than suffered as an error.

LoZ is a feature, not a default

LoZ mode exists specifically for electricians and anyone chasing ghost voltages in building wiring. It's usually a separate setting from the standard voltage range, not something you leave on permanently, since it draws more current from whatever you're probing than the standard 10 MΩ range does.

Which meter to use

Any standard 10 MΩ digital multimeter is fine for the overwhelming majority of electronics and electrical work. If ghost voltages on building wiring are a recurring problem, a meter with a dedicated LoZ mode saves real time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good input impedance for a multimeter?

10 MΩ is the standard for digital multimeters and is high enough that loading error is negligible on almost every circuit you'll measure, electronics included. Older analog meters are rated in ohms-per-volt instead (often 20,000 Ω/V), which works out to a much lower, range-dependent input resistance that can measurably load sensitive circuits.

Why does my multimeter show voltage on a wire that should be dead?

This is almost always a ghost voltage: stray capacitive or inductive coupling from a nearby live wire induces a small voltage on the disconnected one. A standard 10 MΩ meter draws so little current that it can't pull that induced charge down, so it displays a real-looking but meaningless voltage. A LoZ mode fixes this by adding a low-resistance load that bleeds the stray charge away.

Does input impedance affect resistance or current measurements?

Not in the same way. Input impedance specifically describes the voltage ranges. The resistance (ohms) range uses its own internal current source and is a separate specification, and the current (amps) ranges insert a low-value shunt or fuse in series, with its own 'burden voltage' spec rather than an input-impedance figure.

Is a higher input impedance always better?

For avoiding loading error, yes. For spotting an unterminated or floating wire, a very high impedance meter is more likely to show a confusing ghost voltage, not less. That's exactly why LoZ mode exists as a separate, selectable setting rather than the default.

How do I know if my multimeter has a LoZ mode?

Check the meter's spec sheet or manual for 'LoZ' or 'low impedance' mode, or a dedicated button for it. It's a feature on higher-end electrical-focused meters (several Fluke and Klein models carry it) and isn't present on most budget or general-purpose meters.
