Fluke 106 vs Fluke 107

Fluke 106 vs Fluke 107 is a choice between two meters that share one body. Both are 142 × 69 × 28 mm, 200 g, CAT III 600V and 6,000-count, and Fluke documents them in a single users manual. The difference is a short list of functions the 107 adds, so the right pick depends on whether you work on electronics or only on wiring.

What frequency and duty cycle actually show you

Many modern loads are switched on and off thousands of times a second rather than fed a steady voltage: PC and HVAC fans, LED dimmers, small motor controllers and fuel injectors. Put a DC-volts probe on one of those signals and you get an average that tells you little. The 107's Hz and % modes read the switching rate and the share of each cycle spent on, which is what the controller is actually changing.

Worked example, not a measurement: a 4-wire PC fan's PWM input typically runs at about 25 kHz, well inside the 107's 100 kHz limit. If the 107 reads 40.0% duty and the fan still won't spin up, the controller is doing its job and the fan or its tach wiring is the suspect. On a 106 you would only see a fixed-looking DC figure and could not tell a 40% command from a stuck output. The manual also lets the 107 read frequency on its current input, handy for checking line frequency through a load. For a full fan procedure, see how to test a 4-wire fan motor with a multimeter.

Where each meter earns its place

The 106 is a capable wiring meter. Receptacle voltage, a blown cartridge fuse, a heater element's resistance, or the running current of a 5 A appliance on the 10 A range are all inside its reach, with the same tolerance the 107 would give. Where it runs out is semiconductors and switched signals. Without a diode range you can't confirm an LED, a rectifier or a flyback diode, and a resistance reading across a junction depends on a test voltage Fluke doesn't publish for the ohms ranges, so it proves little.

That matters most in three places: hobby electronics, car electrical work and appliance control boards. A car's alternator diodes, LED tail lights and PWM-driven fuel pumps all need either the diode range or the duty-cycle mode. If any of that is on your list, the 107 is the only one of the pair that helps; if your work stays on the mains side of the panel, the 106 loses nothing.

What the diode test is for

A diode test pushes a small current through a semiconductor junction and shows its forward voltage, which is how you check rectifier diodes, LEDs and many transistor junctions. On the 107 that current is under 0.6 mA from an open-circuit voltage of about 2.0 V. Our guide on how to test a diode with a multimeter shows the readings to expect.

Value: what each step up actually buys

We don't quote prices, because they move weekly, but value can still be judged by what each tier adds and what it takes away. Count the measuring functions and the 106 has eight: AC volts, DC volts, AC millivolts, AC amps, DC amps, resistance, continuity and capacitance. The 107 has eleven, adding diode, frequency and duty cycle, and it gives up nothing to get them: the same 200 g body, the same tolerances, the same battery life.

That makes 106 to 107 the cleanest upgrade in Fluke's entry range, a pure addition. The next steps are trades rather than additions, which is why the ladder below says what you lose as well as what you gain.

The value case for the 106 is narrow but real. A second meter for a van or kitchen drawer, a meter for someone who will only ever check receptacles and batteries, or a shared tool where fewer modes means fewer mistakes: in those roles the three missing functions have no worth to you. Everywhere else, the cost of missing them is a second instrument, because there is no reliable workaround for a diode test or a frequency reading on a meter that lacks them. What neither pocket Fluke does

  • No milliamp or microamp ranges. The smallest current range is 4.000 A with 1 mA resolution, too coarse for standby draw or sensor loops. Our milliamp measuring guide explains what to use instead.
  • Not True RMS. Fluke says so outright for the 107, and the 106 is positioned for jobs where True RMS isn't needed. Distorted waveforms can read high or low.
  • No temperature, NCV or low-impedance mode. For those, look at the Fluke 17B+ (temperature) or the Fluke 117 (VoltAlert and LoZ).

Safety: a small meter is still a CAT III tool, not a CAT IV one

Both meters are rated CAT III 600V, which covers branch circuits and panels but not the service drop or meter base. Check the leads and the 11 A fuse before measuring current, never use Data Hold to judge whether an unknown circuit is live, and confirm the meter on a known live source before and after a dead test. If a panel reading surprises you, stop and call a licensed electrician.

Choose the Fluke 106 if…

  • ✓You mainly check outlets, batteries and fuses and never test diodes
  • ✓You want the simpler of the two, with fewer modes behind the yellow button
  • ✓You never look at signal frequency, fan PWM or injector pulses

Choose the Fluke 107 if…

  • ✓You repair electronics and need a diode test
  • ✓You check fan, motor or PWM signals and want frequency and duty cycle
  • ✓You want a backlit display, which Fluke lists for the 107 but not for the 106

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 107 if you touch electronics at all: diode test, frequency, duty cycle and a backlight cost little extra in the same 200 g body, with identical accuracy on everything else. The Fluke 106 is the right pick only as a pure electrical backup meter for voltage, current, resistance and continuity, where those extras would go unused.

Frequently asked questions

Are the Fluke 106 and 107 the same size?+

Yes. Fluke lists both at 142 × 69 × 28 mm and 200 g, both run on two AAA cells for at least 200 hours, and they share one users manual and one accuracy table. Only the function set differs: the 107 adds diode test, frequency, duty cycle and a listed backlight.

Does the Fluke 106 have a diode test?+

No. Fluke's 106/107 manual marks the diode test as a 107-only function, along with frequency and duty cycle. You can sometimes spot a dead short across a diode on the 106's resistance range, but you can't read a forward voltage or confirm an LED lights, so electronics work really calls for the 107.

Is either the Fluke 106 or 107 True RMS?+

No. Fluke states the 107 is not a True RMS meter and positions the 106 for work where True RMS accuracy isn't needed. On clean sine-wave mains that's fine. For variable-speed drives, dimmers and electronic loads, look at a Fluke 115 or 117, which read the true heating value of distorted waveforms.

Can the Fluke 106 or 107 measure milliamps?+

Not usefully. Fluke lists only 4.000 A and 10.00 A current ranges on both, with 1 mA as the finest resolution on the 4 A range and no dedicated mA or µA input. That rules out parasitic-drain checks, 4-20 mA loops and microcontroller sleep current. The Fluke 15B+ and 17B+ add µA and mA ranges.

Which pocket Fluke is better for a car toolbox?+

The 107. Both cover battery voltage, fuse continuity and 10 A current, but the 107's duty-cycle mode reads fuel-injector and PWM fuel-pump signals, its frequency mode checks some sensor outputs, and its diode range tests alternator diodes. Its backlight also helps under a dash or in a dark engine bay.

What frequency can the Fluke 107 measure?+

Fluke specifies 10 Hz to 100 kHz across five ranges with ±(0.1% + 3) accuracy, and duty cycle from 1% to 99% at 0.1% resolution. The manual lets you switch to frequency while reading AC volts or AC current, so you can check a 60 Hz line or a switching signal without moving the leads.

Does the Fluke 106 have a backlight or data hold?+

Fluke's US page for the 106 lists neither, and its product photo shows no backlight button, while the 107 page lists both a backlit display and data hold. If you take readings in a dark panel or somewhere you can't watch the screen, that alone can justify the 107, since every shared measurement is otherwise identical.
