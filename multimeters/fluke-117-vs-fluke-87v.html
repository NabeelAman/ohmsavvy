Fluke 117 vs Fluke 87V

The Fluke 117 is the electrician's meter: CAT III 600V, VoltAlert non-contact detection and a LoZ AutoVolt position for ghost voltage. The Fluke 87V is the industrial troubleshooter: CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V, DC accuracy ten times tighter, microamp resolution, a 19,999-count mode, peak capture and a low-pass filter for motor drives. Buy the 117 for building wiring up to the panel; buy the 87V for service entrances, 1000V systems, drives and fine readings.

Fluke 117 vs 87V spec by spec

Unlike the 115 and 117, which share one accuracy table, these two come from different Fluke families, so almost every line differs. The charts pick out the four numbers that change what you can safely and usefully measure.

To put the accuracy gap in numbers, take a 24.00 V DC control supply. Using Fluke's figures, the 117's ±(0.5% + 2) on its 60.00 V range allows 0.12 V plus 2 counts of 0.01 V, so ±0.14 V. The 87V's ±(0.05% + 1) allows 0.012 V plus one count, roughly ±0.02 V in its standard 6,000-count mode. For checking that a supply is "about 24 V" the 117 is plenty; for calibrating a 4-20 mA transmitter or trimming a reference, the 87V's margin is the reason it exists.

The bar graph isn't an 87V exclusive

Older write-ups, including an earlier version of this page, credit only the 87V with an analog bar graph. Fluke's 117 spec tab lists a 33-segment bar graph refreshing 32 times a second; the 87V's has 32 segments and refreshes 40 times a second. Both let you watch a trend while you adjust something. What the 87V adds for fast events is the separate 250 µs peak-capture mode, which records a spike the bar graph would only flash.

CAT III 600V vs CAT IV 600V: where each meter is allowed

Measurement categories describe how much energy a transient can deliver at the point you probe. Close to the utility transformer, a lightning-induced surge or switching spike has very little wiring impedance to slow it down; at a receptacle on the far end of a branch circuit, it has a lot. The IEC 61010 impulse tables typically set a 6 kV test for CAT III 600V and 8 kV for CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V. Fluke says the 87V is designed to withstand spikes above 8,000 V.

In practice, a residential or commercial electrician working inside panels and on branch circuits stays in CAT III, where the 117 is rated. Readings at the meter socket, the service conductors, an outdoor feeder or a utility-side disconnect are CAT IV work, and so is anything above 600 V, such as some solar strings and 690 V industrial distribution. That is where the 87V's rating, not its accuracy, decides the purchase.

Why drive technicians pick the 87V

A variable-frequency drive builds its output from fast PWM pulses. A True RMS meter reads all of that high-frequency content, so the voltage on the motor leads can look far higher than the fundamental the motor actually responds to. The 87V's low-pass filter, per Fluke, has its 3 dB point at 1 kHz, which strips the carrier and leaves a reading that tracks the drive's set frequency. The 117 has no filter, so its readings on drive outputs are hard to interpret. Add 200 kHz frequency, duty cycle and peak capture, and the 87V covers most of what a drive commissioning check needs short of a scope.

Fluke 117 vs 87V for electronics repair

On a repair bench the questions change. Nobody needs CAT IV to probe a 5 V rail, but you do care how small a change the meter can show, whether it reads a PWM signal's duty cycle, and whether it can measure the few milliamps or microamps a board draws in standby. Here the two Flukes are far apart, and the gap favors the 87V almost everywhere.

What that means in practice: tracing a voltage drop across a PCB trace, a current-sense resistor or a connector pin often comes down to a few millivolts, which the 117's 0.1 mV step shows coarsely and the 87V's 10 µV step shows clearly. Duty cycle lets the 87V confirm that a fan controller or LED dimmer is actually modulating; the 117 can only give you the frequency. And because the 117's smallest current step is 1 mA on a 6 A range, it can't tell a healthy 200 µA sleep draw from a faulty 800 µA one, a job the 87V does in its µA position.

The 117 isn't useless on a bench. It reads capacitance from 1 nF, has a 2 V diode test and a continuity beeper below 20 Ω, and its True RMS AC input handles a mains-side power supply check. If you mostly replace fuses, check rails and swap obvious bad parts, it is enough. The low-pass filter is the one 87V feature that matters less here: it exists for drive outputs and rarely changes a bench reading. Fluke 117 vs 87V for automotive diagnostics

A car is a 12 V DC system, so the 117's headline features, VoltAlert and the LoZ AutoVolt position, do little under the hood. What a technician needs is fast capture of intermittent faults, enough current capacity for a fused circuit, a duty-cycle reading for PWM-controlled parts and, on many jobs, temperature. Neither Fluke lists an RPM or dwell function in the specifications we cite, so tach work needs a dedicated automotive meter or a scan tool.

The Min/Max difference is the one to understand. Both record minimum, maximum and average while you wiggle a connector or crank the engine, and the 117's continuity mode, per Fluke's 110 Series datasheet, flags opens and shorts as brief as 500 µs. The 87V adds a separate peak-capture mode that holds a 250 µs spike, which is how you catch an ignition-related transient or a relay that chatters for a moment.

Parasitic drain is where the 117 stops short. Many technicians use a figure around 50 mA as a rough after-sleep ceiling (check the vehicle maker's number). The 117 will show 0.050 A on its 6 A range, so it can follow a module settling from 35 mA to 22 mA, but only in whole milliamps, and it has no microamp range for motorcycles or other small batteries. The 87V reads the same drain on its mA input with finer steps. Its thermocouple input, -200 °C to 1090 °C at the meter (the 80BK probe in the box is rated -40 °C to 260 °C), also covers coolant and intake air checks. Two things run the other way. The 117 keeps VoltAlert and LoZ, which the 87V doesn't list, so a residential electrician moving up to an 87V usually ends up buying a separate pen detector too. Safety: the rating covers the whole setup

A meter's CAT rating only holds if the test leads carry the same or higher rating and are in good condition. Using a CAT III 600V meter at a meter base, or any meter with cracked or unrated leads, removes the protection the rating describes. Verify on a known live source before and after testing a circuit you expect to be dead. Work on utility-side equipment belongs to qualified electricians; if you aren't one, call one.

Choose the Fluke 117 if…

  • ✓Your work stops at the panel: branch circuits, receptacles, lighting and controls
  • ✓You want VoltAlert non-contact detection and a LoZ check for ghost voltage in one meter
  • ✓You don't need microamps, temperature, 1000V or a CAT IV rating

Choose the Fluke 87V if…

  • ✓You measure at the service entrance, meter base or outdoor feeders, where CAT IV applies
  • ✓You troubleshoot variable-frequency drives and want the low-pass filter and 250 µs peak capture
  • ✓You need microamps for flame sensors, temperature, or 19,999-count resolution on DC

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 117 if you wire and troubleshoot buildings up to the panel: VoltAlert and LoZ solve everyday electrician problems and CAT III 600V covers the job. Buy the Fluke 87V if you work at the service entrance, above 600 V, on drives, or on anything that needs microamps, temperature or 0.05% DC accuracy. Many industrial electricians end up carrying both.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 87V more accurate than the Fluke 117?+

Yes, by a wide margin on DC. Fluke lists the 87V's basic DC volts accuracy at ±(0.05% + 1) and the 117's at ±(0.5% + 2), so the 87V is about ten times tighter. On AC volts the gap is smaller: ±(0.7% + 2) on the 87V against ±(1.0% + 3) on the 117 from 45 to 500 Hz.

Can the Fluke 117 be used on a service entrance?+

It isn't rated for it. The 117 is CAT III 600V, which covers distribution panels, feeders and branch circuits inside the building. The meter base, service drop and outdoor service conductors are CAT IV locations, where transients can carry more energy. For those, use a CAT IV 600V meter such as the Fluke 87V or Fluke 179.

Does the Fluke 87V have non-contact voltage detection?+

No. Fluke doesn't list VoltAlert or any non-contact sensor on the 87V; it is a feature of the 117 in the 110 Series. Most 87V owners carry a separate pen-style voltage detector for quick live checks, then confirm with the test leads before touching anything.

Can the Fluke 117 measure flame sensor microamps?+

No. Its lowest current range is 6.000 A with 1 mA resolution, and it has no mA or µA input. Furnace flame-sensor signals are usually a few microamps, so you need a meter with a µA range, such as the Fluke 87V, which resolves 0.01 µA DC, or the Fluke 116 in the same family as the 117.

Why is the Fluke 87V so much more expensive than the 117?+

It is a different class of instrument. You pay for the CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V rating, tenfold tighter DC accuracy, a 19,999-count mode, microamp and temperature ranges, a drive low-pass filter, peak capture and a lifetime warranty instead of three years. If none of that applies to your work, the 117 measures the rest just as reliably.

Which is better for electronics repair, the Fluke 117 or 87V?+

The 87V. Its 19,999-count mode resolves DC down to 10 µV, it reads duty cycle and frequency to 200 kHz, and its µA range measures standby current on a board. The 117 steps DC in 0.1 mV at best, stops at 50 kHz and has no current range finer than 1 mA, so it suits rail checks and fuse hunting rather than board-level fault finding.

Is the Fluke 117 or 87V better for automotive work?+

The 87V, if you diagnose rather than just check batteries. It adds duty cycle for injectors and PWM fans, 250 µs peak capture, temperature with the supplied 80BK probe, and mA/µA ranges for parasitic drain. Both carry 10 A (20 A for 30 s) and Min/Max. Neither lists RPM or dwell, so tach work needs an automotive meter.

Can the Fluke 117 measure parasitic battery drain?+

Roughly. On its 6 A range it reads in 1 mA steps, so a 50 mA drain shows as 0.050 A and you can see a module going to sleep. It can't resolve anything below a milliamp and has no µA range, so small-battery drains and fine module currents need a meter like the 87V. Wait for the vehicle's modules to go to sleep before you trust the reading.

Is the Fluke 87V worth it for a hobbyist or apprentice?+

Usually not as a first meter. Its value is CAT IV and 1000 V protection, calibration-grade DC accuracy, microamps, a drive filter and peak capture, which hobby and early apprentice work rarely needs. The 117 measures household circuits just as reliably for much less. Buy the 87V once your work moves into industrial, HVAC controls or instrumentation.
