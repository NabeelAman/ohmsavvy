OWON HDS2202S vs FNIRSI DSO152

OWON HDS2202S vs FNIRSI DSO152 is a match between two battery scopes you can carry, built for very different jobs. The OWON packs a two-channel 200 MHz, 1 GSa/s oscilloscope, a 20,000-count multimeter and a 25 MHz signal generator into one handheld. The FNIRSI is a single-channel 200kHz, 2.5 MSa/s pocket scope that weighs 100 g. The right one depends on how fast your signals are and how many tools you want in the bag.

Spec by spec

Put side by side, these two handhelds differ by two or three orders of magnitude on every speed figure, so the first three charts use a log scale. The fourth is a plain count of what you get in the box, because that is the other half of the decision: one is a scope, the other is a scope plus two more instruments.

The timebase chart explains the bandwidth gap in practical terms. At its fastest setting the DSO152 spreads 100µs across a typical 10-division screen, so one cycle of a 1 MHz clock would occupy a tenth of a division. The HDS2202S at 2 ns/div shows 20 ns across the screen, enough to look at the edge of a fast logic signal. At the slow end both reach long timebases (50 s/div on the FNIRSI, 1000 s/div on the OWON) for watching a battery voltage sag or a sensor drift. Our guide to oscilloscope bandwidth covers what the −3 dB rating means, and what GSa/s means covers sample rate.

What each one can and can't measure

A scope's rated bandwidth is the frequency where a sine wave reads about 3 dB low, around 70% of its true size. To see a signal's amplitude and shape with little distortion, the usual advice is a scope rated at three to five times the signal frequency, and more for square waves with sharp edges. That one rule separates these two handhelds cleanly.

FNIRSI DSO152: the low-frequency world

Divide 200kHz by three to five and the DSO152 is at home below roughly 40–65kHz. That covers the whole audio band, 50 or 60Hz mains-related ripple after a rectifier, the 1kHz square wave FNIRSI uses for probe calibration, hobby servo pulses and the slow PWM that dims an LED strip. Its AC coupling lets you strip off a DC level and look at the small wobble riding on it, and at 10mV/div that wobble can fill the screen. What it can't do is follow anything quick: a 1 MHz crystal, the edges of an I2C clock, or the switch node of a buck converter will be rounded off or missed. With one input, it also can't time one event against another.

OWON HDS2202S: a bench-class front end in your hand

At 200 MHz the HDS2202S is comfortable to roughly 40–65 MHz, which covers microcontroller clocks, the SPI and UART traffic of most hobby and industrial boards, motor-drive PWM and switching regulators. Two channels let you trigger on one line and watch another, and the generator lets you feed a filter a sine sweep or a square wave while CH2 shows the response. The limits are in the details: OWON lists an 8K record length, so long captures at high sample rates are short in time, and OWON lists edge triggering only, with no serial decoding. A 3.5-inch screen is larger than the FNIRSI's but small next to any bench scope.

Samples per cycle at each scope's own limit

Divide sample rate by bandwidth and you get the number of samples each scope takes per cycle at its rated ceiling. The DSO152 manages 12.5 (2.5 MSa/s ÷ 200kHz). The HDS2202S manages 5 (1 GSa/s ÷ 200 MHz), comfortably above the Nyquist minimum of 2 but thin enough that a 200 MHz sine leans on the scope's interpolation to look smooth. In everyday use, signals well below 200 MHz get far more samples per cycle, so this matters only near the top of the OWON's range.

One tool or three: what the extra instruments are worth

The HDS2202S's multimeter is a full 20,000-count meter by OWON's figures: voltage to 1000 V DC and 750 V AC, current to 10 A, plus resistance, capacitance, diode and continuity. If you would otherwise carry a separate meter, that removes one tool from the kit. The generator is the more unusual extra in a handheld: sine to 25 MHz, square, pulse and arbitrary waveforms to 5 MHz, from 20 mVpp to 5 Vpp into high impedance. That is enough to stand in for a missing sensor signal, clock a logic chip, or test an amplifier stage on the spot.

The DSO152 offers none of that, and doesn't try to. Its case is that it fits in a shirt pocket, starts showing a waveform after one press of AUTO, and costs little enough to leave in a toolbox. If you already own a decent multimeter and never need to inject a signal, the OWON's extras are worth little to you and the decision comes down to bandwidth and channels alone.

High voltage on handheld scopes

FNIRSI rates the DSO152 at ±40V with a x1 probe and ±400V with a x10 probe. That is a measurement range, not a safety category, so don't treat it as permission to probe mains. OWON gives the HDS2202S scope inputs a 400 V maximum (DC plus AC peak-to-peak at 1 MΩ) and rates its meter to 1000 V DC and 750 V AC. Neither maker's spec summary says the two OWON channels are isolated from each other, so assume their probe grounds are common. For mains work use the meter function or a differential probe, and keep a charging cable unplugged while measuring live circuits.

Choose the OWON HDS2202S if…

  • ✓You service equipment on site and want a scope, a meter and a signal source in one case
  • ✓You need to see two signals together, such as a clock and the data line it drives
  • ✓Your signals run into the megahertz range: PWM drives, switching regulators, microcontroller buses
  • ✓You want to inject a test tone or square wave into a circuit without carrying a separate generator

Choose the FNIRSI DSO152 if…

  • ✓You want the smallest, cheapest way to see a waveform, and one channel is enough
  • ✓Your signals are audio, sensor outputs, slow PWM or other low-frequency work
  • ✓You already own a multimeter and don't need a built-in generator
  • ✓You are learning what waveforms look like and want little at stake

Neither if…

You need decoded serial-bus bytes on screen rather than a waveform to read by eye: neither maker lists serial decoding, and the DSO152's 200kHz bandwidth rules out most bus work entirely — a scope with built-in I2C/SPI/UART decode is the right tool for that.

The bottom line

For anyone working on circuits faster than audio, the OWON HDS2202S is the better buy: 1,000 times the bandwidth, two channels, and a multimeter and 25 MHz generator in the same battery handheld. The FNIRSI DSO152 makes sense if your signals stay below roughly 40–65kHz and you want the smallest, cheapest scope that will show them, or a pocket spare alongside a better instrument.

Frequently asked questions

Is the OWON HDS2202S worth it over the FNIRSI DSO152?+

Yes, if you measure anything faster than audio or need two channels. The HDS2202S has 200 MHz against 200kHz, two inputs against one, and adds a 20,000-count multimeter and a 25 MHz generator. If you only look at slow, single signals and want the lowest cost and smallest size, the DSO152 does that job.

Can the FNIRSI DSO152 replace a multimeter like the one built into the HDS2202S?+

No. The DSO152 is an oscilloscope only, and FNIRSI lists no meter functions. It shows voltage against time on screen, but for resistance, continuity, capacitance or current you need a separate meter. The HDS2202S includes a 20,000-count meter rated to 1000 V DC, 750 V AC and 10 A per OWON.

Which has the better battery life?+

On the makers' figures they are close. OWON quotes 3 to 6 hours of continuous use from the HDS2202S's 18650 cell, and FNIRSI quotes about 4 hours from the DSO152's 1000mAh battery. Both charge over USB-C, and OWON says the HDS2202S can run from a power bank.

Can either handheld decode I2C or UART?+

Neither maker lists serial decoding. The DSO152's 200kHz bandwidth also rules out most bus work, since even a 100kHz I2C clock sits beyond its comfortable range. The HDS2202S has the bandwidth to show UART, I2C and SPI waveforms clearly on two channels, but you read the bits by eye rather than having them decoded.

What can I do with the HDS2202S signal generator?+

Feed a known signal into a circuit and watch what comes out. OWON lists sine to 25 MHz and square, pulse and arbitrary waveforms to 5 MHz, 20 mVpp to 5 Vpp. Typical uses are checking a filter's response, clocking a logic chip, or replacing a sensor signal while you troubleshoot the board that reads it.
