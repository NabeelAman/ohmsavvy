HANMATEK DOS1102 vs FNIRSI 1014D

HANMATEK DOS1102 vs FNIRSI 1014D pits two of the few 7-inch bench scopes that run from a 5 V supply instead of mains. Both have two channels at 100–110 MHz and an 800 × 480 screen. The DOS1102 is the better measuring tool: 30 automatic measurements, a 6-digit frequency counter, a stated 500 MS/s on each channel and PC control. The 1014D is the better stimulus tool: a built-in signal generator and a 100X probe in the kit. If you need to make signals, buy the FNIRSI; if you need to measure them more thoroughly, buy the HANMATEK.

Key differences

  • →Generator: the 1014D has one; the DOS1102 doesn't. HANMATEK sells the DOS1102S with a 10 MHz signal source for buyers who want one.
  • →Sampling with both channels on: HANMATEK's manual states 500 MS/s per channel. FNIRSI states 1 GSa/s but not the two-channel split.
  • →Measurements: 30 automatic measurements plus a 6-digit hardware frequency counter on the DOS1102, against 12 parameters on the 1014D.
  • →Bandwidth: 110 MHz against 100 MHz. On a scope this close, that 10% gap won't decide anything.
  • →Storage: the 1014D keeps 1000 waveforms and 1000 screenshots internally; the DOS1102 stores 16 waveform groups and saves to a USB stick.
  • →Timebase: the DOS1102 reaches 2 ns/div and 1000 s/div; the 1014D 10 ns/div and 50 s/div. The DOS1102 zooms further in and can sweep slower.
  • →Weight: about 1.1 kg for the DOS1102 against 3.46 lb (about 1.57 kg) on the 1014D listing.
  • →Neither carries our Hands-on Tested badge; both assessments are desk research from published specs.

Spec by spec

On the core scope specs these two are close. The charts show where they part: measurement tools, timebase reach and weight.

Both are 7-inch, 800 × 480 and both run from 5 V, which keeps them light and fanless next to the mains bench scopes they compete with. HANMATEK doesn't state the DOS1102's ADC resolution; FNIRSI lists 8-bit for the 1014D.

HANMATEK DOS1102 vs FNIRSI 1014D with both channels running

Most two-channel scopes share one converter, so turning on the second channel halves the rate each one gets. HANMATEK says so plainly: 500 MS/s per channel, 1 GS/s combined. FNIRSI quotes 1 GSa/s and stops there. If the 1014D follows the usual pattern, it also drops to 500 MSa/s per channel, but FNIRSI doesn't confirm it.

At 500 MS/s each channel gets a sample every 2 ns. That is ten samples per cycle at 50 MHz and about 4.5 at the DOS1102's full 110 MHz, so the two-channel shape holds up well into the tens of MHz. For comparing an input and an output, or a clock and data line, the stated figure is what you want to know before buying.

Measurement depth on the DOS1102

The 6-digit hardware counter, rated 2 Hz to 20 MHz on the Amazon listing, reads a crystal or a PWM frequency to a resolution the on-screen period measurement can't match. Current scaling from 100.0 mA/V to 1 kA/V means a clamp probe's output shows directly in amps. Probe multipliers run to 1000X, and SCPI plus LabVIEW support let a PC pull readings for logging. None of that matters for a first look at a waveform; it matters a lot for repair and production checks where you write the numbers down.

Stimulus on the 1014D

The 1014D's generator makes 14 standard waveforms plus a custom shape, sine to 10 MHz and the rest to 2 MHz, at 2.5 Vpp. Feed it into an amplifier, a filter or a logic input and watch the result on the other channel. The external trigger input (0–40 V) is also handy when a separate sync pulse marks the event you want. The FNIRSI 1014D vs OWON SDS1102 page works through what the generator saves you in more detail.

The third option: the DOS1102S

HANMATEK's site lists the DOS1102S, which adds a 10 MHz signal source to the same scope. It isn't one of our checked picks, so we haven't confirmed its Amazon availability. If you like the DOS1102's measurement set but want a generator, it is worth looking up before settling on the 1014D.

What both can and can't do

With the three-to-five-times rule, both show signals up to about 20–35 MHz at close to true size: audio, switching supplies, PWM, I2C, UART and SPI clocks of a few MHz. Neither decodes serial buses; HANMATEK's FAQ says the DOS1102 doesn't, and FNIRSI doesn't list it. Both have short memory (10K points against an unclear "240Kbit"), so long single-shot captures lose time resolution. For decoding and deep memory on a budget, see HANMATEK DOS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10 and Hantek DSO2C10 vs FNIRSI 1014D.

Low-voltage power doesn't mean isolated

A 5 V adapter makes these light, but once the scope is connected to a grounded PC, USB stick host or adapter with an earth pin, the probe ground clips can still be tied to earth. Never clip a ground lead to a mains-live point. HANMATEK's 400 V and FNIRSI's 400 V (10X) are input limits, not CAT safety ratings. Use a differential probe for line voltage, and call an electrician for anything on the mains side you are unsure of.

The trust question: whose numbers are cleaner?

Neither is spotless. HANMATEK's manual is clear, but its product-page FAQ carries copied figures (250 MSa/s, 12,000 points, 3.5-inch) that belong to another model, and the Amazon title says "500 MS/s *2" while the front badge says 1GSa/s. Read the manual and they agree: 500 MS/s on each channel. FNIRSI's spec table is tidy but leaves out the two numbers a two-channel buyer most needs, the split sample rate and points per channel. On transparency, the DOS1102 edges it.

Choose the HANMATEK DOS1102 if…

  • ✓You want 30 automatic measurements and a 6-digit hardware frequency counter
  • ✓You want the two-channel sample rate stated plainly (500 MS/s each)
  • ✓You plan to log or control the scope from a PC with SCPI or LabVIEW
  • ✓You use a current clamp and want amps-per-volt scaling built in

Choose the FNIRSI 1014D if…

  • ✓You need a signal source and don't own a function generator
  • ✓You want to keep many captures: 1000 waveforms and 1000 screenshots on board
  • ✓You want an external trigger input
  • ✓You want a P4100 100X probe included for higher-voltage work

Neither if…

You need to decode I2C, UART or SPI, or capture long single events at full speed. Both have short memory and no serial decoding; a scope like the Hantek DSO2C10 covers that.

The bottom line

Choose the HANMATEK DOS1102 if you measure more than you stimulate: 30 automatic measurements, a 6-digit counter, a clearly stated 500 MS/s per channel, PC control and about 1.1 kg. Choose the FNIRSI 1014D if you need a signal source on the same bench: its DDS generator and 100X probe make it the more complete starter kit.

Frequently asked questions

Does the HANMATEK DOS1102 have a signal generator?+

No. HANMATEK lists a 10 MHz signal source only on the DOS1102S variant. The FNIRSI 1014D has a DDS generator built in, with sine output to 10 MHz, other standard waveforms to 2 MHz and a custom shape to 1 MHz, at 2.5 Vpp.

Is the DOS1102 1 GSa/s or 500 MS/s?+

Both, depending on how you count. HANMATEK's manual gives 500 MS/s per channel, 1 GS/s combined, which matches the Amazon title's "500 MS/s *2" and the front-panel 1GSa/s badge. With both channels on, each one samples at 500 MS/s.

Can both scopes run without mains power?+

Both run from a 5 V supply rather than a mains lead: the DOS1102 from 5 V 2 A, the 1014D from a 5 V adapter rated 2, 3 or 4 A. Neither has a built-in battery, and neither maker says a USB power bank is supported, so plan on the supplied adapter.

Which one has more automatic measurements?+

The HANMATEK DOS1102, with 30 automatic measurements and a customizable measurement menu, plus a 6-digit hardware frequency counter rated 2 Hz to 20 MHz on its Amazon listing. FNIRSI lists 12 automatic parameters for the 1014D, along with cursors, FFT and X-Y mode.

Which is better for a beginner?+

Usually the FNIRSI 1014D, because a beginner rarely owns a function generator and the built-in one lets you practice on known signals. Pick the DOS1102 if you are learning on real repair work, where measurements, a frequency counter and current-probe scaling get used more than a test signal.
