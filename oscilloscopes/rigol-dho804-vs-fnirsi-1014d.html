Rigol DHO804 vs FNIRSI 1014D for Beginners

Rigol DHO804 vs FNIRSI 1014D for beginners is a question of what you want your first scope to teach you. The Rigol is a 70 MHz, four-channel, 12-bit instrument with a 1024 × 600 touchscreen, deep memory and standard serial decoding, but no signal source. The FNIRSI is a 100 MHz, two-channel, 8-bit scope with a built-in DDS generator and loosely documented memory.

Spec by spec

Read the headline numbers alone and the FNIRSI looks like the faster scope. Read the rest of the sheet and the Rigol pulls ahead on channels, resolution and documented memory. The four charts below use only the figures each maker publishes, as recorded in our beginner roundup.

On one channel the two converters are evenly matched for their bandwidths: the DHO804 takes about 18 samples per cycle of a 70 MHz sine and the 1014D 10 per cycle of a 100 MHz sine, both well clear of the Nyquist minimum of two. The gap is in what happens next. Rigol tells you each of four channels gets 312.5 MSa/s; FNIRSI doesn't say what each of its two gets. If samples per second is a new idea, start with what GSa/s means.

Rigol DHO804 vs FNIRSI 1014D for beginners

A first oscilloscope has two jobs. It has to show your own circuits clearly enough that you trust what you see, and it has to help you learn what a correct waveform looks like. These two scopes split those jobs between them.

Learning with known signals: the 1014D's advantage

The fastest way to learn triggering, timebase and volts-per-division is to feed the scope a signal whose shape and frequency you already know. The 1014D makes its own: connect the generator output to channel 1, pick a sine, square or one of FNIRSI's standard waveforms (its spec table and Amazon listing say 14; another part of FNIRSI's page says 12), and practice measuring it. Run the same signal through a simple RC filter to channel 2 and you can see phase shift and attenuation on one screen. A DHO804 owner has to borrow those signals from a microcontroller pin or buy a separate generator.

Seeing real projects clearly: the DHO804's advantage

Once you are debugging your own boards, the bottleneck moves. A typical beginner fault, such as a sensor that answers on I2C only some of the time, involves a clock, a data line and often a supply rail. Four channels show all of them on one trigger, and the DHO804 decodes UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and parallel traffic as standard, so you read bytes rather than counting edges. Our data for the 1014D doesn't list serial decoding. The 25 Mpt memory lets you capture a long burst at a fast timebase and zoom in afterwards; with the 1014D's short "240Kbit" figure you will more often have to choose between a long window and fine detail.

Ease of use

Both have 7-inch screens. The Rigol's is a capacitive touchscreen at 1024 × 600, and it adds HDMI out for a larger monitor, which helps if you are following a course or sharing a bench. The FNIRSI's 800 × 480 panel is the same size at lower resolution. The DHO804 runs from USB-C, so it can sit on a portable power source; the 1014D runs from a 5 V adapter.

What 12 bits buys a beginner

On an 8-bit scope a signal filling one division at a sensitive setting is drawn from about 32 of the 256 steps, so small ripple can look like a staircase. The DHO804 has sixteen times as many steps for the same screen height. You notice it most on power-supply ripple, audio and slow sensor signals, exactly the things many first projects involve.

What each one can and can't measure

A scope's rated bandwidth marks where a sine input has already dropped by 3 dB, so readings are only trustworthy well below it. Applying the usual three-to-five-times margin, the DHO804 handles signals up to roughly 14–23 MHz and the 1014D up to roughly 20–33 MHz. Everything a beginner usually builds sits inside both ranges: Arduino and ESP32 PWM, UART at common baud rates, 100 kHz and 400 kHz I2C, hobby-speed SPI, audio amplifiers and the switching frequency of most small buck converters. Our explainer on oscilloscope bandwidth walks through the margin in detail.

The 1014D's extra bandwidth shows up on fast edges. Using the common 0.35 ÷ bandwidth estimate, the fastest rise time each can display is about 5 ns for the DHO804 and 3.5 ns for the 1014D. That matters when you are judging overshoot on a fast logic edge; it rarely matters for a 1 kHz PWM signal whose edges you only need to time.

Mains and high voltage

The 1014D kit's 100X probe extends the voltage you can display, but its ground clip still connects to the scope's ground, which on a mains-powered bench setup normally means earth. Neither scope's input or isolation rating is in our verified data. Never clip a ground lead onto mains or the primary side of a supply; use a differential probe for that work.

Choose the Rigol DHO804 if…

  • ✓You want to learn on a scope with fully published specs, including memory and per-channel sample rate
  • ✓Your projects involve several signals at once, such as an SPI bus or a set of supply rails
  • ✓You want serial decoding of UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and parallel buses from day one
  • ✓Fine vertical detail matters: ripple, noise, small sensor outputs

Choose the FNIRSI 1014D if…

  • ✓You have no signal generator and want known test signals to practice on
  • ✓Two channels cover what you need to see at once
  • ✓You want a kit that already includes a 100X high-voltage probe alongside two 1X/10X probes
  • ✓You value 100 MHz of rated bandwidth over channel count and vertical resolution

The bottom line

For most beginners who will move quickly from practice signals to real projects, the Rigol DHO804 is the better first scope: four channels, 12-bit resolution, published deep memory, standard serial decoding and a sharper touchscreen. The FNIRSI 1014D is the better buy if you own no other test gear and want a built-in signal source to learn on, and two channels cover your work.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Rigol DHO804 or the FNIRSI 1014D better for a complete beginner?+

It depends on what else you own. With no signal source at all, the 1014D's built-in generator gives you known waveforms to practice triggering and measurement on. If you already have a microcontroller or generator to supply signals, the DHO804's four channels, 12-bit resolution and serial decoding will serve you longer.

Does the FNIRSI 1014D's 100 MHz make it faster than the Rigol DHO804?+

Only in rated bandwidth. The 1014D is rated 100 MHz against 70 MHz, so it shows fast edges a little more faithfully. The DHO804 has the higher single-channel sample rate (1.25 GSa/s against 1 GSa/s), publishes its four-channel rate, and has far deeper documented memory.

Can the Rigol DHO804 generate test signals like the FNIRSI 1014D?+

No. Rigol lists no built-in signal generator or logic channels for the DHO804. The 1014D has a single-channel DDS generator with sine up to 10 MHz and other waveforms up to 2 MHz at 2.5 Vpp. DHO804 owners would add a separate generator or use a microcontroller pin.

How much memory does the FNIRSI 1014D have?+

FNIRSI quotes it only as 240Kbit and doesn't give a per-channel point count, so it can't be compared directly with the DHO804's published 25 Mpts on one channel and 5 Mpts with four. In practice, expect the 1014D to hold shorter captures at fast timebases.

Is 8-bit resolution on the 1014D a problem for learning?+

Not for learning waveform basics: square waves, PWM, clocks and audio signals look fine at 256 levels. It shows up when you zoom into small details such as millivolt ripple on a supply rail, where the DHO804's 12-bit converter draws a smoother, more readable trace.
