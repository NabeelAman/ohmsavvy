Why Does My Multimeter Not Measure Current?

A multimeter not measuring current is almost always set up wrong or protected by a blown fuse: the red lead is still in the V/Ω jack, the meter is connected across the circuit instead of in series with it, or an earlier mistake has blown the mA or A fuse so the meter reads zero on every attempt. Less often the setting is wrong (DC selected on an AC circuit, or an amps range too coarse to show a few milliamps), or the meter simply has no current function at all. Work through the checks below in order and you'll find which one in a few minutes, usually without buying anything.

Safety: current measurements are the risky ones

  • Switch the circuit off before opening it to insert the meter, and before moving the leads. Never touch the probes across a voltage source while the red lead is in an A or mA jack.
  • Respect the jack ratings printed by the input and in the manual. Many 10 A inputs are rated for limited on-time, and an unfused or underrated input can overheat. Car batteries and battery banks can deliver hundreds of amps into a mistake.
  • Don't break into household mains circuits to measure current with test leads. Use a clamp meter on a single conductor, or call a licensed electrician for anything inside a panel.

How a meter measures current

A multimeter can't measure current at a distance. On a current setting, it routes everything entering the A or mA jack through a small internal shunt resistor and back out of COM, and measures the tiny voltage across that shunt. For that to work, the current you want to measure has to flow through the meter, which means the meter must be part of the circuit's single path: in series. A fuse sits in that path to protect the shunt and the meter if too much current tries to flow.

Every way a meter fails to read current breaks one link in that chain. The current goes somewhere else (wrong jack, wrong connection), the path is open (blown fuse), the meter is looking for the wrong kind of current (AC vs DC), or the amount is too small for the selected range to show. Once you think of it that way, the checklist almost writes itself.

Series, not parallel

The single biggest mix-up is treating current like voltage. Voltage is measured across a part, with the meter in parallel; current is measured through it, with the meter in series. Take a 12 V battery feeding a 6 Ω lamp: the lamp draws 2 A (12 ÷ 6). Open the circuit at one point, put the red lead on the battery side of the gap and the black lead on the lamp side, and all 2 A now passes through the meter, which reads 2.00 A while the lamp stays lit.

Put the same meter across the lamp instead and it becomes a short circuit across the battery, because a current range has a resistance of only a fraction of an ohm. The current is limited only by the battery, the leads and the fuse, so the fuse blows almost instantly. That's why "my meter stopped reading current" so often follows "I tried to measure the current of a battery." The arithmetic of that short, and the jack ratings involved, are in why a multimeter fuse blows when measuring current.

Current has to pass through the meter

If you haven't opened the circuit and made the meter the only path between the two loose ends, it isn't measuring the load's current. Every correct current reading starts with a break in the circuit.

1Check the jacks

Most meters have three or four input jacks: COM, V/Ω, and one or two current jacks, often labeled A (or 10A) and mA/µA. With the dial set to amps but the red lead still in V/Ω from a voltage test, the meter's high-impedance voltage input sits in series with the circuit. Almost no current can flow, the load stops working, and the display shows zero. Move the red lead to the current jack that matches the dial position: A for the amps range, mA/µA for the small ranges.

Some meters help here. Klein's Lead-Alert, on models such as the MM460S, gives visual and audible warnings when the leads don't match the selected function, and the Fluke 87V sounds an Input Alert when the leads are in the wrong jacks for the function. If yours doesn't, make it a habit to check the jacks every time you change function.

2Break the circuit and wire in series

With the power off, open the circuit at a convenient point: pull a fuse and probe its two clips, lift one wire off a terminal, or remove a battery link. Connect the red lead to the side coming from the supply and the black lead to the side going to the load, then switch on. If the load runs normally and the meter shows a sensible value, the setup is right.

If you can't open the circuit, a meter in series isn't an option; use a clamp meter or measure the voltage across a known resistor and divide (I = V ÷ R). Those methods are compared in measuring current without breaking the circuit. For a full walkthrough on a small circuit, see how to measure milliamps.

3Test the current fuses

If the setup is right and the reading is still zero, suspect the fuse. Meters with separate A and mA jacks usually have a separate fuse for each, so the mA range can be dead while the A range still works, or the other way round. You can check without opening the case: set the meter to ohms with the red lead in V/Ω, and touch the other end of that lead to the inside of the current jack. A good fuse and shunt read a low resistance; OL means the fuse is open. Some meters display a fuse warning on their own.

Replace a blown fuse only with the exact current, voltage and breaking-capacity rating the maker specifies. The Fluke 117's A input, for example, uses an 11 A, 1000 V fast-acting fuse. A cheap glass fuse may fit the clips but can't safely interrupt a high-energy fault. Then work out what blew it before measuring again, or it will go the same way.

4Match AC or DC

A DC current setting averages the current over time. On an AC circuit, the current flows one way for half of each cycle and the other way for the rest, so the average is close to zero and the meter shows almost nothing, even with amps flowing. The reverse mistake, AC current on a DC circuit, also gives a meaningless or near-zero reading. Select the symbol that matches the circuit (A with a straight line for DC, A with a wave for AC) and check the display annunciator. Some meters toggle AC/DC with a button on a shared dial position, so the dial alone doesn't tell you. Budget meters sometimes can't measure AC current at all; the Klein MM325, for example, has DC current ranges only.

5Pick a range that can see it

A meter on its amps range can't show what it can't resolve. The Fluke 117 measures current on 6.000 A and 10.00 A ranges; its best resolution is 1 mA. A 10 mA indicator LED therefore shows as 0.010 A, a 1 mA load as a single count, and a 50 µA sleep current as 0.000 A. That isn't a fault, just the wrong tool for the job. Meters with mA and µA ranges resolve far smaller currents; the Fluke 87V's µA range resolves 0.01 µA DC, as do the UNI-T UT61E+ and Brymen BM786 on their lowest ranges.

6Make sure the meter has current ranges

Some meters are deliberately built without current. The Fluke 113 and Fluke 114 are voltage, resistance and continuity meters for electrical work and have no current ranges at all, and the Southwire 16030A is similar. If your dial has no A, mA or µA position and there's no current jack, the meter can't measure current with test leads, whatever you try. Other meters stop at milliamps: the KAIWEETS KM602, for instance, has 600 mA, 6 A and 10 A ranges but no µA. Check the specification sheet before you blame the setup; our Fluke 113 vs Fluke 117 comparison shows the difference between a current-free meter and one with an amps input.

7Clamp meters: one conductor only

A clamp meter measures the magnetic field around what's inside its jaws. Close them around a whole appliance cord and the hot and neutral carry the same current in opposite directions, so their fields cancel and the meter reads near zero. A 1,500 W heater on 120 V draws 12.5 A (1,500 ÷ 120): clamp the hot wire alone and you'll see that; clamp the cord and you'll see almost nothing. A line splitter separates the conductors so you can clamp one without stripping the cord.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my multimeter read 0 amps?

The usual causes are a blown current fuse, the red lead left in the V/Ω jack, or the meter connected across the load instead of in series. Less often, the current is smaller than the range can resolve, or DC is selected on an AC circuit. Check the jacks first, then test the fuse with the meter's ohms function before replacing anything.

How do I know if my multimeter's amp fuse is blown?

Set the meter to ohms, put the red lead in V/Ω and touch the other end to the inside of the A or mA jack. A good fuse reads a low resistance, typically from a fraction of an ohm up to a few ohms depending on the fuse and shunt; OL means it's open. Some meters show a fuse warning instead. Replace it only with the exact type in the manual.

Can I measure current by putting the probes across the battery?

No. On a current setting the meter's input is close to a short circuit, so placing it across a battery or supply forces a large current through the meter. At best it blows the fuse; with a large battery or mains it can damage the meter or cause an arc flash. Current is measured by opening the circuit and placing the meter in the gap.

Why does my clamp meter read zero on an extension cord?

A clamp measures the net magnetic field around what's inside its jaws. A cord carries the same current out on the hot wire and back on the neutral, so the two fields cancel and the reading is close to zero. Clamp around a single conductor, or use a line splitter that separates the hot and neutral. A clamp that measures only AC current won't read DC circuits at all.

Why can't my multimeter measure milliamps?

Some meters only have an amps range. The Fluke 117, for instance, measures current on 6 A and 10 A ranges with a best resolution of 1 mA, so a few milliamps show as only a digit or two, and microamps don't register. For small currents use a meter with mA and µA ranges and put the red lead in the matching jack.
