HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test a Throttle Position Sensor With a Multimeter

To test a throttle position sensor (TPS) with a multimeter, confirm about 5 V on the reference wire and a clean ground with the key on, then back-probe the signal wire and slowly open the throttle: the voltage should rise smoothly from typically under 1 V at closed throttle to around 4 to 4.5 V at wide open, with no drops or jumps. A sweep with a dead spot is the classic sign of a worn sensor track. This guide covers the common three-wire potentiometer TPS on cable-throttle engines, with notes on the dual-sensor units in electronic throttle bodies, and what the readings mean for each trouble code.

Work safely around the throttle body

  • Do the tests with the engine off and the key on. Keep fingers out of the throttle bore, and on electronic throttle bodies never hold the plate open by hand with the key on: the motor can snap it closed.
  • Back-probe from the rear of the connector with thin pins, and don't pierce the wire insulation, which invites corrosion inside the harness.
  • Never short the 5 V reference to ground with a probe. On many vehicles the same reference feeds several sensors, and a short can set multiple codes or affect how the engine runs.
  • If the car goes into limp mode or the throttle sticks open while driving, get it safely off the road and have it towed rather than driving on.

How a throttle position sensor works

A conventional TPS is a rotary potentiometer bolted to the end of the throttle shaft. The engine computer puts a regulated 5 V across a resistive track, and a wiper attached to the shaft slides along it as the throttle opens. The wiper's voltage is the signal: low at closed throttle, high at wide open. Because the computer measures volts, not ohms, a crack, worn patch or bit of dirt on the track shows up as a dip or spike in an otherwise smooth line.

The computer uses the signal to add fuel on sudden acceleration, set idle, cut fuel on deceleration and, on many automatics, decide shift points and firmness. So a faulty TPS can feel like a fuel, ignition or transmission problem. The same track-and-wiper physics is explained on a bench part in how to check a potentiometer wiper.

1Identify the three wires

Find the sensor on the side of the throttle body, opposite the throttle linkage. The service manual wiring diagram names the 5 V reference, the sensor ground (sometimes called low reference or signal return) and the signal. Without a diagram you can identify them by measurement: with the key on, the wire showing about 5 V is the reference, the one near 0 V is ground, and the one in between that changes when the throttle moves is the signal.

2Check the 5 V reference

Set the meter to DC volts. Key on, engine off, black lead on a clean engine or battery ground, and back-probe the reference wire. You should see close to 5 V; many manuals accept roughly 4.5 to 5.5 V. If the reference is missing, unplug the TPS and check again. If 5 V returns, the sensor itself was shorting it. If it stays at zero with the sensor unplugged, the wiring or another sensor sharing the same reference is pulling it down.

3Check the sensor ground

A bad ground is easy to miss because the sensor still produces a signal, just shifted upward. Put the red lead on the TPS ground wire and the black lead on the battery negative post, key on. You are measuring voltage drop along the ground path, and it should be very small, typically under about 0.1 V. A few tenths of a volt means corrosion or a loose ground somewhere in the circuit. The method is the same one used in how to check voltage drop on a car.

4Read the closed-throttle signal

Move the red lead to the signal wire and leave the throttle fully closed. Most sensors read typically between about 0.5 and 1 V here. Compare with the service manual figure if it gives one. A reading of exactly 0 V or 5 V points to wiring rather than a worn sensor. On older adjustable sensors, this is the value you set before tightening the mounting screws.

5Sweep the throttle slowly

This is the real test. Open the throttle very slowly by hand at the linkage, taking several seconds to go from closed to wide open, then let it return just as slowly. The voltage should climb steadily with no pauses, dips or jumps, reaching around 4 to 4.5 V at wide open. A digital display updates only a few times a second, so watch the analog bar graph if your meter has one, or use min/max recording: a minimum that falls well below your closed-throttle reading during the sweep means the signal dropped out somewhere.

Tap the sensor body lightly with a screwdriver handle while holding the throttle at a steady part-open position. A good sensor holds steady. A reading that jumps when tapped points to an intermittent track or a loose connector pin; see finding intermittent electrical faults.

6Optional: check resistance with the sensor unplugged

With the connector unplugged, measure ohms between the sensor's reference and ground pins. That is the full track and should not change as the throttle moves. Then measure from the signal pin to ground and sweep slowly; the resistance should change smoothly. Makers rarely publish these resistance values today, and the voltage sweep is the better test, but an open track or a reading that jumps to OL mid-sweep confirms a bad sensor.

The sweep matters more than the endpoints

A worn TPS usually reads fine at closed and wide-open throttle. The fault is a small dead spot in between, so open the throttle slowly and watch for the signal to stumble.

Expected signal through the sweep

The chart shows an illustrative sensor with a 0.6 V closed-throttle reading and a 4.4 V wide-open reading. Between those points the voltage should rise roughly in step with throttle opening. Your sensor's endpoints will differ a little, but the shape should not.

The arithmetic is simple: from 0.6 to 4.4 V is a 3.8 V span, so each 25% of throttle travel should add about 0.95 V. Spot-check that by holding the throttle at roughly half open, where the signal should sit near the middle of your own sensor's span.

Electronic throttle bodies (drive-by-wire)

Most cars built in the last two decades use an electronic throttle body, where a motor opens the throttle plate and the accelerator pedal has its own position sensors. The throttle body usually carries two position signals, often called TPS A and TPS B, which the computer cross-checks. Depending on the maker they may both rise, rise at different rates, or one may rise while the other falls. A disagreement sets P2135 and usually limp mode. You can still back-probe each signal with a meter while an assistant presses the pedal with the key on, but you need the service manual's expected values, and a scan tool showing both percentages side by side is the easier way to see a mismatch.

Which multimeter to use for a TPS

You need DC volts with good resolution near 5 V, which every meter here has. What separates them for this job is how quickly the display reacts during the sweep. Our automotive multimeter guide covers the rest of the car.

Frequently asked questions

What voltage should a throttle position sensor read?

On most three-wire sensors, the reference wire carries about 5 V, the ground reads near 0 V, and the signal sits typically between about 0.5 and 1 V at closed throttle, rising smoothly to around 4 to 4.5 V at wide open. The exact closed-throttle figure varies by maker, so compare against the service manual for your vehicle.

Can you test a throttle position sensor without a scan tool?

Yes. A multimeter on DC volts, back-probed on the signal wire with the key on and engine off, shows the sensor's output directly. Open the throttle slowly by hand and watch for a smooth rise. A scan tool adds the computer's view of the percentage, which helps on electronic throttle bodies, but the meter alone finds most worn sensors.

What are the symptoms of a bad throttle position sensor?

Hesitation or stumbling when you press the pedal, surging at steady speed, a high or hunting idle, harsh or erratic automatic shifts, and a check engine light with codes in the P0120 to P0124 range. Electronic throttle cars may go into reduced-power limp mode. Many of these symptoms overlap with vacuum leaks, so test before replacing.

Do you test a TPS with the engine running?

No, the standard test is key on, engine off. That lets you open the throttle by hand through its full range safely, and the 5 V reference is live without the engine turning. On drive-by-wire throttle bodies, don't push the throttle plate open by hand; use the accelerator pedal with the key on, as the maker's procedure describes.

Can a TPS read correctly at idle and still be bad?

Yes, and it is the most common failure. The wiper wears a groove in the track at the positions it spends most time, just off idle and at cruise. The closed and wide-open readings can look perfect while the signal drops out over a small part of the sweep. That is why a slow sweep with a bar graph matters more than the endpoint numbers.

Does a new throttle position sensor need to be adjusted?

Some older sensors with slotted mounting holes must be set to a specified closed-throttle voltage before tightening the screws. Many later sensors are fixed, and the computer learns the closed position on its own. Electronic throttle bodies often need a relearn procedure. Check the service information for your vehicle before installing a replacement.
