OWON VDS1022I vs Hantek 6022BE

OWON VDS1022I vs Hantek 6022BE is the cheapest end of the PC oscilloscope market: two screenless USB boxes, each with two 8-bit channels and no power supply beyond the USB cable. The OWON offers 25 MHz, 100 MSa/s, five trigger types and an isolated USB link. The Hantek is the lower-cost option at 20 MHz and 48 MS/s, with an edge trigger, DC coupling only and Windows software.

Spec by spec

On paper these two look almost the same: two channels, 8 bits, USB power, no screen. The numbers that separate them are sample rate and sensitivity rather than bandwidth. All figures below come from OWON's VDS series page and datasheet and Hantek's 6022BE spec table; where a maker publishes no figure we leave it out of the charts.

The third chart is the one to dwell on. At 48 MS/s, a 20 MHz sine gets only 2.4 samples per cycle on the Hantek: enough in theory to tell you a frequency is there, far too few to show its shape without heavy interpolation. In practice the 6022BE's sample rate, not its 20 MHz front end, sets the useful ceiling. A common working target is ten or more samples per cycle, which puts that ceiling near 5 MHz on the Hantek and near 10 MHz on the OWON for sine waves, and lower again for square waves, whose edges contain higher harmonics. Background on both figures: what oscilloscope bandwidth is and what GSa/s means.

What each USB scope can and can't measure

By the usual 3–5× bandwidth rule, a 25 MHz scope shows signals up to about 5–8 MHz with reasonable fidelity and a 20 MHz scope up to about 4–7 MHz. Combine that with the sample-rate limit above and the realistic territory for both is the low-megahertz range and below. That still covers a lot of hobby electronics.

Comfortable on both

Audio amplifiers and filters, 555 timer outputs, hobby servo pulses (1–2 ms wide at 50 Hz), LED and motor PWM in the kilohertz range, sensor outputs, and UART at 9600 or 115200 baud all sit far below either scope's limit. With two channels you can put an op-amp's input and output side by side or watch a clock against its data line. Neither maker lists serial decoding, so you read UART or I2C bits by eye and with cursors rather than as decoded text.

Where the OWON pulls ahead

Small signals are the clearest case. Ripple of a few tens of millivolts, a microphone preamp output or a thermocouple amplifier stage fills only a sliver of the screen at 20 mV/div, while 5 mV/div gives four times the vertical detail from the same 8-bit converter. The trigger list matters too: a pulse trigger can catch a single pulse narrower than expected, a slope trigger can fire on a slow ramp, and a video trigger locks onto composite video lines. On the Hantek, an edge trigger is the only tool, so a rare glitch has to be found by watching repeated sweeps.

What the Hantek's DC-only input means

Hantek lists DC coupling only. AC coupling normally strips a steady DC level so you can zoom in on what rides on it, such as ripple on a 12 V rail. On the 6022BE that 12 V stays in the picture, and at 20 mV/div a 12 V offset is far off screen, so ripple checks on a supply rail need a workaround such as an external DC-blocking capacitor in series with the probe tip. For logic levels, PWM and audio centred near ground, DC coupling is no handicap.

Neither scope is fast enough for the edges of modern logic

Using the common 0.35 ÷ bandwidth approximation, a 20 MHz front end has a rise time of about 17.5 ns; OWON quotes 14 ns for the VDS1022I. Logic edges from fast microcontroller pins can be quicker than either, so both will show those edges slower and rounder than they really are. If you need to judge ringing or edge timing on SPI or fast GPIO, a 50 MHz or faster scope is the better tool.

Where the ground clip really connects

The isolation line on OWON's spec sheet is easy to skip, but it changes what the ground clip is tied to. On a non-isolated USB scope the clip, the BNC shells, the USB cable shield and the computer's chassis are one conductor. If the circuit under test is also referenced to earth, through a bench supply or a mains-powered device, clipping ground to anything other than that circuit's own 0 V creates a short through the scope and the USB cable. A smaller version of the same loop shows up as hum and noise on low-level signals, which is where the OWON's 5 mV/div range and its isolated link work together.

USB scopes, grounds and mains

A USB scope's probe ground connects to the computer unless the link is isolated. Hantek lists no isolation for the 6022BE, so its ground clip should be treated as your laptop's ground, and on a desktop PC that is usually mains earth. OWON specifies an isolated USB link on the VDS1022I, which helps with ground loops, but its isolation voltage rating isn't in the data we verified. Don't clip either ground lead to mains or other high-voltage circuits; use a rated differential probe.

Choose the OWON VDS1022I if…

  • ✓You want pulse, slope, video or alternate triggering, not just a rising or falling edge
  • ✓You measure small signals: its most sensitive range is 5 mV/div against the Hantek's 20 mV/div
  • ✓You want the USB link to your laptop isolated from the probe ground
  • ✓You want roughly twice the sample rate for cleaner traces near 5 MHz

Choose the Hantek 6022BE if…

  • ✓Spending as little as possible matters more than features
  • ✓You use a Windows PC and mainly want to learn what a scope shows
  • ✓Your signals are audio, timers, servo pulses and slow PWM, well under a few MHz
  • ✓An edge trigger, X-Y mode and FFT cover what you plan to do

Neither if…

You need to judge ringing or edge timing on fast SPI or GPIO signals: by the usual rise-time approximation, a 20 MHz front end rounds edges faster than many microcontroller pins actually switch, so both show those edges slower and rounder than they really are — a 50 MHz or faster scope is the better tool for that.

The bottom line

The OWON VDS1022I is the better instrument: twice the sample rate, a 5 mV/div range for small signals, five trigger types and an isolated USB link, in a smaller box. The Hantek 6022BE earns its place only on price. If you want the cheapest way to learn on a Windows PC with slow signals and an edge trigger is enough, it does that job; for anything you need to trigger on or measure precisely, spend the extra on the OWON.

Frequently asked questions

Is the OWON VDS1022I better than the Hantek 6022BE?+

For most uses, yes. OWON lists 100 MSa/s against Hantek's 48 MS/s, 25 MHz against 20 MHz, pulse, slope, video and alternate triggers on top of edge, a 5 mV/div range and an isolated USB link. The Hantek's one clear advantage is its very low price.

Does the Hantek 6022BE work on a Mac?+

Hantek lists only Windows versions (10, 8, 7, Vista and XP) for the 6022BE software, so plan on a Windows PC. OWON's VDS product page offers PC software for the VDS1022I but doesn't name the operating systems it supports, so check the download page for your system before buying either.

Can the Hantek 6022BE measure ripple on a power supply?+

Not easily. Hantek lists DC coupling only, so a supply's DC level stays on screen along with the ripple, and the most sensitive setting is 20 mV/div. Small ripple on a 5 V or 12 V rail needs an external DC-blocking capacitor in series with the probe. The OWON reaches 5 mV/div, though OWON doesn't state its coupling options on the page we checked.

How much memory does the Hantek 6022BE have?+

Hantek's spec table doesn't clearly assign a memory figure to the 6022BE, so we don't quote one. The OWON VDS1022I's record length is stated: 5K points, which at 100 MSa/s fills in 50 microseconds. Slower timebases spread those points over a longer window at a lower effective sample rate.

Which USB scope is safer to use with a laptop?+

The OWON, because OWON specifies an isolated USB 2.0 link, which keeps the probe ground off the laptop's ground. Hantek lists no isolation for the 6022BE. Neither isolation feature makes a scope suitable for mains: OWON's isolation voltage rating isn't in our verified data, so use a differential probe for high-voltage work.
