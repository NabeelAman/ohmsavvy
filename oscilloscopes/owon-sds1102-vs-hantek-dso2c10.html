OWON SDS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10

OWON SDS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10 is a question of what you need the trigger to do. Both are two-channel 100 MHz benches at 1 GSa/s with 7-inch 800 × 480 screens. The OWON is slim and light, about 1.1 kg and 70 mm deep, with 10K points and edge and video triggers. The Hantek weighs 1.9 kg but brings 14 trigger types, including runt, window and five serial buses, plus 8 Mpts of memory and decoding. If you hunt intermittent faults or debug digital buses, buy the Hantek; if you mainly view steady waveforms on a cramped bench, the OWON is enough.

Spec by spec

Bandwidth, headline sample rate and screen are ties. The charts cover the four places they differ, and only one of them favors the OWON.

Memory and decoding against the same short-memory chassis are covered on HANMATEK DOS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10, whose HANMATEK publishes the same dimensions, weight and 10K record length as this OWON. This page looks at the other half of the gap: triggering.

OWON SDS1102 vs Hantek DSO2C10 on an intermittent fault

A scope finds intermittent faults through its trigger, not its screen. An edge trigger fires every time the signal crosses a level in one direction. On a clean 1 kHz square wave that is perfect. On a data line where one pulse in ten thousand only reaches half height, the edge trigger fires on the good pulses too, and the bad one flashes past between screen updates.

The DSO2C10's under-amplitude trigger sets two thresholds and fires only on a pulse that crosses the lower one but not the upper. Set it, walk away, and the scope stops on the first runt. Its other triggers work the same way for other faults. Window fires when a signal leaves a voltage band, which suits a supply rail that sags under load. Interval and overtime catch a clock that stretches or a gap that runs too long. Pattern fires when both channels match a logic state. The SDS1102 can only approach these problems by watching many sweeps, and with 10K points per sweep it can't record long stretches at full speed while it waits.

Serial triggers close the loop

For buses, the DSO2C10 can trigger on a specific UART value, I2C address or CAN frame and decode it on screen. That turns "the sensor sometimes reads wrong" into a capture of the exact wrong reply. The OWON can show the bus as edges, but it can't tell one byte from another.

Where the OWON still wins

Size and sensitivity. At about 1.1 kg and 70 mm deep, the SDS1102 fits a shelf or a crowded repair bench where a 1.9 kg scope won't. Its listing gives a 5 mV/div bottom range and a GND coupling position for quick zero checks, useful on ripple and audio. If your signals are steady and your bench is small, those count for more than triggers you won't use.

What both can and can't measure

With the three-to-five-times rule, both show signals up to about 20–35 MHz at close to true size. Neither has a signal generator: OWON lists none, and Hantek's table shows none for the DSO2C10 despite its Amazon listing mentioning waveform output. For a generator on this Hantek platform, see Hantek DSO2C10 vs DSO2D10. Neither has logic channels, and both are 8-bit at their core, so very small signals on top of large ones stay coarse unless the DSO2C10's high-res mode is on.

Mains bench scopes share earth

Both plug into mains, which ties every probe ground clip to earth. Clipping a ground lead to a live mains point or the hot side of an off-line supply shorts it through the scope. Neither listing gives a CAT rating for these models. Use a differential probe or an isolation transformer on the circuit under test, never on the scope, and call an electrician for building wiring.

The trust question: listing photos and claims

The SDS1102 listing's main photos show an SDS1022, a different OWON model, so judge it by OWON's spec pages and check the model badge on delivery. The DSO2C10 listing claims arbitrary waveform output, which Hantek's table contradicts. Both makers publish full spec pages, and on the specs that matter here (triggers, memory, decoding) the two sources agree with each other.

  • ✓You mostly view steady, repeating waveforms that an edge trigger locks easily
  • ✓You want a 5 mV/div bottom range, stated on its Amazon listing
  • ✓You don't need serial decoding or long captures

Choose the Hantek DSO2C10 if…

  • ✓You chase intermittent faults: runt, window, interval and pattern triggers
  • ✓You debug UART, I2C, SPI, CAN or LIN and want triggers and decoding
  • ✓You need long single-shot captures: 8 Mpts on one channel
  • ✓You want measurement statistics, two DVMs and a 6-digit counter

Neither if…

You need a built-in signal generator or four channels. Neither has a generator, and both have two channels.

The bottom line

Buy the Hantek DSO2C10 if you debug digital circuits or hunt intermittent faults: 14 trigger types including runt and window, five serial decoders and 8 Mpts of memory. Buy the OWON SDS1102 if your bench is small and your signals are steady: about 1.1 kg, 70 mm deep and a 5 mV/div range, with edge and video triggers and 10K points.

Frequently asked questions

Does the OWON SDS1102 have serial decoding?+

No. OWON lists edge and video triggers only and no protocol decoding for the SDS1102. You can see a UART or I2C line as edges and read bits by hand. The Hantek DSO2C10 triggers on and decodes RS232/UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN.

What is an under-amplitude (runt) trigger?+

It uses two voltage thresholds and fires only when a pulse crosses the lower one but fails to reach the upper. That isolates pulses that didn't reach a valid logic level, often caused by bus contention or a weak driver. The Hantek DSO2C10 lists it; the OWON SDS1102 doesn't.

Which has more memory?+

The Hantek DSO2C10, with 8 Mpts on one channel and 4 Mpts on two, against 10K points on the OWON SDS1102. At 1 GSa/s that is 8 ms against 10 µs of recorded time, which decides whether a single slow event can be captured at full detail.

Is the OWON SDS1102 lighter than the DSO2C10?+

Yes. OWON lists about 1.1 kg and 301 × 152 × 70 mm; Hantek lists 1.9 kg and 318 × 110 × 150 mm for the DSO2C10. Amazon's item weight for the SDS1102 is 2.2 lb, close to OWON's figure. Both run from mains.

Do both scopes support PC control?+

Yes. The SDS1102 Amazon listing mentions SCPI and LabVIEW support. Hantek lists SCPI and USB host and device ports for the DSO2C10. Either can be scripted to pull measurements or screenshots for logging, though you will need each maker's drivers and command set.
