Fluke 114 vs Fluke 116: Which Meter Fits Your Work?

Pick the Fluke 114 if you are an electrician who checks voltage, continuity and resistance on building wiring; pick the Fluke 116 if you service furnaces, boilers or air conditioning. The two share a case, a True RMS voltage core, AutoVolt LoZ and a CAT III 600V rating. The 116 adds a 600.0 µA flame-sensor range, Type K temperature, capacitance and frequency. Neither reads load amps, so both belong next to a clamp meter.

  • →Only the 116 measures temperature, through a Type K input from -40 to 400 °C, and it ships with an 80BK probe.

One meter is the other plus four functions

Lay Fluke's two spec tabs side by side and the relationship is unusual for a comparison: nothing the 114 measures is missing from the 116. Voltage, millivolts, the AutoVolt low-impedance position, resistance, continuity and Min/Max/Avg all appear on both, with matching voltage accuracy lines. The 116 then adds four functions that only an HVAC technician uses every week.

Two small items run the other way. The 114's spec tab lists AC millivolts as well as DC; for the 116 we could only confirm the DC 600.0 mV range. Fluke also states auto-ranging with manual override for the 114, while the 116 entry in our catalog notes a manual RANGE button and no explicit auto-ranging statement. Neither changes the decision for most buyers. Neither meter carries our Hands-on Tested badge; this page is desk research against Fluke's spec tabs, the 110 Series datasheet and the official Amazon listings.

The electrician's view: what the 116's extras are worth

For a wiring electrician, the 116's additions mostly sit unused. A receptacle check, a ghost reading on a switch leg, a continuity test on a home run and a resistance check on a baseboard heating element are the same on both meters, because the voltage, LoZ and resistance lines are identical. The microamp jack and thermocouple input exist for gas heating equipment.

Capacitance is the exception that can tip an electrician toward the 116. Ceiling fans with a slow speed, bath fans that hum and condenser fans on a service call all point at a run capacitor, and the 114 can't read one. Frequency helps too if you check portable or standby generators. If those calls are rare, the 114's shorter dial is a fair trade; if they come up monthly, the 116 earns its place even without a furnace in sight.

The HVAC technician's view: why the 114 falls short

On heating and cooling equipment the picture flips. Take a typical no-heat or no-cool call and count the readings a technician reaches for. The 114 handles the voltage side well, including thermostat wiring where its LoZ position sorts induced voltage on spare conductors from a real 24 V call. It stops at the components.

Can the 116 tell you a capacitor is out of tolerance?

This is the 116 function electricians and HVAC technicians share, so it is worth checking that the accuracy is good enough. Run capacitors are commonly labeled with a tolerance such as ±6%. Take an illustrative 35 µF capacitor with that marking: the acceptable band is 35 × 0.94 = 32.9 µF to 35 × 1.06 = 37.1 µF.

Fluke 116

What that means

Motor, blower, compressor and appliance amps need a clamp.

The 117 adds 6 A and 10 A ranges but no µA or temperature.

It is easy to read "µA" on the 116's dial and assume it covers current in general. It doesn't: 600.0 µA is 0.0006 A, far below a 24 V control load, let alone a motor. Buyers coming from the 114 sometimes expect the 116 to fill that gap, and it won't. In practice both meters ride in a bag with a clamp meter; our clamp meter buying guide covers that second tool. Notes on Fluke's published specs

Conflicts we found

Fluke's 114 page describes True RMS "voltage and current" in its overview, yet the spec table lists no current range and the meter has only two jacks, so we treat it as having none. The same page carries a temperature footnote with no temperature function listed; only the 116 measures temperature. Fluke gives the 114 as 550 g while Amazon lists 0.51 kg. For the 116, Fluke doesn't state auto-ranging explicitly.

Safety: CAT III 600V, and the µA jack is for control circuits only

Both meters are rated CAT III 600V, suitable for panels, feeders, branch circuits and HVAC disconnects, but not the meter base or service drop. Put the 116's µA input in series only with flame-sensor or similar low-current control circuits, never across a voltage source. Discharge capacitors before measuring them, and don't attach the thermocouple probe to energized parts. Prove circuits dead with a live-dead-live check, and call a licensed electrician or HVAC contractor for work you aren't qualified to do.

Choose the Fluke 114 if…

  • ✓Your day is receptacles, switch legs, feeders and panels, not heating equipment
  • ✓You want the shortest dial in the series, with LoZ and nothing you won't use
  • ✓You carry a clamp meter for current and never need to read a capacitor

Choose the Fluke 116 if…

  • ✓You check gas furnace flame sensors, which need a microamp range
  • ✓You want supply, return or line temperature without a second instrument
  • ✓You test run and start capacitors on condensers, blowers and pumps

The bottom line

Electricians who only check voltage, continuity and resistance on building wiring are well served by the Fluke 114, with its AutoVolt LoZ and short dial. Anyone who works on furnaces, air conditioners or motor capacitors should buy the Fluke 116: it keeps every 114 function and adds microamps, temperature, capacitance and frequency. Pair either with a clamp meter, because neither reads amps.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 116 just a Fluke 114 with HVAC functions?+

Close to it. Per Fluke's spec tabs, the 116 has every measurement the 114 has, with the same voltage accuracy, AutoVolt LoZ input and 40 MΩ resistance, and adds a 600.0 µA range, Type K temperature, capacitance and frequency. The 114 lists AC millivolts and states auto-ranging explicitly; the 116 page we used does not.

Does the Fluke 114 measure temperature?+

No. The 114's spec tab carries a temperature footnote, but no temperature function or range is listed, and the meter has no thermocouple input. In the 110 Series, Fluke's datasheet gives temperature to the 116 only, which reads -40 to 400 °C with a Type K probe and ships with the 80BK.

Can the Fluke 114 test a run capacitor?+

No. Capacitance isn't among the 114's functions, so it can only show whether a capacitor is shorted on a resistance range. The 116 measures up to 9,999 µF at ±(1.9% + 2), which is tight enough to compare most run capacitors against the tolerance printed on the can.

Can either the Fluke 114 or 116 measure amps?+

Not load current. The 114 has no current function. The 116's only current input reads up to 600.0 µA, intended for flame sensors and small control currents. For blower, compressor or appliance current, use a clamp meter, or choose the Fluke 117, which has 6 A and 10 A ranges.

Do the Fluke 114 and 116 both have LoZ?+

Yes. Both have the AutoVolt position, which selects AC or DC and drops the input to about 3 kΩ so coupled ghost voltage drains away. Fluke gives the same ±(2.0% + 3) accuracy on the 600.0 V range for both, so they behave identically on spare thermostat conductors or abandoned branch wiring.

Which is better for a furnace that won't stay lit?+

The Fluke 116. A burner that lights and then shuts down often has a weak flame signal, and the only way to read it is a microamp range in series with the flame rod. The 116's 600.0 µA range resolves 0.1 µA steps. The 114 can confirm voltages and check igniter resistance but can't read the flame signal.
