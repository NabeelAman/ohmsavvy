What Arduino and board-level work asks of a meter

Microcontroller projects live at low voltage and low current. You check that a regulator really delivers 3.300 V and not 3.21 V, that a voltage divider feeds the ADC the value your code expects, and that a battery-powered sensor sleeps at a few microamps rather than a few milliamps. On the board itself you hunt for open traces, solder bridges and reversed diodes. So the meters that rank well here have fine DC resolution, a genuine microamp range with a low or at least published burden voltage, quick continuity, and a diode test with enough voltage to light an LED. Our walk-throughs on measuring an Arduino's current consumption, checking a voltage regulator's output and testing a voltage divider show each job step by step.

We ranked the ten on those needs using the makers' manuals and spec pages, which puts 20,000-count and higher bench meters at the top and simpler 4,000 and 6,000-count meters lower down. CAT ratings count for less here than on an electrician's list, though they still matter if a project touches mains. For current measurement technique, read how to measure microamps; for board faults, see finding a short on a PCB and testing a diode. When a meter isn't enough, our oscilloscopes for Arduino and ESP32 projects guide picks up where this one stops.

Current mode is a short circuit

In µA, mA or A mode the meter is a near-zero resistance between its leads. Touch those leads across a power rail or a battery and you short it, which can blow the meter's fuse, damage a USB port or overheat a lithium cell. Always break the circuit and put the meter in series, move the red lead back to the V/Ω jack when you finish, and keep inrush and Wi-Fi transmit bursts off the µA range.

What to look for in a meter for Arduino and PCBs

A real µA range

A sleeping microcontroller draws microamps, so the meter needs a range of a few hundred µA with 0.1 µA or finer steps.

Low burden voltage

The meter's shunt drops voltage in series with your board; a few tens of millivolts can be enough to brown out a marginal 3.3 V rail.

Fine DC volts resolution

Checking a regulator, a divider or an analog sensor output means reading the third or fourth decimal place reliably.

Fast continuity

A quick, latching beeper catches a broken trace or a cold joint as you sweep the probe along a header row.

Diode test near 3 V

Enough open-circuit voltage to check LEDs and the protection diodes on GPIO pins.

Capacitance and frequency

Confirms a decoupling cap's value and a PWM output's frequency and duty cycle without reaching for a scope.

Counts decide how many digits you see

On a 6,000-count meter a 3.3 V rail lands on the 6 V range and reads 3.300 V. A 22,000-count meter shows that rail as 3.300 V too, since it falls just past the 2.2 V range, but a 1.8 V rail or an ADC reference reads to 1.8000 V. At 50,000 or 60,000 counts the 5 V or 6 V range shows 3.3000 V, a full extra digit on the most common Arduino rail. Extra digits only help if accuracy keeps up, so check the maker's DC accuracy alongside the count. If your readings jump around, our guide to unstable multimeter readings covers the usual causes, and an intermittent continuity beep explains slow or patchy beepers.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Match the µA range to the sleep current you expect

A bare ATmega or ESP32 module in deep sleep can draw single-digit microamps, while a development board with a USB chip and power LED may draw milliamps even when asleep. A meter with a 200 to 600 µA range and 0.01 µA steps resolves the first case; a 0.1 µA meter is fine for the second. If you only ever measure milliamps, the cheaper meters lower in the list are enough.

Think about burden before trusting a current reading

Burden is the voltage the meter steals from your circuit. Multiply the reading by the published figure: 300 µA on a 0.2 mV/µA meter is 60 mV, while on a 100 µV/µA meter it is 30 mV. If the board resets or behaves differently with the meter in line, switch up a range, which lowers the drop, or use a meter with a lower published burden.

Use logging for battery-life estimates

A board that sleeps for a minute and wakes for 200 ms has an average current no single reading shows. Meters that record over time, such as the OWON B41T+, OWON OW18E and Fluke 287, let you see the pattern and estimate battery life. For the fast wake-up spikes themselves, a scope across a small shunt resistor is still the better tool.

Don't overpay for safety ratings you won't use, but don't ignore them

Arduino projects run on 5 V and 3.3 V, so a CAT IV rating adds little on the bench. It matters the day you check a mains power supply or a relay board switching household loads. If your projects ever touch mains, choose one of the meters here with a published CAT III or CAT IV rating, and keep the cheap meter for the breadboard.

Methodology

How we chose

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What multimeter features do I need for Arduino projects?+

Start with auto-ranging DC volts, continuity, a diode test and a microamp current range. Those cover checking a 5 V or 3.3 V rail, finding broken jumper wires, testing LEDs and measuring how much current a sketch draws in sleep. Capacitance and frequency are useful extras. Meters such as the UNI-T UT61E+ and EEVblog Brymen BM786 add the fine resolution that makes those readings trustworthy.

How do I measure the sleep current of an Arduino or ESP32?+

Break the supply line and put the meter in series, with the red lead in the µA/mA jack and the dial on the lowest µA range. Let the board enter sleep before reading, because start-up and Wi-Fi bursts can be hundreds of milliamps and may blow the µA fuse. Some makers short the meter with a jumper during boot, then remove it once the board sleeps.

What is burden voltage and why does it matter on a 3.3 V board?+

Burden voltage is the drop across the meter's internal shunt when it measures current. Brymen publishes 0.2 mV per µA for the BM786, so a 500 µA reading costs 100 mV of the board's supply. On a 3.3 V rail with little headroom that can change behavior or trigger a brown-out reset, so a lower-burden meter or a higher range helps.

Is a cheap multimeter good enough for Arduino work?+

For checking that 5 V is present and wires are connected, yes. Budget meters start to struggle with sleep-current work, because many lack a µA range or only resolve 0.1 mA. The KAIWEETS HT118E and OWON OW18E are among the cheaper meters here that still offer a 200 µA range with 20,000-count displays.

Do I need True RMS for electronics and Arduino projects?+

Rarely for the board itself, since most readings are DC. True RMS matters when you measure AC from a transformer or a distorted waveform. Be aware that a multimeter set to DC volts shows the average of a PWM signal, not its peak; for the shape of the waveform you need an oscilloscope. All ten meters here are True RMS on AC volts.

Can a multimeter replace an oscilloscope for Arduino debugging?+

No. A meter reads steady values: rail voltages, resistance, average current, frequency and duty cycle. It cannot show a glitch, the timing of an I2C transaction or ringing on a fast edge. Many hobbyists buy a good meter first and add a scope when serial buses or motor drivers misbehave; see our oscilloscope guide for Arduino projects.
