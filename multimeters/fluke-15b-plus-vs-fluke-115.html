Fluke 15B+ vs Fluke 115: Budget Multimeter or Professional Meter?

Buy the Fluke 115 if you troubleshoot live equipment for a living or work on anything with electronics in the load; buy the Fluke 15B+ if you mainly check voltage, continuity and small currents and want a Fluke at the entry tier. The 115 is True RMS, shows 6,000 counts with a fast bar graph and carries a 3-year warranty. The 15B+ is not described as True RMS, but it keeps microamp and milliamp ranges that the 115 lacks entirely.

Key differences

  • →Waveform handling: the 115 is True RMS. Fluke makes no True RMS claim for the 15B+, so trust its AC readings on sine-wave loads only.
  • →Small current: the 15B+ starts at a 400.0 µA range and has four ranges below 4 A. The 115's lowest current range is 6.000 A, so it can't usefully read milliamps.
  • →Display: 6,000 counts plus a 33-segment bar graph refreshed 32 times a second on the 115, against 4,000 counts updated three times a second on the 15B+.
  • →Warranty: three years on the 115 versus one year on the 15B+, a direct signal of which tier each meter sits in.
  • →Diagnostics: only the 115 adds Min/Max/Avg recording, frequency and a capacitance range reaching 9,999 µF, ten times the 15B+'s 1,000 µF.
  • →Same on paper: CAT III 600V protection, ±0.5% DC and ±1.0% AC basic accuracy, resistance to 40 MΩ, a backlight, and no temperature on either.

What each price tier actually buys

The 15B+ is Fluke's entry point: a sturdy case, honest basic accuracy and a dial with only HOLD and RANGE buttons. The 115 sits a tier up, aimed at field technicians who measure on equipment they did not design and cannot simplify. The step between them is not mostly about build; both are Fluke-made, CAT III 600V meters. It is about three things the professional tier adds and one thing it gives up.

The 115 adds

True RMS on AC volts and amps, so electronic loads read correctly.

The 115 adds

A bar graph plus Min/Max/Avg, for readings that move or come and go.

The 115 adds

A 3-year warranty, three times the 15B+'s one year.

The 115 gives up

Every current range below 6 A: no µA, no mA, 1 mA finest step.

Budget tier keeps

1000 V range (CAT II), 2 × AA power rated 500 h minimum, IP40.

Neither has

Temperature, non-contact voltage or a low-impedance mode.

The Fluke 115 carries our Hands-on Tested badge; the owner has used it. The 15B+ has not been through our hands, so what we say about it is desk research against Fluke's product page and the 15B+/17B+ users manual. Prices move, so we don't quote them; the 115 costs noticeably more, and the question is whether you use what that money pays for.

Who really needs True RMS

A meter that is not True RMS works out an AC value by assuming the waveform is a perfect sine. On a receptacle, a toaster, a baseboard heater or a transformer secondary, that assumption holds well enough and the 15B+ and the 115 show the same thing. The assumption breaks on current drawn by electronics: LED drivers, computer and TV power supplies, variable-speed blowers and inverters pull current in short, tall bursts rather than a smooth wave. There a sine-assuming meter can read well under the real value, which hides an overloaded circuit.

Current: the one area where the budget Fluke wins

The current specs run in opposite directions. The 15B+ has six current ranges from 400.0 µA up to 10.00 A, all at ±(1.5% + 3). The 115 has two, 6.000 A and 10.00 A, with tighter percentage figures, ±(1.0% + 3) DC and ±(1.5% + 3) AC. That makes the 115 the better meter on amps and the 15B+ the only one of the pair that can measure milliamps meaningfully. The arithmetic below uses each meter's published worst-case figure on the range the reading would land on.

These are limits from the spec sheets, not readings from our bench. The 50 mA case is the telling one: the 115 would show three digits after the decimal point in amps, of which only the last one moves, and its allowance is more than three times wider than the 15B+'s. For anything below about 100 mA, such as parasitic drain on a vehicle or the supply current of a small board, the budget meter is the right tool. For loads of a few amps, the 115 roughly halves the uncertainty.

Readings that won't sit still

Field work rarely produces a steady number. Voltage sags when a compressor starts, a loose terminal flickers and a supply drifts as it warms. The 15B+ updates its digits three times a second and has no recording function beyond HOLD, so a brief dip can pass unseen. The 115 pairs a bar graph that refreshes 32 times a second with Min/Max/Avg, which keeps the lowest, highest and average value along with elapsed time while you watch the equipment rather than the meter.

Continuity differs in a way that matters for wiring checks. The 15B+ beeps below 70 Ω; the 115 beeps below about 20 Ω and, per Fluke's datasheet, catches opens or shorts lasting 500 µs or more, useful when you flex a harness or wiggle a connector to find an intermittent break. For that technique, see how to find an intermittent electrical fault.

Capacitors and frequency

The 115 reads capacitance from 1 nF to 9,999 µF, enough for HVAC run capacitors and large power-supply electrolytics. The 15B+ stops at 1,000 µF. Only the 115 reads frequency; Fluke's spec page gives 50 kHz while the 2020 users manual lists a 99.99 kHz range, so treat 50 kHz as the safe figure.

Safety ratings and warranty side by side

Both meters are rated CAT III 600V, which covers distribution panels, feeders and branch circuits in homes and light commercial buildings. The 15B+ also lists CAT II 1000V and reads up to 1000 V, but CAT II means plug-in equipment and appliance-level circuits, not fixed 1000 V distribution. Neither meter is rated CAT IV, so neither belongs on the service drop, meter base or main disconnect.

The warranty gap is the clearest tier marker. Fluke's users manual gives the 15B+ one year; the 115 gets three. For a meter that rides in a service van, that is real cover. For a meter that lives in a kitchen drawer and comes out a few times a year, one year is usually enough.

Match the meter to the location

Prove either meter on a known live source before and after testing a circuit you plan to touch, and keep to CAT III locations or lower. Work at the service entrance needs a CAT IV-rated meter and leads, and anything you aren't qualified to do belongs with a licensed electrician.

The bottom line

The Fluke 115 is the better meter for anyone who earns money with it: True RMS, a 6,000-count display with a fast bar graph, Min/Max/Avg and a 3-year warranty. The Fluke 15B+ is the sensible budget Fluke for home, hobby and DC work, and it is the only one of the two that reads microamps and milliamps. Skip the 15B+ if you meet electronic loads often; skip the 115 if small currents matter more than AC accuracy.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 15B+ True RMS?+

Fluke does not describe the 15B+ as True RMS on its product page or in the users manual. Its AC readings are reliable on sine-wave loads such as outlets and heaters, but they can read low on LED drivers, power supplies and variable-speed equipment. The Fluke 115 is True RMS on both AC volts and AC amps.

Can the Fluke 115 measure milliamps or microamps?+

Not usefully. Its only current ranges are 6.000 A and 10.00 A, so the finest step is 1 mA and a 50 mA reading carries a worst-case allowance of about ±3.5 mA. For parasitic drain, sensor loops or flame-sensor current you need a meter with mA and µA inputs, such as the 15B+, whose lowest range is 400.0 µA.

What warranty do the Fluke 15B+ and 115 have?+

Fluke's 15B+/17B+ users manual gives the 15B+ a one-year warranty; Fluke's US product page doesn't state a term. The Fluke 115 has a three-year warranty. Keep proof of purchase for either meter, and check the Amazon seller is an authorized source if warranty support matters to you.

Can I use the Fluke 15B+ on 1000 V circuits?+

Its voltage ranges reach 1000 V, but its rating at that level is CAT II 1000V, which covers plug-in equipment rather than fixed distribution. For panels and branch circuits it is rated CAT III 600V, the same as the Fluke 115. For 1000 V industrial systems or the service entrance, use a meter rated CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V.

Does either meter measure temperature?+

No. Per Fluke's manuals and datasheets, temperature is a 17B+ function rather than a 15B+ one, and in the 114 to 117 family only the 116 has it. If a thermocouple input matters, look at the 17B+ instead of the 15B+, or at the 116 instead of the 115, and check what else changes between those siblings before you buy.

Is the Fluke 115 worth the extra cost for home use?+

Usually not, unless your home projects include LED lighting circuits, inverters, solar equipment with AC output or HVAC equipment with variable-speed motors. For outlets, batteries, fuses, switches and continuity, the 15B+ gives Fluke build quality at the entry tier. The 115 earns its higher price through True RMS, Min/Max/Avg and its longer warranty.
