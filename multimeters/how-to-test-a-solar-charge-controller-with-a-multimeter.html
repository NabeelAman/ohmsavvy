How to Test a Solar Charge Controller With a Multimeter

To test a solar charge controller with a multimeter, measure DC volts at three places in sunlight: the PV input terminals, the battery terminals and the load terminals. A working controller shows panel voltage at the input, a battery voltage that rises toward its charge setpoint (around 14.4 V for many 12 V batteries) at the output, and the load output switching on and off as its settings say. This page shows what those readings should look like for PWM and MPPT controllers, how to tell a dead controller from a weak panel or a bad connection, and how to check the charge current without overloading your meter.

Any meter with a clean DC volts range does most of this test. A backlit display like the Fluke 115's helps in a dim battery locker. See our Fluke 115 review.

Battery and PV wiring can hurt you

  • A 12 V battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short. Remove rings and watches, and never let a probe or wrench bridge the battery terminals or the controller's battery screws.
  • Panels make voltage whenever light hits them, and you can't switch them off. Series strings on larger systems can exceed 100 V DC, which is a shock hazard. Cover the panels or open the PV breaker before you touch any PV conductor.
  • Never move the red lead to the amps jack and then probe across the battery or the panel; that shorts the source through the meter. Keep the meter on DC volts for every step except step 6.
  • Roof-mounted or grid-tied systems belong to a licensed solar installer. This guide is for small off-grid 12/24 V systems on RVs, boats, sheds and cabins.

What a healthy charge controller does

A charge controller sits between the panel and the battery and does one job: it lets as much solar current through as the battery can safely take, and no more. Early in the day, with a partly empty battery, it runs in bulk and passes all the current the panel can make. When the battery reaches its absorption voltage it holds that voltage and lets the current taper. When the battery is full it drops to a lower float voltage, or for some lithium profiles stops charging altogether. Many controllers also have a load output that switches DC loads off when the battery gets too low.

A multimeter can check every part of that behavior, because each stage shows up as a voltage at a terminal. The trick is to measure at the right moment. A controller tested at dusk, or with a full battery at noon, looks broken when it's doing exactly what it should. Test with sun on the panel and some room in the battery: run a load for an hour first if the battery is already full.

1Read the controller and panel labels first

Write down four numbers before you measure anything. From the panel label: open-circuit voltage (Voc), maximum-power voltage (Vmp) and maximum-power current (Imp). From the controller: its maximum PV input voltage and its rated charge current. A typical 100 W panel sold for 12 V systems lists a Voc of about 21–23 V and a Vmp of about 17–19 V, with Imp around 5–6 A, but check yours. Then check which battery type is selected in the controller's menu or DIP switches. A lithium battery on a flooded lead-acid profile, or the other way round, gives readings that look like a fault and aren't. If the panel itself is in doubt, run the solar panel Voc and Isc test first.

2Measure the PV input (V1)

Set the meter to DC volts. Put the black probe on the controller's PV negative screw and the red probe on PV positive, with everything connected and the sun on the panel. You're reading the panel voltage while the controller loads it. Then open the PV breaker or unplug the panel connector and read again at the panel side: that open-circuit figure should be close to the label's Voc, adjusted for temperature (cold panels read higher, hot panels lower).

What the connected reading should be depends on the controller type. A PWM controller ties the panel to the battery through a switch, so while it charges, V1 sits only a little above battery voltage, perhaps 13–14 V on a 12 V system. An MPPT controller works the panel near its Vmp, so V1 stays well above the battery, often in the 17–18 V range for a 12 V-class panel. If V1 equals the full Voc while the battery is clearly not full, the controller isn't drawing any power from the panel.

No voltage at all at the controller, but a normal Voc at the panel, means an open in between: a blown PV fuse, a tripped breaker, an MC4 connector that isn't latched, or a cable chewed through on the roof. Read across the fuse or breaker with both sides connected; a healthy one shows nearly 0 V across it, while an open one shows the full panel voltage.

3Measure the battery output (V2)

Probe the controller's battery positive and negative screws. Note the reading, then watch it for 15 to 30 minutes in steady sun. A battery that is charging rises. A 12 V lead-acid battery climbs from its resting 12.2–12.6 V toward its absorption setpoint; a 12 V LiFePO4 rises slowly through the 13.2–13.4 V plateau and then quickly near the top. When it reaches absorption, the voltage should hold steady at the setpoint, not keep climbing. A reading that climbs above about 15 V on a 12 V system, outside an equalization cycle you started, means the controller isn't regulating, and you should disconnect the PV.

Compare the peak reading against the battery maker's figures, not a generic chart. Many 12 V AGM batteries want about 14.4–14.7 V absorption and 13.5–13.8 V float; many 12 V LiFePO4 batteries want 14.2–14.6 V and either no float or a low one. A controller that never gets past 13.8 V on a LiFePO4 bank is leaving capacity unused, which is one reason a lithium battery won't charge to 100%.

4Check for wiring loss between controller and battery

While it charges, read V2 at the controller's screws and then directly on the battery posts. The difference is lost in cables, fuses and lugs. The controller regulates the voltage at its own terminals, so if 0.4 V disappears in the wiring, a battery that should see 14.4 V only gets 14.0 V and never finishes its absorption stage.

Ohm's law gives a quick sense of scale. At 20 A, a round trip of cable, fuse and connections totaling 0.02 Ω drops 20 × 0.02 = 0.4 V. Keep the drop under a few tenths of a volt at full charge current; less is better. If it's high, read across each joint in turn. A warm lug or a fuse holder with a large drop across it is the culprit, and the voltage drop method used on car wiring works the same way here.

5Test the load terminals (V3)

If your controller has a load output, read across its screws. With the load mode set to "always on" and a battery above the reconnect voltage, V3 should be within a few tenths of a volt of battery voltage. If it reads zero, check three things in order: the load mode (many controllers ship with dusk-to-dawn or timer modes that turn the output off in daylight), the low-voltage disconnect, and any load fuse.

The low-voltage disconnect is a feature, not a fault. On many controllers it switches the load off around 11–11.5 V for lead-acid profiles and reconnects once the battery recovers to a higher voltage; lithium profiles use different thresholds. If V3 drops out at a battery voltage well above the manual's disconnect figure, the controller's load switch or its voltage sensing is suspect. Note that load outputs are usually rated for much less current than the charge side. Wiring an inverter to them is a common way to kill a controller.

6Check the charge current without overloading the meter

Voltage alone proves the controller is regulating; current proves how much energy actually reaches the battery. Most handheld meters have a 10 A range rated for limited periods, so they can only measure current on small systems. On a single 100 W panel, current is low enough: open the PV breaker, insert the meter in series in the PV positive lead with the red lead in the 10 A jack, close the breaker and read. Then put everything back and return the red lead to the V jack straight away.

For bigger arrays, use the controller's own display or a battery monitor shunt, and use the meter to confirm the voltages those readings imply. You can also cross-check power: an MPPT controller converts high panel voltage into more battery current. If the panel delivers 17.5 V × 5.4 A ≈ 94.5 W, and the battery sits at 13.5 V, an MPPT controller at about 95% efficiency should push roughly 94.5 × 0.95 ÷ 13.5 ≈ 6.6 A into the battery. A PWM controller can't do that conversion: battery current is about the same as panel current.

Measure both sides at once

A charge controller is a converter, so the evidence is always a comparison: panel voltage in, battery voltage out, at the same moment and in the same sun. One reading on its own rarely proves anything.

PWM vs MPPT: what the readings should look like

The two controller types behave so differently that readings from one would look like a fault on the other. Use this table to set expectations before you decide anything is wrong.

ReadingPWM controllerMPPT controller
PV voltage while charging (V1)Near battery voltage, a little aboveNear the panel's Vmp, well above the battery
PV voltage when battery is fullRises toward Voc as the switch opensRises toward Voc as it draws less power
Battery current vs panel currentAbout the sameHigher than panel current at low battery voltage
Start-up in weak lightStarts once PV exceeds batteryOften needs PV a few volts above battery first
Effect of a 24 V-class panel on a 12 V batteryWastes much of the panel's powerConverts the extra voltage into current

The start-up row explains a common morning complaint. Many MPPT controllers wait until the PV voltage is several volts above the battery before they begin tracking, so on a dull morning V1 can show 15 V at the input while nothing reaches the battery. That isn't a fault. If it still won't start by midday in clear sun, the panel's Voc may be too close to the battery voltage for that controller, which happens when a heavily shaded panel or a single 12 V-class panel feeds a 24 V bank.

Which multimeter to use on a charge controller

You need a stable DC volts range with enough resolution to see 0.01 V, because absorption setpoints and wiring losses live in the second decimal place. Any 4,000- or 6,000-count meter does that. A 10 A range is handy for small panels, and a CAT rating matters if the same meter goes near mains or high-voltage PV strings. Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my solar charge controller is bad?

Measure the PV terminals and the battery terminals in full sun with the battery below full. If the panel voltage is present at the controller, the battery is connected and correctly set, and the battery voltage still doesn't rise over 15 to 30 minutes, the controller isn't charging. Confirm the fuse, the settings and the battery type before you replace it.

What voltage should a solar charge controller output?

It depends on the charge stage and battery type. For a 12 V lead-acid or AGM battery, bulk and absorption usually reach about 14.2 to 14.7 V, then float drops to about 13.5 to 13.8 V. A 12 V LiFePO4 profile commonly charges to 14.2 to 14.6 V. The controller's manual lists the exact setpoints for each battery type it supports.

Why does my PV voltage drop when the controller is connected?

That's normal. A PWM controller connects the panel almost directly to the battery, so the panel voltage falls close to battery voltage while it charges. An MPPT controller loads the panel near its maximum-power voltage, often around 17 to 18 V for a typical 12 V-class panel. Only open-circuit voltage, measured with the panel disconnected, should match the label's Voc.

Can I test a charge controller without a panel or in the dark?

You can check that it powers up from the battery, shows the right battery voltage and switches the load output. You can't test charging without a PV source. Some people use a bench power supply on the PV input, but only within the controller's limits and with current limiting set; most makers don't document that as a test method.

Should I connect the battery or the solar panel first?

Most charge controller manuals say connect the battery first so the controller can detect the system voltage, then the panel. Disconnect in the reverse order: panel first, then battery. Connecting the panel with no battery can confuse 12/24 V auto-detection or, on some models, expose the battery terminals to panel voltage. Follow your own manual if it says otherwise.
