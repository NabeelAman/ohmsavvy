Fluke 289 vs Fluke 115: Is a Logging Multimeter Worth It?

A logging multimeter like the Fluke 289 is worth it if you regularly chase faults that come and go when nobody is watching, or you have to hand someone a record of what a circuit did. If you mostly find a fault and fix it on the spot inside CAT III 600V, the Fluke 115 does that job and the 289's extra cost buys features you won't use. Both are True RMS Flukes; the 289 adds time-stamped recording, a microamp range, CAT IV 600V, temperature and 50,000 counts, while the 115 is lighter and simpler.

Key differences

  • →Logging: the 289 records up to 10,000 intervals plus events against a real-time clock and graphs them on screen. The 115 keeps one minimum, one maximum and an average, with elapsed time.
  • →Documentation: the 289's IR link sends recordings to FlukeView Forms on a PC; Fluke lists no PC link for the 115.
  • →Safety: the 289 is rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V; the 115 stops at CAT III 600V.
  • →Low current: the 289 resolves 0.01 µA; the 115 has no mA or µA input and resolves 1 mA at best.
  • →Extras only on the 289: temperature, LoZ, a 1 kHz low-pass filter, a 50 Ω low-ohm range and 250 µs peak capture.
  • →Living with it: the 115 is 550 g and runs about 400 hours on one 9 V battery; the 289 is 871 g on six AA cells, rated 100 hours minimum.
  • →Shared: both are True RMS, auto-ranging Flukes. The 289's warranty is lifetime limited; the 115's is 3 years.

What logging actually buys you

"Logging" on the Fluke 289 means three things working together. The meter stores readings as intervals and events, up to 10,000 intervals in Fluke's manual. Each record carries a time from the built-in real-time clock. And TrendCapture draws the record as a graph on the meter's own screen, so you can review a dip without a laptop. The IR link then moves the data to FlukeView Forms when you need a printable report.

The Fluke 115 has a simpler tool for the same problem: Min/Max/Avg with elapsed time. Leave it on a circuit and it keeps the lowest reading, the highest and the average, and tells you how long after you started each extreme occurred. That's genuinely useful, and it's more than many meters offer. But it holds one minimum. If the fault happens twice, the shallower event disappears.

The difference matters most with repetition. Two dips at 23:41 and 04:12 that both match a well pump or a furnace cycle point to a cause. A single minimum with an elapsed time of six hours confirms a problem and stops there. Our guide to finding an intermittent electrical fault walks through using either approach.

How long a recording can run

Two limits decide how long the 289 can watch a circuit: memory and battery. Fluke's manual gives up to 10,000 recorded intervals and a battery life of 200 hours in logging mode. Our data doesn't list the interval settings, so here is illustrative arithmetic only: if the meter closed one interval per minute, 10,000 intervals would cover 10,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 167 hours, about 6.9 days, and the 200-hour battery figure (about 8.3 days) would outlast it. At one interval every 30 seconds, memory fills in about 83 hours, roughly three and a half days.

Either way, a weekend or a full work week is within reach, which is the window most intermittent complaints need. The 115 has no memory limit to worry about because it stores only three values, and its 9 V battery is rated about 400 hours, so it can sit in Min/Max/Avg for a long time too. It just can't tell you much when you come back.

Precision and safety, beyond logging

Logging isn't the only thing the 289's higher tier buys. Its DC accuracy is twenty times tighter on the percentage term, its display has more than eight times the counts, and its rating extends to CAT IV 600V. Here is what the accuracy gap means at two readings, using each meter's published line and the resolution of the range the reading lands on.

ReadingFluke 289 allowanceFluke 115 allowance
24.000 V DC control circuit0.006 + 0.002 = ±0.008 V0.12 + 0.02 = ±0.14 V
120.0 V AC outlet, 60 Hz0.36 + 0.25 = ±0.61 V1.2 + 0.3 = ±1.5 V

The arithmetic: 0.5% of 24 V is 0.12 V, plus 2 counts of 0.01 V on the 115's 60.00 V range; the 289 adds 2 counts of 0.001 V to its 0.006 V. At 120 V AC the 115 sits on its 600.0 V range and the 289 on 500.00 V. These are worst-case limits from published specs, not measurements. For checking an outlet or a 24 V thermostat circuit, ±1.5 V and ±0.14 V are entirely adequate, which is why the 115 is enough for most field service.

CAT III 600V is a real boundary

The 115 is rated for branch circuits, receptacles and distribution panels inside the building. It isn't rated for the service entrance, meter base or outdoor service conductors, where CAT IV applies; the 289 is. Whichever meter you use, prove it on a known live source before and after a dead test, and leave utility-side work to the utility or a licensed electrician.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 115 log data?+

Not in the sense the 289 does. The 115 has Min/Max/Avg with elapsed time, which keeps the lowest, highest and average readings and how long after the start each extreme happened. It has no memory of separate events, no real-time clock and no PC link listed. For a timeline of every dip or surge you need a logging meter such as the 289.

What is TrendCapture on the Fluke 289?+

TrendCapture is Fluke's name for the 289's on-screen graph of recorded readings. After a recording session you can view the trend on the meter itself without a computer. The recording holds up to 10,000 intervals plus events against a real-time clock, and the IR link sends it to FlukeView Forms when you need a report.

How long can the Fluke 289 record?+

It depends on the interval setting, which our data doesn't list. Fluke's manual gives up to 10,000 recorded intervals and 200 hours of battery in logging mode. As illustrative arithmetic, one interval per minute would fill memory in about 167 hours, close to seven days, before the batteries run down. Start long runs with fresh AA cells.

Is the Fluke 289 overkill for home electrical work?+

Usually, yes. Household outlets, switches, appliances and branch circuits sit inside the 115's CAT III 600V rating, and its True RMS and 6,000-count display handle them well. The 289 earns its place at home only for a fault that comes and goes, such as nightly dimming, where a recording shows when it happens and how often.

Does the Fluke 115 measure temperature or microamps?+

No to both. Fluke's datasheet gives the thermocouple input to the Fluke 116, not the 115, and the 115's current inputs are 6 A and 10 A with 1 mA best resolution. The 289 reads temperature from -200 to 1350 °C excluding the probe and resolves 0.01 µA on its 500 µA range, which covers HVAC flame-rod checks.

Is a dedicated data logger better than a logging multimeter?+

For permanent or multi-week monitoring of one quantity, a dedicated logger is often simpler. The 289's advantage is that the same meter does live troubleshooting and recording, so you can switch from probing to logging on the spot. If you rarely log, a 115 for daily work plus a borrowed or rented logger can cover the occasional long job.
