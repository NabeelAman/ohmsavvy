How to Compare Input and Output Signals on an Oscilloscope

The quickest way to find out what a circuit is doing to a signal is to compare input and output signals on an oscilloscope side by side: input on CH1, output on CH2, both locked to the same trigger. One glance shows whether a stage amplifies, attenuates, inverts or distorts, and a couple of readings turn that into a gain figure in decibels. Below is the setup, the settings that keep the comparison honest, and how to read each result.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 22, 2026

A two-channel scope with a built-in waveform generator, like the Hantek DSO2D10 in our full review, can supply the test input and measure both sides of the stage.

1Connect CH1 to the input and CH2 to the output

Feed the circuit a known test signal, usually a sine from a function generator at a frequency inside the range the stage is meant to handle. Clip the CH1 probe tip to the input node and the CH2 probe tip to the output node. Both ground clips go to the circuit's ground, the same ground the generator returns to. Trigger on CH1, since the input is the reference that everything else is judged against.

2Match the probe and channel settings on both sides

A comparison is only as good as its weakest channel setup. Use the same probe type on both sides, set each channel's attenuation to match its probe (10x probe, 10x channel), and compensate both probes against the scope's calibration output. Start with the same V/div on both channels so equal heights really mean equal voltages; you can change one later if the output is much larger, as long as you account for it. Keep both on DC coupling when you want to see clipping against a supply rail, and switch the output to AC coupling only if a DC bias pushes it off screen.

3Overlay or stack the two traces

With both channels centered on the same zero line, the traces sit on top of each other and any difference in height or timing is obvious. That overlay works well for gain. When the shapes get busy, use each channel's vertical position to stack them, input in the upper half and output in the lower half, so you can study each waveform without the other one in the way. Stacking is also kinder to inverted outputs, which otherwise cross the input trace twice every cycle.

4Read the gain and convert it to decibels

Measure the same quantity on both channels, peak-to-peak for example, then divide: gain = Vout ÷ Vin. In dB that becomes 20 × log10(Vout ÷ Vin). On the overlay below the input peaks at 1 division and the output at 2, both at 1 V/div, so the input is 2 Vpp, the output is 4 Vpp, the gain is 2 and the stage provides about +6 dB.

5Spot inversion, clipping and filter attenuation

Inversion shows up as the output peaking while the input bottoms out, as in the stacked view above. Clipping appears when you push the input up and the output stops growing: its tops and bottoms flatten against the supply rails while the input stays a clean sine. Turn the input down until the flat tops disappear to find the largest undistorted swing.

With a filter, sweep the generator frequency and watch the output shrink relative to the input; the frequency where the ratio falls to about 0.707 is the −3 dB point. The output usually shifts in time as well, which you can turn into degrees with our phase difference guide. For fast logic, the same two-channel setup also shows propagation delay, the time from an input edge to the matching output edge. Many scopes can also plot CH1 minus CH2 as a math trace, which shows the difference between two signals directly; see how to subtract two signals with scope math.

Troubleshooting an input/output comparison

SymptomLikely causeFix
Gain off by exactly 10x (20 dB)Probe attenuation and channel setting do not matchSet each channel to the probe's 1x or 10x position
Output trace drifts while input is stableTrigger set to CH2, or output frequency differsTrigger on CH1; check the stage is not oscillating
Square-wave input shows overshoot or roundingProbe not compensatedCompensate both probes on the calibration output
Output sits off screenDC bias on the outputUse the channel position control or AC coupling on CH2
Large hum on one trace onlyThat probe's ground clip is loose or on a different groundReturn both clips to the same circuit ground point

One ground, not two

On a bench scope, both probe grounds connect to each other and to mains earth inside the instrument. Clipping the CH2 ground to anything other than the circuit's ground shorts that point to earth through the scope.

Mains-powered circuits

Never clip a scope ground onto a non-isolated mains circuit. Use an isolation transformer on the device under test, a differential probe, or an isolated-input scope, and keep within the CAT and voltage ratings printed on the probes and the scope inputs.

Scopes for input/output comparisons

Two channels are the minimum for this job, since both traces must share one trigger and one timebase. A built-in generator saves a separate box for driving the input, and extra channels let you watch an intermediate stage at the same time.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I use 20 log and not 10 log for voltage gain?

Decibels are defined from a power ratio as 10 × log10(P2/P1). Power in a given resistance goes with the square of voltage, so the voltage version becomes 20 × log10(Vout/Vin). Use 20 log when you are working from two voltage readings on the scope, which is almost always the case here.

Should I compare peak-to-peak or RMS readings?

Either works as long as you use the same measurement on both channels. Peak-to-peak is easiest to read off the grid, but it includes any noise spikes. RMS from the Measure menu tends to be steadier on a clean sine. Mixing the two, such as Vpp on CH1 and RMS on CH2, gives a ratio that is wrong by a fixed factor.

Can I compare input and output with a one-channel scope?

Only for steady, repeating signals and only for amplitude. Measure the input, move the probe to the output, and measure again without changing the source. You lose the shared time reference, so inversion and phase shift cannot be seen reliably. Two channels make the comparison far quicker and more trustworthy.

My output is bigger than expected by exactly 10 times. What happened?

Almost certainly a probe attenuation mismatch. One channel is set to 1x while a 10x probe is attached, or the other way round, so its reading is off by a factor of ten, which is 20 dB. Check the attenuation setting in each channel menu against the switch on each probe.

Do I need a signal generator to compare input and output signals?

Not always. Any steady, repeating signal the circuit already handles, such as audio from a phone or a sensor output, lets you compare the two sides. A generator helps because it gives a clean sine at a known frequency and amplitude, so gain and distortion are easy to judge. Some bench scopes include one; otherwise a basic function generator or a microcontroller PWM pin will do.
