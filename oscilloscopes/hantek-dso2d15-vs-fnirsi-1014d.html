Hantek DSO2D15 vs FNIRSI 1014D

Hantek DSO2D15 vs FNIRSI 1014D puts two benchtops that each carry a built-in generator side by side. Both have two channels, a 1 GSa/s peak rate, an 8-bit converter and a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen. The Hantek goes further on almost every published figure: 150 MHz against 100 MHz, 8 Mpts of memory, UART to LIN decoding, and a generator reaching 25 MHz at up to 7 Vpp.

Key differences

  • →Bandwidth: 150 MHz on the DSO2D15 against 100 MHz on the 1014D. Hantek quotes a 2.4 ns typical rise time; FNIRSI says under 3 ns.
  • →Sample rate: both peak at 1 GSa/s. Hantek states 500 MSa/s per channel with two on; FNIRSI doesn't say what the 1014D does with both channels running.
  • →Memory: Hantek publishes 8 Mpts on one channel and 4 Mpts on two. FNIRSI writes only "240Kbit", with no point count.
  • →Generator: the DSO2D15's reaches 25 MHz sine and 10 MHz square or arbitrary at up to 7 Vpp into high impedance, with burst and modulation. The 1014D's gives sine to 10 MHz, other waves to 2 MHz, at 2.5 Vpp.
  • →Decoding: Hantek lists UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding. FNIRSI lists none for the 1014D.
  • →Measurement tools: 32 automatic measurements with statistics, two DVMs and a 6-digit hardware counter on the Hantek; 12 automatic parameters on the FNIRSI.
  • →Power and weight: the DSO2D15 runs on mains; Hantek says 1900 g, but Amazon lists 5.5 lb, a conflict we can't resolve. The 1014D runs from a 5 V adapter and Amazon lists 3.46 lb.

Spec by spec

The two share a screen size, a converter width and a peak sample rate, so the charts below concentrate on the published numbers that differ. Hantek's figures come from its DSO2000 series table and FNIRSI's from the 1014D specification table.

With both channels on, the DSO2D15 still takes about three samples per cycle at its full 150 MHz rating and fifty per cycle of a 10 MHz clock, above the Nyquist minimum of two. We can't do the same sum for the 1014D because FNIRSI doesn't publish the shared rate. See what GSa/s means for why the per-channel number is the one that counts.

Memory: how long each scope can look

Memory decides how much time fits in one capture at a fast sample rate, and it is where this pair differs most. At 1 GSa/s the DSO2D15's 8 Mpts holds 8 ms of signal; with both channels at 500 MSa/s, 4 Mpts each still covers 8 ms. That is enough to catch a complete CAN frame, a whole I2C transaction at 100 kHz, or several cycles of a 1 kHz PWM signal with every edge sampled at full speed.

FNIRSI writes the 1014D's memory as "240Kbit". Read it generously as 240,000 points and the scope holds 0.24 ms at 1 GSa/s, about a thirtieth of the Hantek's window. If it really means bits at 8 bits per sample, the window is shorter still. Either way, on a long timebase the 1014D must lower its sample rate to fit, which is when narrow glitches disappear between samples.

Capture time = memory ÷ sample rate

8,000,000 points ÷ 1,000,000,000 samples per second = 8 ms. Halve the sample rate and the window doubles; halve the memory and it shrinks by half. Comparing scopes on this one division tells you more than either number alone.

What each can and can't measure

Rated bandwidth is where a sine has already lost 3 dB, so apply a three-to-five-times margin: the DSO2D15 suits signals up to roughly 30 to 50 MHz and the 1014D up to roughly 20 to 33 MHz. Both clear Arduino and ESP32 PWM, audio, hobby SPI and most buck-converter switching. The Hantek's margin pays off on fast logic edges and higher-speed SPI clocks, where you want to judge edge shape rather than just count transitions. Our explainer on oscilloscope bandwidth covers the rule.

Decoding is the bigger practical gap for anyone working on cars, robots or sensor boards. With CAN or LIN decoding, the DSO2D15 can trigger on a message and print its contents under the trace, so a body-module fault becomes a list of frames instead of a screen of square waves. On the 1014D you would capture the same bus and work out the bits by hand, within its shorter memory window.

The generators split along similar lines. A 7 Vpp swing lets the Hantek stand in for a 5 V logic clock or a sensor output when testing a microcontroller input, and burst mode can send a fixed number of pulses. The 1014D's 2.5 Vpp source, with 1 Hz frequency steps, is better matched to driving op-amp and filter inputs, where small signals are the point.

Mains-powered means earth-referenced

The DSO2D15 plugs into the mains, and like most bench scopes its probe ground normally connects to mains earth. Hantek rates its input at 300 Vrms with a 10X probe and CAT II 300 V; FNIRSI lists 400 V for the 1014D on 10X but gives no CAT rating. Neither figure allows clipping a ground lead onto a live conductor. Use a differential probe or an isolated setup for line-side measurements.

Choose the Hantek DSO2D15 if…

  • ✓You debug UART, I2C, SPI, CAN or LIN traffic and want the bytes decoded on screen
  • ✓You want long captures: 8 Mpts on one channel or 4 Mpts each on two
  • ✓Your generator has to swing a full 5 V logic level or reach past 10 MHz
  • ✓You want every spec published, including the two-channel sample rate

Choose the FNIRSI 1014D if…

  • ✓Your work is analog: filters, audio and sensor stages well below 20 MHz
  • ✓You want a kit with two 1X/10X probes and a 100X probe already in the box
  • ✓You prefer a scope that runs from a 5 V adapter rather than a mains cord
  • ✓You want an external trigger input, listed by FNIRSI at 0 to 40 V

Neither if…

You need to clip a ground lead onto a live conductor: both are mains-powered and earth-referenced, and while Hantek rates the DSO2D15 CAT II 300V, FNIRSI gives no CAT rating at all for the 1014D's 400V 10X figure — line-side work needs a differential probe or an isolated setup either way.

The bottom line

The Hantek DSO2D15 is the stronger instrument on published specs: 150 MHz, a stated 500 MSa/s per channel with both on, 8 Mpts of memory, five serial decoders and a 25 MHz, 7 Vpp generator. Choose it for digital, automotive and microcontroller work. The FNIRSI 1014D makes sense for analog and audio experiments below about 20 MHz, where its 2.5 Vpp source, 5 V adapter power and 100X probe kit cover what you need.

Frequently asked questions

Is 150 MHz on the Hantek DSO2D15 worth it over the FNIRSI 1014D's 100 MHz?+

Only if you look at fast edges or signals above about 20 MHz. By the three-to-five-times rule the DSO2D15 handles signals to roughly 30 to 50 MHz and the 1014D to roughly 20 to 33 MHz. For PWM, audio and slow serial buses both are enough; the memory and decoding gaps matter more.

Can the FNIRSI 1014D decode CAN or I2C like the Hantek DSO2D15?+

FNIRSI lists no serial decoding for the 1014D. Hantek lists UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding on the DSO2D15, so it can trigger on bus traffic and show the decoded bytes. On the 1014D you would read the bits from the waveform yourself.

Which has the better built-in signal generator?+

The DSO2D15 on every published figure: sine to 25 MHz, square and arbitrary to 10 MHz, up to 7 Vpp into high impedance, plus burst and modulation. The 1014D gives sine to 10 MHz, other waveforms to 2 MHz and a custom wave to 1 MHz at 2.5 Vpp, with 1 Hz frequency resolution.

Why does the DSO2D15's sample rate drop with two channels on?+

Hantek's 1 GSa/s converter is shared, so each channel gets 500 MSa/s when both run, and memory splits from 8 Mpts to 4 Mpts each. That is common on two-channel budget scopes. FNIRSI doesn't publish the 1014D's two-channel rate, so you can't assume it keeps 1 GSa/s per channel.

How much does the Hantek DSO2D15 weigh compared with the 1014D?+

The sources disagree for the Hantek. Hantek's own spec table says 1900 g, while Hantek's plain DSO2D15 Amazon listing gives an item weight of 5.5 lb and a net weight of 2900 g in its product description. The 1014D's Amazon listing says 3.46 lb. Either way, both are desk instruments; the DSO2D15 needs a mains socket and the 1014D a 5 V adapter.
