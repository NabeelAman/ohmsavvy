How to Measure Resistance of a Heating Cartridge With a Multimeter

A heating cartridge is the small metal cylinder that heats a 3D printer hot end, and similar parts sit in soldering stations, glue guns and industrial molds. When a printer throws a heating error or the nozzle won't reach temperature, the first job is to measure resistance of a heating cartridge with a multimeter. The catch is that low-voltage cartridges are only a few ohms, so the way you measure matters as much as the number: a few tenths of an ohm in your test leads can make a good part look wrong.

1Work out the expected resistance

A cartridge is sold by voltage and wattage, not ohms, but the two are linked by R = V² / P. Square the rated voltage and divide by the rated power. Because voltage is squared, cartridges with the same wattage differ by a factor of four between 12V and 24V versions, which is the single most useful thing to know when identifying an unmarked part.

These figures are nominal. Cartridges are made to a tolerance, commonly around ±10%, so a 24V 40W part reading 13.5 or 15.5 Ω is not a fault. Mains cartridges for industrial and soldering use follow the same formula with a much larger V², so they read tens to hundreds of ohms. For the general method on mains elements, see our heating element resistance guide.

2Null the test leads

A handheld meter measures resistance with the same two wires that carry its test current, so it reads the leads, the probe tips and the contact resistance as part of the answer. On a 10 kΩ resistor that is invisible. On a 3.6 Ω cartridge, 0.3 Ω of lead resistance is an error of more than 8%. Set the meter to its lowest resistance range, press the probe tips firmly together, and note the reading. Subtract it from everything you measure, or use the meter's relative or zero mode if it has one.

3Measure the cartridge

Switch the printer off and unplug it, then let the hot end cool fully. Unplug the heater leads from the control board, or unscrew them from the terminal block, so the MOSFET and board traces aren't in the measurement. Measure across the two heater wires and subtract the lead figure. While the probes are on, flex the wires near the cartridge body and at the crimp: a reading that jumps or opens when the cable moves is a broken conductor, a very common failure on moving print heads.

4Check for a short to the sheath

Move to a high resistance range and measure from each heater lead to the cartridge's metal sheath, or to the heater block it sits in. Both should read OL. A finite reading means the element has broken through its insulation to the casing. On a low-voltage printer this can feed heater voltage into the hot end, the nozzle and anything touching it, and on a mains cartridge it is a shock hazard. Either way the cartridge should be replaced. Our washing machine element guide walks through the same terminal-to-sheath check on a mains appliance.

Cold vs hot resistance

Heater cartridges use resistance alloys chosen for a low temperature coefficient, so their resistance changes only a little between room temperature and printing temperature. Always measure cold, both for safety and so you can compare against the calculated figure. If a cartridge reads correctly cold but the printer still reports a heater fault once warm, suspect a lead that opens when hot or a poor crimp at the board connector rather than the element.

12V cartridge on 24V: why it's dangerous

Power is V² / R, so doubling the voltage across the same resistance quadruples the power. A 12V 40W cartridge of about 3.6 Ω fed from a 24V board tries to dissipate around 160W. It heats far faster than the firmware's control loop expects, overshoots, and can overheat the block, melt nearby plastic or wiring, and stress the board's heater MOSFET and connector. The opposite mistake is safe but useless: a 24V cartridge on 12V delivers only a quarter of its rating and may never reach temperature.

Check before you fit

Measure every replacement cartridge before installing it, even when it came from a kit labeled for your printer. Confirm the printer's supply voltage, make sure thermal runaway protection is enabled in the firmware, and never leave a printer heating unattended after changing a heater.

Frequently asked questions

What resistance should a 24V 40W heater cartridge have?

About 14.4 ohms, from R = V² / P, which is 24 × 24 / 40. Cartridges are made to a tolerance, so a reading within roughly 10% of that, after subtracting your lead resistance, is normally fine. A 12V 40W cartridge works out to 3.6 ohms.

How can I tell if my hot end cartridge is 12V or 24V?

Measure it. A 40W cartridge for 12V reads about 3.6 ohms and one for 24V reads about 14.4 ohms, a four-to-one difference that's easy to see even on a basic meter. Some cartridges have the rating printed on the lead or sleeve, but resistance is the reliable check when the marking is missing.

Why does my meter read 0.4 ohms with the probes touching?

That's the resistance of the test leads, probe tips and the meter's input connections. It's normal, and on high-value resistors it doesn't matter. On a cartridge of a few ohms it's a significant error, so subtract it from every reading, or use your meter's relative or zero function if it has one.

Can a heater cartridge test fine and still cause a thermal runaway error?

Yes. Thermal runaway and heating-failed errors often come from the thermistor or thermocouple, a cartridge that has slipped partly out of the heater block, a loose set screw, a part-cooling fan blowing on the block, or a crimp that goes open when hot. A correct resistance rules out only the heater element itself.

Is a mains-voltage industrial cartridge tested the same way?

The resistance method is the same, but the expected value is far higher because of the higher voltage, and insulation matters more. A cartridge that reads anything other than OL from a lead to its metal sheath must not be powered from mains. An insulation resistance tester is the proper tool for that check.
