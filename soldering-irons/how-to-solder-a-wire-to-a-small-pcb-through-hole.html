HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Solder a Wire to a Small PCB Through Hole

To solder a wire to a PCB through hole, strip about 3 mm of insulation, twist and tin the strands, push the wire in from the component side, then hold the iron on the pad and wire together and feed solder into the far side of the joint. On a small pad that takes roughly two to four seconds at a typical 320-350 °C leaded setpoint, and you stop when a smooth, concave fillet wraps the whole pad. Then trim the tail to about 1 mm and anchor the wire so flexing never reaches the joint. If solder refuses to go into the hole, jump to the causes and fixes table.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 22, 2026

A compact iron such as the Pinecil V2 (USB-C PD powered, about 12 s heat-up) suits small board work. See our Pinecil V2 review.

Hot tips, fumes and flying wire ends

  • Unplug the device and remove any battery before the iron goes near the board. If a lithium pack is swollen, hot or hissing, stop, move it outdoors onto a nonflammable surface and call 911 if it starts to smoke.
  • Park the iron in its stand between joints; the tip runs well above 300 °C.
  • Ventilate the bench: flux smoke irritates the airways. Wash your hands after handling leaded solder.
  • Wear eye protection when trimming; clipped wire ends fly.

Choose a wire gauge that fits the PCB hole

Work backwards from the hole. Small boards and breakout modules mostly use finished holes of about 0.8 mm to 1.0 mm, sized for component leads and 2.54 mm headers. As a typical rule, aim for a hole roughly 0.2 mm to 0.4 mm wider than the conductor so solder can flow down the gap.

AWGConductor diameter (nominal)Typical hole it fitsCommon use
300.25 mm0.6 mm and upWire-wrap, mods, bodge wires
280.32 mm0.6-0.8 mmRibbon cable, fine signal wiring
260.40 mm0.8 mmSensors, servo leads, I2C runs
240.51 mm0.8-1.0 mmSignal and low-current power
220.64 mm1.0 mmHook-up wire, LED strips
200.81 mm1.2 mm and upSmall power leads

These are solid-equivalent diameters; a stranded bundle measures slightly more, so test-fit a tinned end first. For wires carrying real current, size the wire for the load, not the hole.

You can check the fit with simple arithmetic. Board designers commonly size a plated hole at the largest lead diameter plus about 0.2 mm, a clearance derived from the IPC-2222 design standard. For stranded hook-up wire, the lead is the whole bundle: seven strands laid around a center strand measure about three strand diameters across. A 22 AWG 7/30 wire therefore spans 3 × 0.255 mm ≈ 0.76 mm, and 0.76 + 0.2 = 0.96 mm, so it slides into a 1.0 mm hole with room for solder. A 24 AWG 7/32 wire spans 3 × 0.203 ≈ 0.61 mm and wants about 0.81 mm, which makes a 0.8 mm hole a tight, borderline fit unless the tinning is thin.

1Strip the right length

The bare length should equal the board thickness plus about 1 mm to 2 mm of protrusion. On a standard 1.6 mm board that means stripping about 3 mm. Too much and bare copper above the board can touch a neighboring pad; too little and the strands never reach the solder side. Use the stripper notch for your gauge so no strands are cut, and leave roughly 0.5 mm to 1 mm between insulation and board.

2Twist and tin the wire

Twist the strands tightly in the direction of their lay, then touch the iron under the wire and let a little solder run into the strands, binding them into one smooth rod rather than leaving a blob on the end. Tinning stops stray strands fanning over the pad. If the tinned end will not pass through the hole, it is carrying too much solder.

3Insert the wire from the component side

Push the tinned end through from the side where parts sit, so the joint forms on the opposite face. A slight bend in the tail stops it dropping out. Clamp the board in a holder so nothing moves while the solder sets.

4Heat the pad and the wire together

Lay the side of the tip against the copper pad and the wire at once; a small bead of solder on the tip acts as a heat bridge. After about a second, feed solder where wire meets pad, away from the tip, so it flows down into the hole. Typical setpoints are 320 °C to 350 °C leaded and 340 °C to 370 °C lead-free; see our soldering temperature guide.

5How much solder to feed

Less than most beginners expect: on a 0.8 mm hole with a 1.5 mm pad, a few millimeters of 0.6 mm or 0.8 mm solder is usually enough. Stop once the fillet covers the pad while the wire outline still shows. Withdraw the solder, then the iron, and hold the wire still while the joint freezes.

A worked example shows why so little is needed. Take a 22 AWG wire (about 0.76 mm across) in a 1.0 mm hole through a 1.6 mm board. The ring of space around the wire is π/4 × (1.0² − 0.76²) ≈ 0.33 mm², and over the 1.6 mm barrel that is roughly 0.53 mm³. Add an illustrative 0.3-0.4 mm³ for a concave fillet on a 1.8 mm pad and a small ring on the top side, and the joint holds about 1 mm³ of metal. A 0.8 mm solder wire carries π/4 × 0.8² ≈ 0.50 mm³ per millimeter, so about 2 mm of feed fills it; with 0.6 mm solder (0.28 mm³ per millimeter) it is closer to 3.5 mm. Allow a little extra for the flux core and the film left on the tip, and you land on the 2-4 mm most joints take. Past that, you are building a ball, not a better joint. The solder wire diameter guide covers which thickness to buy.

On a plated hole, a small solder ring on the component side shows the barrel filled. For more on judging fillet shape, see how to use a soldering iron.

Does solder need to come through to the other side?

Not all the way, but most of the way. The IPC-A-610 workmanship standard is commonly summarized as asking for at least 75% vertical fill of a plated barrel on Class 2 and Class 3 assemblies, with some relief for holes tied to large copper planes. On a 1.6 mm board, 0.75 × 1.6 = 1.2 mm of the barrel should hold solder. In practice, when the fillet on your side is concave and a thin silver ring or meniscus shows around the wire on the component side, the barrel has filled. No ring at all usually means the solder only capped the hole; reheat with fresh flux rather than adding more solder on top.

6Trim the tail

Trim the end to about 1 mm with flush cutters, flat face toward the board, in one clean snip. Twisting the cutters can crack a fresh joint. Trimming before soldering also works if enough still pokes through for a fillet.

7Add strain relief

A solder joint is rigid and the wire beside it is not, so bends concentrate where the solder stops and strands break there. Support the wire so the joint never takes the load:

  • Loop the wire up through a spare or dedicated strain-relief hole first.
  • Run a bead of hot glue or neutral-cure silicone over the insulation and board.

Keep the heat off thin insulation

A short dwell with a hot, clean tip beats a long, cool touch. See soldering wires without overheating insulation.

Soldering wires to a row of header holes (Arduino, ESP32 and sensor boards)

Development boards and breakout modules put their connection holes on a 2.54 mm grid, and those holes are the usual place you end up soldering wires directly. A typical pad there is about 1.7 mm across, so the copper gap between neighbors is only 2.54 − 1.7 = 0.84 mm. A single escaped strand from 22 AWG wire is about 0.25 mm thick and easily reaches across, so the risk on a header row is a short, not a weak joint.

  • Work from one end of the row and solder every other hole first, so the board between joints cools.
  • Pre-tin every wire end before any of them goes in; tinning in place is how strands escape.
  • Alternate insulation colors and dress the wires the same way, so you can spot a crossed lead.
  • Check adjacent pins with a meter on continuity before powering up.

If the wires will be unplugged later, a header pin plus a jumper housing is often the better choice than direct wiring. For general bridge prevention, see how to prevent solder bridges.

How to solder fine wire (28-30 AWG) into a through hole

Fine wire has the opposite problem from thick wire: it rattles in the hole, wanders while you feed solder and breaks next to the joint. A 30 AWG 7/38 wire spans about 0.30 mm, which leaves roughly 0.35 mm of empty space on each side in a 1.0 mm hole. Three fixes help. Fold the tinned end back on itself once to double its thickness to about 0.6 mm, so it fills the hole and grips before you heat it. Tape the insulation to the board a few millimeters from the hole, so the wire cannot move while the joint freezes. Then drop to a 0.5-0.6 mm solder and a small chisel tip, and keep the touch to two or three seconds, because thin insulation gives up first. Once the joint cools, glue the wire down: fine conductors fatigue within a few bends. For enamel-coated wire, the coating has to come off or burn through first; follow soldering enamelled wire to a PCB pad or how to solder magnet wire.

Plated vs unplated holes (perfboard and single-sided boards)

Everything above assumes a plated through hole, where a copper barrel joins the pads on both faces and solder is pulled through it. Cheap perfboard, stripboard and many single-sided boards have bare holes with copper on one side only. There, the hole just locates the wire: insert it from the bare side, make the fillet on the copper side, and do not expect solder to climb the hole. The joint relies on that one pad's adhesive, so strain relief matters even more. Hold the board up to a light to tell: a plated hole shows a copper lining, a bare one shows fiberglass. Mounting holes are often unplated, so never use one as an electrical connection.

Which soldering iron and tip to use for small through holes

Any temperature-controlled iron works; the tip matters more. A 1.5 mm to 2 mm chisel moves more heat into a pad than a needle-point conical and still fits between close holes. The tip types guide compares the shapes; for wire-to-wire splices, see how to solder wires together.

Frequently asked questions

Should a wire be soldered on the top or bottom of a PCB?

Push the wire in from the component side, the face where parts sit, and make the fillet on the opposite solder side. The insulation and bend stay on one face and the joint on the other, so the board supports the wire. On a plated hole, solder drawn up the barrel shows as a small ring on the component side.

My wire is too thick for the PCB hole. What can I do?

Use the next thinner gauge if the current is low, as it is for most signal and sensor wiring. If you need the thicker wire, solder it to a header pin or a short tail of thinner wire first. Never drill out a plated hole: the drill removes the copper barrel linking the pads.

Can I solder a wire to a PCB pad without putting it through the hole?

You can tack a tinned wire onto a pad's surface, but it is much weaker. The thin copper pad and its adhesive take all the pull, and a sharp tug can lift it. Passing the wire through the hole lets the barrel and both pads share the load.

How long should the iron stay on a through-hole joint?

Typically two to four seconds for a small pad and thin wire with a suitable tip. If solder has not flowed by about five seconds, lift off, let it cool and fix the cause: a bigger tip, a slightly higher setpoint or fresh flux. Holding on longer mostly melts insulation.

Do I need extra flux when soldering a wire to a PCB?

Rosin-core solder carries enough flux for a clean new board and freshly tinned wire. Add a little liquid or paste flux when the pad is old, tarnished or reworked, or the tinned strands have dulled. Clean the residue off with isopropyl alcohol unless the flux is a no-clean type.

Why won't solder go through the hole to the other side?

Usually the barrel never reached melting temperature, so solder caps the top instead of being drawn down. Heat the pad and wire together, not the wire alone, and feed solder opposite the tip. Old oxidized plating needs extra flux, and a hole tied to a ground plane may need a bigger tip. If the hole is plugged with old solder, clear it first.

Do you need solder on both sides of a through hole?

No. On a plated hole you solder from one side, and the barrel carries solder through. A fillet on the solder side plus a thin ring around the wire on the component side shows good fill. Adding a second fillet from the top just reheats the joint and risks melting insulation. Unplated holes only ever get solder on the copper side.

Can you put two wires in one PCB hole?

You can if both fit with room for solder, but it is rarely the best option. Two 26 AWG bundles side by side span about 0.96 mm, too tight for a 1.0 mm hole. Twist and tin two thin wires together first, or join them off the board with heat-shrink and run a single wire into the hole. Each wire needs strain relief either way.

How do I stop a thin wire falling out of the hole while soldering?

Put a small bend in the tail after it pokes through, and tape the insulation to the board a few millimeters away. With very fine wire, fold the tinned end back on itself so it fills more of the hole. Clamp the board in a holder rather than holding it, so your free hand feeds solder and nothing moves while the joint cools.

Is it okay to solder a wire to a PCB hole meant for a header pin?

Yes. Header holes on development boards are normally plated and sized around 1.0 mm, which suits 22-26 AWG stranded wire. Pre-tin each wire, solder alternate holes so the row stays cool, and check neighbors for shorts. If the wire may need to come off later, fit a header pin and a jumper connector instead of soldering the wire directly.
