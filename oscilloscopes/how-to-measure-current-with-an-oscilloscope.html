HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Measure Current With an Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope only measures voltage, so to measure current you either pass the current through a small known shunt resistor and read the voltage across it (I = V ÷ R), or you clamp a current probe around the wire, which converts current into a voltage the scope can display. With a 0.1 Ω shunt, every 100 mV on the screen is 1 A. This guide covers choosing the shunt, wiring it on the low side so the scope's ground clip can't short anything, scaling the channel to read amps, and when a current probe is the better tool.

The ground clip is the hazard

  • On a mains-powered bench scope, every probe ground clip is connected to protective earth through the power cord. Clipping it to any point that isn't at ground potential creates a short circuit through the scope, which can melt the lead, damage the circuit and the scope, and cause injury.
  • Never defeat the scope's earth connection with a cheater plug to "float" it. That puts the whole chassis at circuit voltage.
  • Don't put shunts into mains wiring. For mains current use an isolated current probe or a clamp meter rated for the job, and leave live mains work to a qualified electrician.
  • Shunts carrying several amps get hot. Use a part rated for the power and keep fingers off it.

Shunt or current probe: the two ways to see current

A shunt is the cheap, accurate route for low-voltage DC circuits: a small, precise resistor in series with the load, and Ohm's law. A current probe clamps around the wire without breaking it, is isolated from the conductor, and is the safe choice for higher voltages, but it costs more and has its own offset and bandwidth limits. A multimeter tells you the average current; the scope shows how it changes, such as the startup surge of a motor or the pulses drawn by a switching regulator.

1Choose the shunt

Aim for roughly 10 to 100 mV across the shunt at the highest current you expect. Below that, scope noise swamps the signal; above it, the shunt steals voltage from the load and gets hot. Power is I² × R, so pick a shunt rated at least twice the worst-case figure. The table works the arithmetic for common values.

Max currentShuntVoltage across it (I × R)Power (I² × R)
100 mA1 Ω100 mV10 mW
1 A0.1 Ω100 mV0.1 W
5 A0.02 Ω100 mV0.5 W
10 A0.01 Ω100 mV1 W
30 A0.001 Ω30 mV0.9 W

Use a metal-element or thick-film current-sense resistor, not a wire-wound one: wire-wound resistors have inductance that adds false spikes on fast current edges. Check the shunt's real value before trusting it; our guide to testing a shunt resistor with a multimeter explains how to measure milliohms.

2Wire the shunt on the low side

Put the shunt in the ground return, between the load's negative and the supply's negative. One end of the shunt is then at ground, so the probe's ground clip goes on that end and the tip goes on the other. Nothing gets shorted through the scope's earth. This is the single most important step: on the high side, between the positive supply and the load, the ground clip would connect the positive rail to earth.

3Set the probe and the channel

For small shunt voltages, switch the probe to 1x and set the channel's probe factor to match, so you get the full signal rather than a tenth of it. Use DC coupling so the average current shows as a level, not just the changes. If the current has fast edges, use the probe's short ground spring instead of the long clip lead, which picks up noise. Turn on the 20 MHz bandwidth limit if you only care about power-level changes; it cleans up the trace considerably. Our guide to measuring the noise floor shows how small a signal your scope can resolve.

4Scale the reading to amps

Divide the voltage by the shunt resistance. With a 0.1 Ω shunt, 1 mV is 10 mA and 100 mV is 1 A. Many scopes let you change the channel unit from V to A and enter a probe ratio, so the screen and the automatic measurements read amps directly. For a 0.1 Ω shunt the ratio is 10 A per volt; check your scope's manual for how it labels this setting. Write the scale on a label next to the shunt so you don't misread it later.

5Capture and measure the current

Trigger on a rising edge of the shunt channel at a level just above the normal current. For a repeating load such as a PWM motor, use normal trigger mode and read peak, average and RMS with the automatic measurements. For a one-off event such as the inrush when a device switches on, use single-shot mode and read the peak with a cursor. Put the supply voltage on a second channel to see how far it sags during the surge.

High-side shunts and mains current

Sometimes the shunt must sit in the positive lead, for example when the load's negative is bolted to a chassis. Then put CH1 on one end of the shunt and CH2 on the other, both with ground clips on circuit ground, and display CH1 − CH2 with the math function. It works for modest supply voltages, but the two channels each see the full rail, so small gain differences between them show up as error. Use the same probes, the same V/div on both, and compensate both probes first. Our guides to measuring a differential signal with two channels and compensating a probe cover both steps.

For mains-powered loads, a shunt is the wrong tool. Use an isolated current probe rated for the voltage category, or simply read the RMS current with a clamp meter; see how to measure AC current with a clamp meter. To catch the moment a supply misbehaves under a current surge, our guide to capturing a power supply transient pairs well with this one.

Turn current into voltage, safely

A scope only sees voltage. A shunt in the ground return turns current into a few tens of millivolts with the ground clip on a point that is already grounded. Divide by the shunt value and the trace is in amps.

Scopes to use for current measurements

Two channels are the minimum, so you can see voltage and current together; four channels let you watch supply, current and control signals at once. Fine vertical sensitivity matters for small shunt voltages. Compare more models in our best oscilloscopes for power supply testing.

Frequently asked questions

Can an oscilloscope measure current directly?

No. A scope input measures voltage, so current has to be converted first. A shunt resistor does it with Ohm's law, and a current probe does it with a magnetic sensor around the wire. Many scopes let you set the channel unit to amps and enter a scale factor, so the screen reads current directly once the conversion is set up.

How do I measure inrush current with an oscilloscope?

Put a low-side shunt or a DC-capable current probe in the supply return, set the scope to single-shot capture, and trigger on a rising edge just above the normal running current. Switch the device on and the scope freezes the surge. Read the peak with a cursor and divide by the shunt resistance. On mains-powered equipment, use a rated current probe rather than a shunt.

What size shunt resistor should I use with an oscilloscope?

Choose one that gives roughly 10 to 100 mV at the highest current you expect. That is large enough to read above noise and small enough not to disturb the circuit. For 1 A, 0.1 Ω gives 100 mV and dissipates 0.1 W. For 10 A, 0.01 Ω gives 100 mV and 1 W. Use a non-inductive, low-tolerance part rated well above that power.

Why should I use a 1x probe setting for a current shunt?

A 10x probe divides the signal by ten, so a 20 mV shunt signal arrives as 2 mV, close to the scope's noise floor. A 1x setting keeps the full signal. The trade-off is bandwidth: 1x probes are usually limited to a few megahertz, which is fine for most power and motor current but not for very fast edges.

Can I measure AC mains current with an oscilloscope?

Not with a shunt and an ordinary probe; the shunt sits at mains potential and the scope's ground clip is tied to earth. Use a current probe or clamp rated for the voltage category, which is isolated from the conductor, or a properly rated differential probe. If you are unsure, measure mains current with a clamp meter instead and leave the waveform to a qualified technician.

What is the difference between a Hall-effect current probe and a current transformer?

A Hall-effect probe senses the magnetic field directly, so it measures DC and AC and needs a battery or power supply. A current transformer measures only AC, because it relies on a changing field, but needs no power. For battery, motor startup and DC power supply work, choose a DC-capable Hall-effect probe.
