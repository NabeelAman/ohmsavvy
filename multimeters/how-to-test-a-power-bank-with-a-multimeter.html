How to Test a Power Bank With a Multimeter

To test a power bank with a multimeter, measure its USB output voltage through a USB breakout board (it should sit between about 4.75 and 5.25 V on a standard port), then draw a steady current from it with the meter in series and time how long it runs. Output current multiplied by run time gives the real capacity at 5 V, which will always be lower than the mAh printed on the case. This guide shows the setup, the arithmetic that explains why a 10,000 mAh bank delivers roughly 6,000 mAh to your phone, and the readings that mean the bank is worn out rather than just misunderstood.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 15, 2026

A meter with a 10 A range, such as the Fluke 15B+, can carry a 1–2 A power bank load for the whole run. See our Fluke 15B+ review.

Power banks are lithium batteries

  • Don't test a power bank that is swollen, cracked, has been dropped hard, or smells sweet or chemical. Stop using it, keep it away from anything flammable, and take it to a battery recycler.
  • Never open the case to probe the cells. The pouch cells inside can short against a screwdriver tip and catch fire. Every test on this page works from the USB ports.
  • The load resistor gets hot. A 5 Ω resistor at 5 V dissipates about 5 W, so mount it on a heatsink or a ceramic tile, and stay with the test.
  • Stop if the bank becomes too hot to hold comfortably. A bank that overheats under its own rated load has a fault; see why a power bank gets hot.

Why the rated mAh shrinks before it reaches your phone

Power bank makers quote capacity at the voltage of the lithium cells inside, which is about 3.6 to 3.7 V nominal. Your phone is fed at 5 V or higher. Energy is what survives the conversion, so the honest way to compare is watt-hours: Wh = mAh × V ÷ 1,000. A 10,000 mAh bank holds 10,000 × 3.7 ÷ 1,000 = 37 Wh.

The boost converter that raises 3.7 V to 5 V is efficient but not perfect. Converter efficiencies of roughly 80 to 90% are typical for this kind of circuit, and the exact figure depends on the design and the load. At 85%, the bank delivers 37 × 0.85 ≈ 31.5 Wh. At 5 V that is 31.5 ÷ 5 = 6.3 Ah, or about 6,300 mAh. That is the number your multimeter test will reveal, and it is why a bank that "only" gives 6,000-odd mAh at 5 V can still be perfectly healthy.

People usually test a power bank because it empties faster than expected or refuses to charge something. Start by matching the symptom, because a cable or a sleeping output looks a lot like a dead bank.

1Inspect the bank and charge it fully

Look for bulging seams, a case that rocks on a flat table, scorch marks around the ports, or loose USB sockets. If anything looks wrong, stop here. Otherwise charge the bank with an adapter that meets its input rating until every indicator LED is solid or the display reads 100%, and give it half an hour to finish topping off. A capacity test only means something if it starts from a genuinely full charge. If the bank never reaches full, the input side is the problem, which our guide on why a power bank won't charge covers.

2Connect a USB breakout board

A USB-A breakout board (a short plug with screw terminals for VBUS, D+, D− and GND) is the cleanest way to put multimeter probes on a power bank. Cutting a cable open works, but the conductors are thin and easy to short together. For USB-C ports, use a USB-C breakout or a USB-A port on the same bank; a bare USB-C port won't turn on its output unless it sees the correct pull-down resistor on the CC pin, which a proper sink breakout provides.

Leave D+ and D− unconnected for this test. Without a phone on the other end, the bank will stay at its default 5 V and won't try to negotiate a higher fast-charge voltage. That keeps the arithmetic simple and the load resistor within its rating.

3Check the output voltage with no load

Set the meter to DC volts and put the red probe on VBUS and the black probe on GND. Press the bank's power button. You should see a reading close to 5 V; many banks sit slightly high, around 5.0 to 5.2 V, to allow for cable losses. If the reading flickers on and drops to 0 V after a few seconds, the output is timing out because nothing is drawing current. That is a design feature, not a fault.

4Run a timed load test

Pick a load near half to two-thirds of the port's rating. For a 2.4 A port, 1 A is comfortable. By Ohm's law, R = V ÷ I = 5 ÷ 1 = 5 Ω, and the power is P = V × I = 5 W, so use a 5 Ω resistor rated at 10 W or more. An adjustable electronic load is better because it holds the current constant. Move the meter's red lead to the 10 A jack, set it to DC amps, and wire it in series between VBUS and the load. If you have a second meter, keep it across VBUS and GND to watch the voltage.

Start a timer when you connect the load and note the current every 15 to 30 minutes. A power bank holds its output voltage steady until the very end, so with a resistor the current stays nearly constant, which makes the log easy. The test ends when the output shuts off. Check that the meter's manual allows continuous use of the 10 A range at your current; many meters limit high currents to short bursts, but 1 A is usually well within continuous limits.

5Calculate capacity at 5 V and at the cells

Multiply average current by hours. Say a 10,000 mAh bank delivered an average of 0.98 A for 6 hours 15 minutes: 15 minutes is 0.25 h, so 0.98 × 6.25 = 6.13 Ah, or 6,130 mAh at 5 V. Energy out is 6.13 Ah × 5.0 V ≈ 30.6 Wh. Divide by the label's 37 Wh and you get 83%, which combines the converter's efficiency and any capacity the cells have lost. 6Judge the result

If the delivered energy is 75 to 90% of the rated watt-hours, the bank is behaving as designed. Between about 60 and 75%, the cells have aged or the converter is inefficient at your test current; that is common after a few hundred cycles. Well under 60% means real capacity loss or a bank that was over-labeled from the start. Lithium cells are commonly considered worn at around 80% of their original capacity, the same end-of-life line our lithium battery capacity test uses for bare cells.

Compare watt-hours, not mAh

The label counts milliamp-hours at 3.7 V, while your test counts them at 5 V. Convert both to watt-hours before you decide whether the bank is short-changing you.

Which multimeter to use to test a power bank

The current meter needs a DC range above your test current, and a fused 10 A input is the safe choice. For voltage, a 6,000-count meter shows the port's sag to the nearest 10 mV. If you have never placed a meter in series before, read how to measure milliamps with a multimeter first, and check your cable separately with our USB cable test, because a bad cable makes a good bank look weak.

Frequently asked questions

Can I check a power bank's capacity with just a multimeter?

Not from a single reading. The meter gives you volts and amps at one moment, and capacity needs time as well. Draw a steady current, for example 1 A through a 5 Ω resistor, log the current, and time the run until the bank shuts off. Amps multiplied by hours gives amp-hours at 5 V, which you can convert back to cell mAh.

Why does my 10,000 mAh power bank only charge my phone twice?

The 10,000 mAh is measured at the cell voltage of about 3.7 V, which is 37 Wh. The bank boosts that to 5 V or more and loses around 10 to 20 percent as heat, and your phone loses more charging its own battery. A typical 4,000 to 5,000 mAh phone battery therefore gets roughly one and a half to two full charges.

What voltage should a power bank USB port read?

A standard USB port should read close to 5 V, within the USB specification's tolerance of roughly 4.75 to 5.25 V at the connector. With nothing plugged in, some banks read 0 V because the output is switched off until a load is detected. Fast-charge ports only rise to 9, 12, 15 or 20 V after negotiating with a compatible device.

Why does my power bank turn off during the test?

Most power banks switch off their output when the current falls below a small threshold, so they don't drain themselves into an idle cable. A very light load, such as a single LED or a meter's own resistance, may not keep it awake. Use a load of a few hundred milliamps or more, and make sure the meter's fuse and range are correct.

Is it bad if my power bank output voltage drops under load?

A small drop is normal. At 2 A the bank's converter, port and cable resistance might pull 5.1 V down toward 4.9 V. A drop below about 4.75 V at the bank's port at its rated current, or the voltage sagging further as the test continues, suggests a weak converter, worn cells with high internal resistance, or a dirty USB socket.
