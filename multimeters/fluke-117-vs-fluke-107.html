Fluke 117 vs Fluke 107

Fluke 117 vs Fluke 107 (or Fluke 107 vs Fluke 117, as many people search it) compares Fluke's electrician's meter with its palm-sized one. Both are CAT III 600V with 6,000-count backlit displays and 10A current. The 117 is True RMS and adds VoltAlert non-contact detection, AutoVolt and a low-impedance mode; the 107 weighs 200 g, fits in a pocket, and Fluke states plainly that it is not True RMS.

How much smaller is the Fluke 107?

Size is the 107's whole argument. It is roughly a third of the 117's weight and noticeably thinner, which is the difference between a meter that lives in your pocket and one that lives in the van.

By volume the gap is bigger than either chart suggests. Multiply out Fluke's dimensions and the 117's case is about 646 cm³ against roughly 274 cm³ for the 107, so the small meter takes up about 42% of the space. The 107 also runs on two AAA cells you can buy anywhere, while the 117 takes a 9V battery.

Fluke 117 vs 107 spec by spec

On the numbers Fluke publishes, the two are closer than the price gap suggests. Basic DC volts accuracy is ±0.5% on both, with one extra count of uncertainty on the 107. AC volts are ±(1.0% + 3) on both. The real differences are what the meter does with a distorted AC signal, how far its AC current reading stays accurate up the frequency scale, and how long it keeps going between battery changes.

What True RMS changes in practice

An average-responding meter such as the 107 rectifies the AC signal, measures its average and multiplies by 1.11, the ratio between RMS and rectified average for a pure sine wave. On a clean sine that trick works, so on a healthy 120 V receptacle the 107 and the 117 should agree within their stated accuracy. A True RMS meter like the 117 computes the heating value of the waveform directly, so it doesn't care what shape the wave is.

Shape is where the methods split. A square wave of 10 V peak has a rectified average of 10 V, so the 1.11 multiplier makes an average-responding meter show about 11.1 V against the true 10.0 V, an 11% error. The error runs the other way on the narrow, peaky current drawn by computer power supplies and LED drivers, where an averaging meter tends to read low. Variable-speed drives, dimmers and switch-mode loads all produce these shapes, which is why True RMS matters most for drive and lighting work. A second meter, not a replacement

The 107 works well alongside a True RMS meter, for quick checks where carrying the larger 117 is a nuisance. For variable-speed drives and modern lighting circuits, keep using the 117. If you want a True RMS Fluke without the 117's extras, see Fluke 115 vs Fluke 117.

The missing low-impedance mode is the gap electricians feel most. With an input above 10 MΩ, the 107 can show a believable voltage on a disconnected conductor that shares a conduit with a live one. The 117's AutoVolt position drops to about 3 kΩ and drains that phantom away. If you carry the 107 for wiring work, learn the load-check method in our guide to measuring voltage on a floating circuit.

Home electrical testing with the 117 or the 107

Around a house, most readings are the easy kind: is the receptacle getting its 120 V, is the battery flat, is the fuse or the lamp cord open. On those the two meters are equals, because Fluke gives them the same AC volts line and nearly the same DC line. The 117 pulls ahead on the three jobs that involve uncertainty about what is live: finding which box or cable is energized, telling a dead circuit from one showing phantom voltage, and reading circuits fed by dimmers or LED drivers.

Safety: both stop at CAT III 600V

The 117 and 107 share a CAT III 600V rating, which covers panels, feeders and branch circuits but not the service entrance or meter base, where CAT IV applies. A non-contact beep from the 117 is a first check only; confirm dead with the test leads, proving the meter on a known live source before and after. If a panel shows heat damage, buzzing or a burning smell, switch off at the main if it is safe to do so and call a licensed electrician.

Choose the Fluke 117 if…

  • ✓You work on circuits with drives, LED drivers or electronic loads, where True RMS matters
  • ✓You want NCV, AutoVolt and LoZ for fast, safe checks on live electrical work
  • ✓This will be your main meter, used every day

Choose the Fluke 107 if…

  • ✓You want a Fluke small enough for a pocket, glovebox or second toolbag
  • ✓You mostly check batteries, outlets, continuity and resistance
  • ✓You want frequency, duty cycle and diode test in a compact meter

The bottom line

Electricians should buy the Fluke 117: True RMS, NCV, AutoVolt and LoZ make it the right main meter for live electrical work. The Fluke 107 makes sense as a compact second meter, or for anyone who mainly checks batteries, outlets and continuity and values pocket size over True RMS.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 107 True RMS?+

No. Fluke's own answer is that the 107 is not a True RMS meter; it is average-responding. It reads clean sine waves correctly, but on the distorted waveforms from drives, dimmers and LED drivers it can read high or low. The 117 is True RMS, so its AC readings hold up on those loads.

Does the Fluke 107 have non-contact voltage detection?+

No. Fluke lists no NCV sensor for the 107, and it has no low-impedance mode either. The 117's VoltAlert lights an LED and beeps near a live AC conductor without contact. If you carry the 107 for wiring work, pair it with a separate pen-style voltage tester and always confirm with the test leads.

Are the Fluke 117 and 107 rated for the same work?+

Both are rated CAT III 600V, so both are suitable for panels, feeders and branch circuits at that voltage. Neither is rated CAT IV, so neither belongs at the meter base or service drop. The difference between them is in measurement features such as True RMS and LoZ, not in the safety category printed on the case.

Can the Fluke 107 measure current?+

Yes, AC and DC current on 4A and 10A ranges, with ±(1.5% + 3) accuracy. Fluke specifies AC current only from 40 Hz to 200 Hz on the 107, against 45 Hz to 500 Hz on the 117. Neither has a milliamp or microamp range, so both are too coarse for small electronics currents.

Is the Fluke 107 enough for home electrical testing?+

For outlets, batteries, fuses, cords and doorbell transformers, yes; its AC volts accuracy matches the 117's at ±(1.0% + 3). It lacks the 117's non-contact VoltAlert and LoZ mode, so on switched-off circuits it can show phantom voltage and can't flag a live cable without contact. If you replace outlets or fixtures yourself, the 117 is the safer pick.

Which Fluke is better for automotive work, the 117 or the 107?+

For most car work the 107 is enough. Automotive circuits are 12 V DC, where True RMS, VoltAlert and LoZ add little, and the 107 reads DC volts, DC current to 10A, resistance, diode and duty cycle for injector or PWM fan checks. Its 200 g body and 1 m drop rating also suit a glovebox.
