Siglent SDS814X HD vs SDS1104X-E: Is 12-bit Worth the Step?

Siglent SDS814X HD vs SDS1104X-E is Siglent against Siglent: two 100 MHz, four-channel bench scopes with the same probes, the same 80,000-frame history and the same 500 MSa/s per channel with all four on. The SDS814X HD adds a 12-bit converter, 2 GSa/s on one channel, up to 50 Mpts of memory and a 1024 × 600 touchscreen. The SDS1104X-E answers with a faster normal-mode update rate and years on the bench, including our owner's.

Spec by spec

The HD leads on detail and depth; the X-E holds its own on four-channel speed and update rate.

With all four on, full-rate record length is 20 ms on the HD (10 Mpts at 500 MSa/s) and 14 ms on the X-E (7 Mpts at 500 MSa/s). On one channel it is 25 ms on the HD (50 Mpts at 2 GSa/s) and 14 ms on the X-E. The HD is ahead everywhere on depth, but not by an order of magnitude.

What each one can and can't measure

Siglent SDS814X HD: small signals and long records

Siglent lists a 70 µVrms noise floor at 1 mV/div and full bandwidth, and a 12-bit converter. Together those make the HD the one for millivolt ripple, sensor noise and the shape of small signals sitting on large ones. Its 50 Mpts on a single channel suits slow events captured at a high rate, such as a power-up sequence. It is also the more modern interface: a capacitive touchscreen, mouse and keyboard support, and web control over LAN.

Siglent SDS1104X-E: fast, proven, 8 bits

Siglent's application note explains the X-E's architecture: two converters and two 14 Mpt memory modules, one per channel pair. That is how it keeps 500 MSa/s on all four channels. It also means memory depends on which channels you use. Put your two probes on CH1 and CH3 and each keeps 14 Mpts; put them on CH1 and CH2 and each gets 7 Mpts. On small signals its 8-bit converter steps in about 0.31 mV at 10 mV/div, so ripple is visible but coarse.

Use CH1 and CH3 on the SDS1104X-E

For two-channel work on the X-E, pick one channel from each pair. You keep 14 Mpts per channel instead of 7, at no cost. It is the single most useful habit for getting the X-E's full memory, and it comes straight from Siglent's own application note.

Earthed probe grounds on both

Both run from mains with probe grounds tied to earth. Clipping a ground lead to anything other than circuit ground on a mains-powered device causes a short through the scope. Use a differential probe for mains work, and stop if you are unsure which side of a circuit is earth referenced.

The question with the SDS814X HD: what "12-bit" delivers

Siglent deserves credit for publishing an ENOB figure at all: 8.4 bits typical, measured with a 24.99 MHz signal at 20 mV/div. Effective bits fall at higher frequencies and with noise, so a 12-bit converter rarely delivers 12 bits on a fast signal. At low frequencies and with bandwidth limiting, the HD's detail advantage over an 8-bit scope is real; on a 25 MHz signal it is smaller than the label suggests. Siglent doesn't publish an ENOB for the SDS1104X-E in the sources we checked, so the two can't be compared on that line. The X-E is the scope our owner tested; this page uses Siglent's figures only.

Choose the Siglent SDS814X HD if…

  • ✓You measure small signals on large ones: ripple, noise, sensor outputs
  • ✓You want long captures, with up to 50 Mpts on one channel
  • ✓You want a touchscreen and a 2 Mpts FFT
  • ✓You might add the SLA1016 logic probe or the SAG1021I USB generator later

Choose the Siglent SDS1104X-E if…

  • ✓You want the faster normal-mode update rate (100,000 wfm/s)
  • ✓You prefer a button-and-knob interface

Neither if…

You need more than 100 MHz. Siglent's SDS824X HD and the SDS1204X-E are the 200 MHz models in each line; neither is compared here.

The bottom line

Buy the Siglent SDS814X HD if you measure small signals, ripple or noise, or capture long events: 12 bits, a 70 µVrms noise floor, up to 50 Mpts and 2 GSa/s on one channel, on a touchscreen. Buy the Siglent SDS1104X-E if your work is mostly digital timing and four-channel debugging: it matches the HD's 500 MSa/s with four channels on, updates faster in normal mode, and it is the one our owner has tested.

Frequently asked questions

Is the SDS814X HD a replacement for the SDS1104X-E?+

Siglent sells both, and the SDS1104X-E was still in stock on Amazon when we checked. The HD is the newer design with 12 bits, deeper memory and a touchscreen. Siglent doesn't describe it as a replacement on the pages we used, so treat them as two current options at different price points.

What does ENOB 8.4 bits mean for the SDS814X HD?+

Effective number of bits measures how much real resolution survives noise and distortion. Siglent lists 8.4 bits typical with a 24.99 MHz input at 20 mV/div. At lower frequencies and with the 20 MHz bandwidth limit on, more of the 12 bits are usable, which is where the HD's detail advantage shows most.

Do both use the same probes?+

Yes. Siglent lists PP510 passive probes for the 100 MHz SDS800X HD models and for the 100 MHz SDS1000X-E models. That also means probes move between the two without losing bandwidth if you own one and add the other.

Can both add logic channels?+

Yes, both as options. The SDS814X HD takes the SLA1016 16-channel logic probe, which Siglent lists at up to 1 GSa/s and 10 Mpts. The four-channel SDS1104X-E takes Siglent's 16-channel mixed-signal option. Neither includes logic channels in the box.

Which one is better for a beginner?+

Both are reasonable. The SDS1104X-E has more third-party learning material and a straightforward button layout. The SDS814X HD's touchscreen and larger memory are easier to grow into. If budget allows and you expect to measure supplies and sensors, the HD's 12 bits will matter sooner than you think.
