The scope on a home bench rarely gets a single job. It might check the ripple on a bench supply, follow a signal through a guitar pedal, verify the timing of a microcontroller bus and then help repair a broken gadget, all in the same month. That makes a home lab oscilloscope different from a specialist tool: it has to be broadly capable, comfortable to use every day and good enough that you will not want to replace it as your projects grow. Our oscilloscope buying guide explains the core specs if you are starting from scratch.

For that reason we ranked these ten benchtop scopes on breadth rather than any single headline number. Four channels, 12-bit resolution, deep memory, a built-in signal generator and an upgrade path to logic channels each widen what one instrument can do; bandwidth matters, but most hobby circuits sit well below 100 MHz. Every pick is a mains or USB-C powered bench model sold on Amazon.com, from compact budget Hantek units to Rigol's 12-bit DHO900 series. Once your scope arrives, compensate the probes before anything else.

Mains safety on a home bench

A mains-powered bench oscilloscope ties every probe ground clip to earth. Never clip a ground lead onto a mains-referenced point or the primary side of an unisolated power supply: it creates a short through the scope. Use a differential probe or an isolation transformer rated for the job, and keep high-voltage work separate from everyday low-voltage projects.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Buy channels before bandwidth

A home lab sees varied work: an audio amplifier one weekend, a switching regulator the next, then a microcontroller bus. Four channels let you watch input, output, a supply rail and a trigger signal together, which is often how a fault finally reveals itself. Six of our ten picks have four analog channels. Most hobby circuits sit well inside 70 to 100 MHz, so extra channels usually pay off before extra bandwidth does.

Decide whether 12-bit matters to your projects

The Rigol DHO800 and DHO900 models digitise at 12 bits, giving sixteen times finer vertical steps than an 8-bit converter. That shows up when you look at small ripple on a supply rail, low-level audio or noise riding on a larger signal. If your work is mostly digital timing, an 8-bit scope such as the Siglent SDS1104X-E or a Hantek DSO2000 model serves you well and the difference matters less.

Count the instruments a built-in generator replaces

A signal source turns a scope into a test station: feed a sine wave into a filter, sweep an amplifier, or fake a sensor input. The DHO914S, DS1104Z-S Plus, DSO2D15 and DSO2D10 have one built in, and the DHO914S adds a Bode plot function that sweeps a circuit's frequency response automatically. If bench space or budget is tight, a combined unit avoids buying a separate function generator later.

Plan for logic channels if you build digital projects

Mixed-signal capability lets you watch a parallel bus or several GPIO lines alongside analog signals. The DHO914 and DHO914S include 16 digital channels that need the PLA2216 probe, the DS1104Z-S Plus can be upgraded with the RPL1116 kit, and Siglent sells an MSO option for the four-channel SDS1104X-E. None of these probes is in the box, so add the cost when you compare.

Mind the bench: power, screen and footprint

The Rigol DHO models run from a USB-C 15 V supply and are compact enough for a shelf-mounted bench; their 1024 × 600 touchscreens have more pixels than the 800 × 480 displays on the older Rigol, Siglent and Hantek designs. Mains-powered scopes are bulkier but need no external adapter. Whatever you choose, learn to compensate your probes before taking measurements you rely on.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is a 4-channel scope worth it for a home lab, or is 2 enough?+

For a general home lab, four channels are worth it. Varied projects regularly need more than two signals at once, such as a clock against data, an input against an output plus the supply rail, or a full SPI bus. Two-channel scopes like the Siglent SDS1202X-E or Rigol DHO802 make sense when bandwidth or budget matters more and your work rarely involves more than two related signals.

Should I buy the higher-bandwidth option now or upgrade later?+

Match bandwidth to the fastest signals you expect within a few years, not the fastest you can imagine. Audio, power supplies and microcontroller buses sit comfortably within 70 to 100 MHz. If faster digital logic or switching edges are on your roadmap, a 125 MHz DHO914 or 200 MHz SDS1202X-E avoids a second purchase. Otherwise, spend the difference on channels, memory or a generator.

Is a 12-bit oscilloscope worth it for a hobbyist?+

It is worth it if you measure small details on larger signals: power-supply ripple, audio distortion, sensor noise. Twelve bits give 4,096 vertical levels against 256 for an 8-bit converter, so fine detail is not lost in quantisation steps. Rigol's DHO800 and DHO900 scopes offer this at hobby prices. For mainly digital timing work, an 8-bit scope is still entirely adequate.

Do I need an oscilloscope with a built-in signal generator?+

Not strictly, but it is convenient. A generator lets you test filters, amplifiers and sensor inputs with a known waveform, and the DHO914S can use its generator for automatic Bode plots. If you already own a function generator, a scope without one, such as the DHO914 or DHO804, puts the money into the scope itself. If you own neither, a combined unit saves bench space.

What memory depth should a home lab oscilloscope have?+

Several million points is a sensible floor. Deep memory lets the scope keep a high sample rate on long time bases, so you can capture a full power-up sequence or a burst of serial traffic and zoom in without blurring. Our picks range from 8 Mpts on the Hantek DSO2000 models to 50 Mpts on the Rigol DHO900 models with one channel enabled.

Can I measure mains voltage with a bench oscilloscope?+

Not directly with standard probes. The ground clip on a mains-powered bench scope connects to earth, so clipping it onto a mains-referenced point creates a short circuit. Mains measurements need a differential probe or an isolation approach rated for the job. Learn on low-voltage circuits first, and treat anything connected to the wall outlet as a separate, safety-rated task.
