Fluke 175 vs Fluke 177: Which Multimeter Should You Choose?

Choose the Fluke 177 if you work in dim panels, attics or under dashboards, or if you want the tighter DC volts spec: Fluke lists ±(0.09% + 2) on the 177 against ±(0.15% + 2) on the 175, and the 177 has a backlit display. Everywhere else Fluke's spec tables match line for line: True RMS AC volts and amps, 1000 V, 10 A, 50 MΩ, 10,000 µF, 100 kHz, CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, the same 420 g case and a lifetime warranty. If you work in good light and your DC readings don't need better than about a sixth of a percent, the 175 does the same job.

What 0.09% vs 0.15% means on a real reading

An accuracy spec such as ±(0.15% + 2) has two parts. The first is a share of whatever the display shows. The second is a fixed number of counts, meaning steps of the last digit on the range in use. Both meters use "+ 2", so the gap between them is the percentage term, and that term grows with the reading.

Worked through for common DC jobs, the percentage term alone gives these figures:

DC reading175 (0.15%)177 (0.09%)Gap
1.5 V AA cell±2.3 mV±1.4 mV0.9 mV
12.6 V lead-acid battery±18.9 mV±11.3 mV7.6 mV
24 V control supply±36 mV±21.6 mV14.4 mV
48 V battery bank±72 mV±43.2 mV28.8 mV
400 V DC string (600 V range)±0.60 V±0.40 V (0.1% on this range)0.20 V

Fluke's 170 Series datasheet confirms both meters use the same 6,000-count display, which lets us finish the arithmetic. 12.6 V lands on the 60.00 V range, which steps in 0.01 V, so the two counts add 0.02 V on either meter: about ±0.039 V in total on the 175 and ±0.031 V on the 177. Either way, the spread on a car battery is tens of millivolts. The difference between a charged and a half-discharged lead-acid battery is a few hundred millivolts, so either meter makes that call easily.

The tighter spec starts to matter when you are checking something with its own tight tolerance. A common rule of thumb is that the meter should be several times more accurate than the thing being judged. Checking a 0.5% regulator output or a sensor excitation voltage against a datasheet limit leaves more margin at 0.09% than at 0.15%. For a broader explanation, read our guide to multimeter accuracy for electronics repair.

A few measurement details apply to both meters and are worth knowing before you buy either. Min/Max/Avg is specified at ±12 counts for DC changes lasting longer than 275 ms and ±40 counts for AC changes longer than 1.2 s, so it suits sags and slow drifts rather than spikes. The continuity beeper catches opens or shorts as brief as 250 µs, which makes a wiggle test on a cord or harness useful. Above 1,000 µF the capacitance reading is a 10% typical figure, which is fine for a sanity check on a big filter capacitor but too loose to grade one against its tolerance. In the mA and A ranges, frequency is specified only to 30 kHz.

Two source conflicts to note. Fluke's product pages give the frequency ceiling as 100 kHz and the range table runs to 99.99 kHz, while the datasheet's nominal summary says 2 Hz to 50 kHz; we follow the range table. The product pages list capacitance to 10,000 µF, the datasheet's top range is 9,999 µF. Neither conflict separates the 175 from the 177, because both models share those lines.

Do you need the backlight?

The backlight is the difference you notice most in daily use. Fluke describes the 177's display as having a backlight for poorly lit areas. The 175's page doesn't mention one, Fluke's 170 Series datasheet lists the backlight for the 177 and 179 only, and the 175's Amazon photo shows no backlight key on the front panel. Think about where you will be holding the meter:

Battery life is the trade-off. Fluke quotes about 200 hours from an alkaline 9 V for both meters, and both pages qualify the figure as "without backlight", the same wording even on the 175. Fluke doesn't say how much the light shortens it, but any display light draws extra current, so a 177 owner who leaves it on will change batteries more often than a 175 owner. A headlamp gets a 175 owner through the same jobs, at the cost of a free hand.

What both meters share, and what neither does

Most of what you pay for in a 170-series Fluke is common to both. AC readings are True RMS, so the readings from LED drivers, switch-mode supplies and drive inputs stay within the published spec instead of drifting the way an average-responding meter's do. Both accept 1000 V AC or DC, carry CAT IV 600V for service-entrance work, resolve current to 0.01 mA on the low range and take 10 A on a separate jack. Capacitance runs to 10,000 µF, enough for large motor-start and power-supply capacitors, and frequency runs to 100 kHz.

The gaps are shared too. Fluke lists no temperature function on either; that is what the Fluke 179 adds over the 177. Neither has a low-impedance (LoZ) input for ghost voltage or a non-contact sensor; for those, compare the Fluke 177 with the 117. Neither lists a low-pass filter for the motor side of a variable-frequency drive, so for that work see Fluke 87V vs 179.

Same case, same warranty, one real upgrade

The 175 and 177 share the same 43 × 90 × 185 mm, 420 g body, the same ratings and the same lifetime warranty. So the decision comes down to one feature you can see (the backlight) and one you can calculate (40% less percentage error on DC volts). If neither matters for your work, the 175 is not a compromise.

Safety: the rating only counts if the leads match it

Both meters are rated CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V, but a measurement is only as safe as its lowest-rated part. Use test leads rated at least as high as the circuit, keep fingers behind the probe guards, and pull the red lead out of the 10 A or 400 mA jack before measuring voltage, because a lead left in a current jack shorts a live circuit through the meter's fuse. Prove a circuit dead with the live-dead-live check before touching it. Leave work at the service entrance to a licensed electrician unless it is your trade.

  • ✓You check DC sources against a tight tolerance and want the 0.09% basic DC accuracy
  • ✓You want the 170 Series backlight without paying for the 179's thermometer

Choose the Fluke 175 if…

  • ✓You mostly work in daylight or a well-lit shop and won't miss a backlight
  • ✓Your DC work is batteries, supplies and control voltages, where 0.15% is already far finer than the job needs
  • ✓You want the CAT IV 600V rating and lifetime warranty of the 170 series at the lowest entry point

The bottom line

The Fluke 177 is the better meter, but only by two things: a backlit display and DC volts accuracy of ±(0.09% + 2) against the 175's ±(0.15% + 2). If you work in poor light or check DC against tight limits, buy the 177. If you work in good light and your DC readings are batteries, supplies and controls, the 175 gives you the same True RMS ranges, CAT IV 600V rating, 420 g body and lifetime warranty.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Fluke 175 and 177?+

Two things, per Fluke's spec pages. The 177 has a backlit display, and its DC volts accuracy is ±(0.09% + 2) against the 175's ±(0.15% + 2). Every other published line matches, including True RMS AC, 10 A current, 50 MΩ, 10,000 µF, 100 kHz, the CAT IV 600V rating, the 420 g body and the lifetime warranty.

Is the Fluke 177 more accurate than the 175 on every range?+

Only on DC volts, and not by the same margin on every range. Fluke's 170 Series datasheet gives the 177 ±(0.09% + 2) from 600 mV to 60 V and ±(0.1% + 2) on the 600 V and 1000 V ranges, against ±(0.15% + 2) throughout on the 175. AC volts, current, resistance, capacitance, frequency and diode test carry identical specs on both.

Does the Fluke 175 have a backlight?+

No. Fluke's 170 Series detailed specifications list the backlight as a 177 and 179 feature only, the 175 product page never describes a lit display, and the main photo on the official Amazon listing shows no backlight key, while the 177's photo does. If you often read the meter in a dark panel or crawlspace, the 177 is the safer choice.

How many counts is the Fluke 175 display?+

6,000 counts, the same as the 177. The 175 product page doesn't say, but Fluke's 170 Series detailed specifications give 6,000 counts at four updates a second and a 33-segment bar graph at 40 updates a second, and state that every spec other than the backlight, DC volts accuracy and temperature is shared by the 175, 177 and 179.

Is the Fluke 177 worth the upgrade from the 175?+

It is if you work in dim places or need tighter DC readings. The backlight saves holding a flashlight in panels, attics and footwells, and 0.09% DC accuracy leaves more margin when checking regulated supplies. If your work is in good light and you read batteries and control voltages, the 175 measures the same things with the same safety rating.

Can the Fluke 175 or 177 measure temperature?+

No. Fluke lists temperature only on the Fluke 179 in this series, with a −40 °C to 400 °C range and an 80BK probe in the box. The 175 and 177 have no thermocouple input. If you do HVAC work, compare the 177 and 179, which otherwise share the same accuracy table.

Are the Fluke 175 and 177 safe for electrical panels?+

Yes, within their ratings. Both carry CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, which covers distribution panels and the service entrance on systems up to 600 V to ground. Use leads with a matching rating, check the meter on a known live source before and after testing, and leave service-entrance work to qualified electricians.
