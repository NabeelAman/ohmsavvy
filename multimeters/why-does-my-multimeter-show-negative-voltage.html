HOW-TO GUIDE

Why Does My Multimeter Show Negative Voltage?

On DC voltage mode, a negative sign simply means the probes are connected the opposite way round from the circuit's actual polarity at that point — the red probe is touching somewhere at a lower potential than the black probe. It's not a fault, and on a digital meter it causes no damage; the magnitude of the reading is identical either way, only the sign flips. The exceptions worth knowing are leads plugged into the wrong jacks, a stored REL offset, circuits with a deliberately negative rail, and real reverse-polarity wiring, all covered below.

What the negative sign actually means

A digital multimeter on DC voltage mode reports the potential at the red probe relative to the black probe. When the red probe is at a higher potential, the display reads positive; when it's at a lower potential than the black probe, the display reads negative. Swapping the two probes on the exact same two points simply flips the sign — the magnitude stays identical.

A useful way to remember it: the meter subtracts. It takes the voltage at the red probe, subtracts the voltage at the black probe, and shows the answer with its sign. Red at 12 V and black at 0 V gives 12 − 0 = +12. Red at 0 V and black at 12 V gives 0 − 12 = −12. Nothing else in the meter is involved, which is why the minus sign is information about your probe placement rather than about the health of the circuit.

Safety: batteries, solar and mains

  • A minus sign on a car battery, battery bank or solar string usually means swapped probes, but if it persists with the probes correct, the wiring may be reversed. Do not connect chargers, inverters or jump leads until polarity is confirmed; reversed connections on high-current batteries can cause sparks, burns and damaged equipment.
  • Keep fingers behind the probe guards and use a meter rated for the location (CAT III 600V or better for household circuits). Lithium packs, battery banks and rooftop solar wiring with suspected reverse polarity are work for a qualified installer.

1Check which jack each lead is in

Before thinking about the circuit, look at the meter. The black lead belongs in COM and the red lead in the jack marked V. Lead colors are a convention, not wiring: if the black lead is plugged into V and the red into COM, every DC reading you take will carry a minus sign even when the red probe is on the positive terminal. It is an easy mistake when leads are swapped between meters or after a current test. If you are unsure which symbol marks which jack, the multimeter symbols guide shows them.

2Swap the probes on the same points

Hold the two tips on exactly the same points and swap them. A reading of −12.6 V that becomes +12.6 V proves the meter is working and the circuit is behaving normally; you were reading it backward. If the number changes in size as well as sign, one of the tips was not making good contact, so clean the contact points and repeat.

About 12.6 V is the commonly quoted resting value for a fully charged 12 V lead-acid battery; our guide to testing a car battery with a multimeter explains what lower readings mean.

3Clear REL and offsets

REL (relative mode, often marked with a delta symbol) stores a reading and subtracts it from every reading that follows. Press it while measuring a 12.00 V battery, then measure a 5.00 V USB supply, and the display shows 5.00 − 12.00 = −7.00 V. The meter is doing exactly what it was told. Look for the REL or Δ icon and press the button again, or turn the dial off and on, to return to absolute readings. The same button causes a flat zero in a different situation, described in why a multimeter reads 0 volts on a working circuit.

4Identify the reference point

Ask what the black probe is touching. In a car, clipping COM to the battery positive and probing a ground wire gives a negative reading of about the battery voltage, which is correct for that arrangement. On a board, clipping COM to the wrong rail does the same. Choose one reference, usually the supply negative or the circuit's marked ground, keep the black probe there for the whole session, and every reading becomes directly comparable with the schematic. Isolated sections with no shared ground behave differently again; see measuring voltage on a floating ground circuit.

5Compare with the expected polarity

With the jacks right, REL off and the black probe on the reference, the sign is now meaningful. Compare it with what the device should have at that point: a label, a schematic, or the markings on a connector, battery or panel. A match ends the investigation. A mismatch, such as a supply that should be +12 V reading −12 V to its own ground, means the wiring is reversed somewhere between the source and that point. Measure at the source terminals first, then at each connector toward the device, until the sign changes; the reversal is between those two points.

Negative by design: split supplies

Some circuits need a voltage below ground. Op-amp and audio circuits often run from a split supply, such as ±12 V or ±15 V, with ground in the middle. Digital meters vs. old analog meters

This is one area where digital meters are simply easier to use than the moving-coil analog meters they've mostly replaced. An analog meter's needle physically deflects in one direction only; reversed polarity drives it hard against the mechanical stop, which can damage the movement on some ranges. A digital meter has no such risk — it just displays a minus sign and the correct magnitude, regardless of which way the probes are connected.

Why AC mode never shows a negative sign

AC voltage is, by definition, constantly reversing direction many times a second, so a meaningful single sign doesn't apply the way it does to a steady DC level. AC voltage mode reports a magnitude (typically the RMS value) with no sign at all — if you're expecting to see "negative AC," that's not how the measurement works. The AC vs DC voltage guide explains the difference, and our oscilloscope vs multimeter guide covers seeing the actual shape of a signal that reverses over time.

When a negative reading does signal a problem

Treat it as a real issue only when it contradicts what the circuit is supposed to do at that exact point — for example, a supply rail that a schematic specifies as positive relative to ground reading persistently negative, or a reading that flips unexpectedly compared to a known-good reference unit. In those cases the polarity itself is informative and worth following up, rather than just a result of probe placement. If the sign is right but the size of the number is off, that is a different question, answered in why a multimeter shows the wrong voltage.

The quick check

If a negative reading surprises you, swap the two probes on the same two points before assuming anything is wrong. If the sign simply flips back to positive with the same magnitude, the circuit is behaving normally — you were just reading it backward. Then confirm the black lead is in COM, REL is off, and the black probe sits on the circuit's own reference.

Frequently asked questions

Does a negative voltage reading mean my circuit is broken?

Not by itself. It only tells you which probe is at the higher potential — the circuit can be working exactly as designed. It's worth investigating further only if the sign or magnitude contradicts what the circuit's documentation or schematic says you should see at that point.

Can reversed DC probes damage my multimeter?

No, on a standard digital multimeter. The input circuitry handles either polarity on DC voltage mode without any risk — this is specifically one of the advantages digital meters have over old moving-coil analog meters, which could be damaged by a hard reverse-polarity deflection on some ranges.

Why is my multimeter showing negative voltage on a car battery?

The red probe is on the negative post and the black on the positive, or the leads are plugged into the wrong jacks, so a healthy battery shows something like -12.6 V. Swap the probes: if it reads +12.6 V, the battery is fine. A battery that truly reads reversed with the probes correct has been charged backward and should be checked by a professional.

Why did my reading flip from positive to negative when I touched a different point?

The reading is always the red probe's potential relative to the black probe. Move the red probe from a point above the black probe's potential to one below it, such as from a 12V rail to a point on the negative side of a load, and the sign flips. In a dual-supply circuit, anything on the negative rail reads negative to ground by design.

Is there a difference between -5V and 5V with reversed probes?

Electrically, no — both readings describe the exact same potential difference, just referenced from opposite probes. If a schematic specifically calls for a negative supply rail (common in op-amp circuits needing a dual supply), that's a genuinely different, intentionally negative voltage relative to the circuit's own ground — not just a probe-reversal artifact.

Why does my multimeter show a tiny negative voltage with nothing connected?

A reading of a few counts, such as -0.002 V or -0.3 mV, with open or shorted leads is normal offset and pickup on the input, not a real voltage. It sits within the meter's least-significant-digit tolerance. Short the probes together: if the display settles near zero, the meter is fine. A reading that stays far from zero points to a damaged input or low battery.

Why does my multimeter show negative current?

On the DC current function the sign shows which way current flows through the meter. Negative means it enters through COM and leaves through the current jack, the reverse of the direction you assumed. On a battery that can charge and discharge, the sign tells you which one is happening. Swap the leads if you prefer positive readings, but note which way you connected them.
