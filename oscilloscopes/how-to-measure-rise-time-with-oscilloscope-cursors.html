How to Measure Rise Time With Oscilloscope Cursors

Rise time is conventionally defined as the time a signal takes to go from 10% to 90% of its final amplitude on a rising edge — not the full 0% to 100% transition. Measuring it with cursors means finding those two specific amplitude levels, placing time cursors where the edge crosses each one, and reading the time difference the scope calculates between them.

What rise time actually measures

Rise time is the time between a rising edge crossing 10% of its final amplitude and crossing 90% of it — a standard convention, not the full low-to-high transition.

Why you'll likely need to zoom in

Rise time is usually a tiny fraction of a signal's overall period, especially on fast digital edges — at a time/div setting that shows several complete cycles, the edge itself can be just a sliver, too narrow to place cursors on accurately. Increase the horizontal scale (a faster time/div) or use a zoom/delay feature if your scope has one, so the edge itself fills a good portion of the screen before placing cursors.

1Measuring rise time step by step

Zoom in on the edge so it's clearly visible. Identify the flat levels before and after the transition (the 0% and 100% reference points), and calculate the 10% and 90% levels from that span. Place one time cursor where the edge crosses the 10% level, and the second where it crosses the 90% level. Read the time difference the scope displays between the two cursors — that's the rise time.

Fall time works the same way, in reverse

Fall time uses the same 10%/90% convention on a falling edge, just measured from the 90% crossing down to the 10% crossing instead. The cursor placement technique and zoom considerations are identical — only which edge you're measuring changes.

Your scope's own rise time sets a hard limit

A scope (and probe) can only display an edge as fast as its own rated rise time allows — a measurement faster than that limit gets rounded off and reads artificially slow. See our bandwidth guide for the relationship between bandwidth and a scope's own rise time, and the margin you need over the signal you're measuring.

Common mistakes

MistakeResult
Placing cursors at 0%/100% instead of 10%/90%Measures a different, non-standard quantity than conventional rise time
Not zoomed in enough on the edgeImprecise cursor placement on a sliver of screen width
Reference levels taken from a noisy or non-flat baseline10%/90% calculation is off from the start

Cursors measure what the scope shows, not the edge itself

A cursor reading is only as fast as the probe, front end and sample rate let the edge appear. Set the 10% and 90% levels from flat, settled parts of the waveform, zoom until the edge spans several divisions, and compare the result with the scope's own rise time before you trust it.

Scopes for edge measurements

Using the common 0.35 ÷ bandwidth approximation, a 50 MHz scope settles at about 7 ns of its own rise time, 70 MHz at about 5 ns and 100 MHz at about 3.5 ns. Pick the bandwidth that keeps your scope well faster than the edges you need to measure.

Frequently asked questions

Why 10% to 90% instead of the full 0% to 100% transition?

The very start and end of a real edge often have a curved, less well-defined transition region that makes exact 0%/100% crossing points hard to pin down consistently. The 10%-90% convention gives a standard, repeatable measurement that's widely used across datasheets and test equipment, which is why it's the default most scopes' automatic rise-time measurement uses too.

Can I just use the scope's automatic rise-time measurement instead of cursors?

Yes, most digital scopes calculate it automatically in the Measure menu using the same 10%-90% convention. Manual cursors are useful for verifying that automatic reading, understanding exactly what's being measured, or when a specific noisy or unusual edge makes you want to confirm the measurement by eye.

Does the trigger point affect where I place the cursors?

Not directly — the trigger just needs to give you a stable, non-moving display of the edge so you can place cursors accurately. Where you place the actual cursors depends only on the 10% and 90% amplitude levels and where the waveform crosses them, independent of the trigger settings themselves.

Why does my measured rise time seem slower than the signal source should produce?

Check your probe and scope's own bandwidth and rise time specs first — a scope or probe with insufficient bandwidth rounds off fast edges, making the measured rise time look slower than the signal actually is at its source. This is covered in more depth in our oscilloscope bandwidth guide.

How do I correct a measured rise time for my scope's own rise time?

Rise times add roughly as a root sum of squares, so the signal's own value is about √(measured² − scope²). A 100 MHz scope has a system rise time near 0.35 ÷ 100 MHz = 3.5 ns. If it shows 5 ns, the edge is about √(25 − 12.25) ≈ 3.6 ns. The correction becomes unreliable once the measured value is under about twice the scope's own figure.
