Fluke 115 vs Klein MM400

The Fluke 115 vs Klein MM400 question comes down to accuracy versus extras. Both are auto-ranging, CAT III 600V meters for household and light commercial electrical work, but only the Fluke is True RMS, and its 6,000-count display resolves finer steps. The Klein answers with a temperature input, duty cycle and a lighter body, and Klein has now discontinued it.

Display resolution and capacitance range compared

Two numbers separate these meters more than any others. Display counts decide how finely a reading is shown: on its 6V range the Fluke shows 5.999 V, where a 4,000-count meter switches ranges and shows fewer digits sooner. Capacitance range decides which components you can test at all.

In practice the count difference shows up on 5 V logic rails and small batteries. A 4,000-count meter tops out at 3.999 on its lowest volts range, so a 4.5 V or 5 V reading drops to the next range up, typically with one fewer decimal place. The capacitance gap is more decisive: an HVAC dual run capacitor sits well inside both meters, but the large electrolytics in amplifier and inverter power supplies often exceed the MM400's 200 µF limit, and then it simply shows an overload.

Why True RMS changes the reading on modern loads

An average-responding meter rectifies the AC signal, takes its average and multiplies by 1.11, the ratio between RMS and average that holds only for a pure sine. A True RMS meter like the Fluke 115 computes the heating value of whatever waveform it sees, so it needs no assumption about shape. On a clean utility sine the two agree; on the current drawn by a switch-mode power supply, a dimmer or a drive they don't.

The arithmetic behind the diagram: take rectangular current pulses of height P that are on for 25% of the time. The true RMS value is P × √0.25 = 0.50 P. The rectified average is 0.25 P, and an average-responding meter displays 1.11 × 0.25 P ≈ 0.28 P. That is a 44% under-read, enough to make an overloaded circuit look comfortable. Real loads are rarely this extreme, but a computer power supply or LED driver draws narrow current peaks of this general shape. The reverse error happens too: on a square wave the average equals the RMS, so the 1.11 factor makes an average-responding meter read about 11% high.

When the MM400 is still accurate enough

On resistive loads such as heaters, incandescent lamps and plain utility voltage at the outlet, the waveform is close to a sine and the MM400's reading is trustworthy. The gap opens on electronic loads, which is why True RMS matters most to electricians and technicians working on drives, lighting and HVAC equipment with variable-speed blowers. Neither meter suits HVAC flame-sensor work: the 115 has no microamp range at all, and Klein's product page lists no dedicated µA range for the MM400 either. For that job, see our best multimeters for measuring microamps or the Fluke 116 discussed in Fluke 115 vs Fluke 117.

Buying a discontinued meter

Klein's own page says the MM400 is no longer in production and points buyers to the MM420. On Amazon it is still sold through third-party offers, so stock and condition vary by seller. That isn't a reason to avoid it outright, since replacement 10 A and 500 mA fuses are still listed by Klein, but it makes the price the deciding factor: the MM400 is only good value at a clear discount to the Fluke. The 115, by contrast, is a current Fluke model that carries our Hands-on Tested badge; read the Fluke 115 review and the Klein MM400 review for the full picture.

Same CAT rating, same limits

Both meters are rated CAT III 600V, which covers branch circuits, receptacles and distribution panels but not the service drop or meter base, where CAT IV applies. Check either meter on a known live source before and after you test a circuit you plan to touch, and call a licensed electrician for anything at the service entrance.

Choose the Fluke 115 if…

  • ✓You measure on variable-speed drives, LED drivers or other electronic loads, where True RMS matters
  • ✓You want a 6,000-count display and a 33-segment bar graph for changing readings
  • ✓You want a model that is still in production, with a 3-year warranty

Choose the Klein Tools MM400 if…

  • ✓You want temperature readings from the included thermocouple
  • ✓You want duty cycle as well as frequency for checking PWM controls
  • ✓You find one at a clear discount and mostly measure ordinary sine-wave mains

The bottom line

For most buyers the Fluke 115 is the better meter: True RMS, a finer 6,000-count display, far wider capacitance range and a current model with a 3-year warranty. The Klein MM400 makes sense only if you need its temperature input or duty cycle and can buy one at a clear discount before stock runs out.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Klein MM400 True RMS like the Fluke 115?+

No. Klein's multimeter feature chart lists True RMS only for the MM700 in that range, so the MM400 is average-responding. The Fluke 115 is True RMS, which makes it the safer choice on variable-speed drives, LED lighting and other electronic loads, where an average-responding meter can read well off the true value.

Which is lighter, the Fluke 115 or the Klein MM400?+

The Klein MM400, at 232 g against the Fluke's 550 g per each maker's specifications. The Fluke runs on a single 9V battery rated for about 400 hours typical; the Klein uses two AAA cells, and Klein doesn't publish a battery life figure on the product page we checked.

Can the Fluke 115 measure temperature?+

No. Fluke's 114/115/116/117 datasheet lists the K-type thermocouple function for the Fluke 116 only, even though a comparison table on Fluke's product pages suggests otherwise. Don't buy the 115 for temperature work. The Klein MM400 includes a thermocouple and reads 0°F to 1000°F (-18°C to 538°C).

Is the Klein MM400 still made?+

No. Klein lists it as no longer in production and points to the MM420 as its replacement, so remaining stock comes from third-party sellers and may be limited. The Fluke 115 is a current model with a 3-year warranty, which matters if you want parts and support for years to come.

Which meter is better for HVAC work?+

It depends on the task. The MM400's thermocouple helps with supply and return air and line temperatures. The Fluke 115's True RMS and 9,999 µF capacitance range help with variable-speed blowers and run capacitors. Neither has a dedicated microamp range for flame sensors, so see our HVAC multimeter guide for meters that do.
