Fluke 113 vs Fluke 117: Which Is Better for Electrical Testing?

For general electrical testing the Fluke 117 is the better meter, because it measures current, reads volts with or without a low-impedance load and adds VoltAlert non-contact detection. The Fluke 113 is not a cheaper 117. It is a verification tool: its single VCHEK position proves a conductor live, dead or carrying phantom voltage, Fluke rates it CAT IV 600V, and it has no amps at all. Buy it only when proving power wiring is the whole job.

What the Fluke 113 is actually for

Most people who search for the 113 assume it sits below the 117 on a ladder of electrician's meters. It doesn't. Fluke sells it as a utility meter, and its dial is shaped around a single question asked over and over: is this conductor energized, and if not, is it the one I think it is? The 117 answers that question too, then goes on to measure what the circuit is doing.

113: the job

Prove live, dead or ghost; then confirm the conductor by continuity.

117: the job

Prove the circuit, then measure the load: amps, Hz, capacitors.

113: the edge

CAT IV 600V per Fluke, 404 g, one-position VCHEK.

117: the edge

Two volts inputs, a 10 A range and VoltAlert built in.

Shared

LoZ against phantom voltage, 6,000 counts, backlight, 9 V battery.

The Fluke 117 is one of the meters the site owner has bought and used, so it carries our Hands-on Tested badge, and the Fluke 117 review goes into it in depth. Both have LoZ; only the 117 lets you switch it off

This is the difference that decides the pair, and spec tables bury it. Fluke lists a low-impedance input of about 3 kΩ on the 113's VCHEK position and on the 117's AutoVolt position, with the same ±(2.0% + 3) accuracy line. On a shared conduit full of abandoned conductors, the two behave alike: coupled voltage drains through the meter and a real supply stays put.

The 117 then has a second choice the 113 lacks. Turn one click to V AC or V DC and the input rises above 5 MΩ or 10 MΩ, so the meter barely disturbs what it reads. That matters on anything that isn't a stiff mains supply: a thermostat output, a doorbell transformer under load, a photocell or occupancy-sensor lead, a battery bank feeding a small inverter. On the 113 those readings all pass through 3 kΩ, and Fluke adds that its autorange won't register below 3 V DC, so low-voltage signals are largely out of reach.

LoZ also draws real current, and Fluke's 113 page warns that phase-to-earth LoZ checks can trip a GFCI or RCD. The same physics applies to the 117's AutoVolt position. The difference is that a 117 user can make the hot-to-ground check on V AC instead, while a 113 user has to test hot to neutral or reset the device afterward. Our explainer on why a GFCI keeps tripping covers the trip threshold.

The 113's real advantage: CAT IV 600V

Measurement category describes how much transient energy a meter is built to survive at a point in the supply. The closer you work to the utility, the larger the possible surge. Fluke lists the 113 under IEC 61010-2-033 at CAT IV 600V, and the meter body in the listing photo is printed the same. The 117 is rated CAT III 600V.

A verification routine with each meter

Picture opening a disconnect to isolate a load and proving it dead before work. With the 113 the routine never leaves one dial position: check the meter on a known live source, test each conductor on the load side, and when nothing is there the same position confirms the conductor by continuity. With the 117 you can sweep the enclosure with VoltAlert before the leads come out, then use AutoVolt for the contact checks.

The routine is the same on both, and that is the point: for verification alone, the 113 gives up nothing important. The gap opens the moment the job turns into diagnosis. A small motor that trips its overload needs a current reading, and a portable generator holding the wrong speed needs a frequency reading; the 117 takes both and the 113 lists neither. Where the published specs disagree

Read these before you buy

On the 113, Fluke and Amazon disagree on the safety rating (CAT IV 600V against CAT III 600V / CAT IV 300V), True RMS appears on the meter label and Amazon title but only in a footnote on Fluke's spec tab, and Fluke states no warranty term for a new unit (its reconditioned R-FLUKE-113 lists 3 years). On the 117, Fluke's 2020 users manual lists a 99.99 kHz frequency range that the datasheet's 50.00 kHz ceiling doesn't show; the 117's 3-year warranty is published.

Safety: a LoZ reading near zero is not permission to touch

Both meters drain phantom voltage on their low-impedance positions, which makes a near-zero reading more meaningful but still not proof of isolation. Prove the meter on a known live source before and after, follow your lockout procedure, and keep the 117 off the service drop and meter base, where its CAT III 600V rating doesn't apply. VoltAlert silence on the 117 is not proof of dead either. If you aren't qualified to work inside panels or at the service entrance, call a licensed electrician or the utility.

Choose the Fluke 113 if…

  • ✓Your job is proving conductors live or dead, not diagnosing the loads on them
  • ✓You need Fluke's CAT IV 600V rating for work at the origin of the installation
  • ✓You already carry a clamp meter for every current reading

Choose the Fluke 117 if…

  • ✓You troubleshoot circuits and equipment, not just verify them
  • ✓You want both a LoZ check and an ordinary high-impedance volts reading on one dial
  • ✓You want current, frequency and non-contact detection without a second tool

The bottom line

The Fluke 117 is the better meter for electrical testing in general: it reads volts through either a LoZ or a high-impedance input, measures current to 10 A, and adds VoltAlert and frequency. The Fluke 113 is the right choice only for verification work, where its one-position VCHEK check, Fluke-stated CAT IV 600V rating and lighter body matter and a clamp meter handles every amp reading.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Fluke 113 used for?+

It is a utility meter for proving conductors live or dead. Its VCHEK position reads voltage through a low input impedance of about 3 kΩ and switches to a continuity check when nothing is there. It also reads resistance to 60 kΩ, capacitance and diodes, but it has no current ranges, so it is a verification tool rather than a troubleshooting meter.

Do the Fluke 113 and 117 both have LoZ?+

Yes. Fluke lists about 3 kΩ on the 113's VCHEK position and on the 117's AutoVolt position, with the same ±(2.0% + 3) accuracy. The difference is that the 113 has no other way to read volts, while the 117 also has normal V AC and V DC positions above 5 MΩ and 10 MΩ for circuits a low-impedance input would pull down.

Which is safer to use at the service entrance?+

The Fluke 113, on paper. Fluke rates it CAT IV 600V, which covers the meter base and service conductors, and the 117 is rated CAT III 600V, which stops at the distribution panel. One Amazon bullet for the 113 quotes CAT IV 300V instead, so confirm the rating printed on the meter you receive.

Can a Fluke 113 replace a Fluke 117 for an electrician?+

Only for part of the work. The 113 handles live-dead checks, ghost voltage and continuity, but it can't measure current, frequency or resistance above 60 kΩ, and it has no non-contact sensor. An electrician who also carries a clamp meter and a pen tester can manage with it; most service electricians are better served by the 117.

Does the Fluke 113 have non-contact voltage detection?+

Fluke's 113 spec tab lists no non-contact function, so plan on a separate pen tester. In this pair, only the 117 has it: VoltAlert, a sensor in the top edge with Lo and Hi sensitivity settings. A silent non-contact check is never proof that a conductor is dead, on either tool.

Can the Fluke 113 or 117 measure temperature?+

No. Neither meter lists a temperature function. Fluke's 110 Series datasheet gives the Type K thermocouple input to the Fluke 116 only, which also adds a 600.0 µA range for furnace flame sensors. If you need temperature and current on one meter, look outside the 110 Series at a model such as the Fluke 179.
