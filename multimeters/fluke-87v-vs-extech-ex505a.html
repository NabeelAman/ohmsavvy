Fluke 87V vs Extech EX505A: Which Is Better for Industrial Testing?

Both meters carry the same CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating, so the choice comes down to the work itself. Plant technicians who troubleshoot variable-frequency drives, calibrate 4–20 mA loops or chase intermittent faults should buy the Fluke 87V: it has a low-pass filter, 250 µs peak capture and DC accuracy ten times tighter. Buy the Extech EX505A for washdown areas, outdoor yards and rough sites, where its IP67 housing matters more than precision. The 87V cost nearly four times as much on Amazon when we checked.

A 480 V drive running a conveyor at 40 Hz

This is the job that splits the pair. A drive feeding a 480 V motor at reduced speed produces a PWM waveform, not a sine wave, at a fundamental below 60 Hz. Fluke built the 87V for it: a low-pass filter rolls off at 3 dB at 1 kHz so the meter reports something close to the fundamental the motor sees. Extech's manual for the EX505A specifies AC volts from 50 Hz to 1 kHz for sine waves and at 50/60 Hz for all other waveforms, and the datasheet marks the low-pass filter as absent on this model.

Put those together and a 40 Hz PWM output falls outside the EX505A's specification twice over: it is below 50 Hz, and it isn't a sine. The meter will display a number, but Extech makes no accuracy claim for it. The 87V, with its filter, gives a reading you can compare phase to phase and against the drive's own display. We cover drive measurement on the 87V in more depth in Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179, which is the comparison to read if drives are your main job.

If you want Extech with a filter

Extech's datasheet gives LoZ and the low-pass filter to the EX520A and EX530A in the same IP67 housing. Neither is on our verified list yet, so we can't recommend one here; check that model's datasheet before assuming the EX505A has the same functions.

A 4–20 mA loop and a 24 V DC supply: where 0.05% shows

Instrument technicians feel the accuracy gap more than electricians do. Worked on each maker's spec for a transmitter reading 12.00 mA: the 87V's 60 mA range at ±(0.2% + 4 digits) allows ±(0.024 + 0.04) = ±0.064 mA. Extech's manual table groups its DC current ranges at ±(1.0% + 3) and ±(1.5% + 3), and the layout doesn't make clear which applies to 60.00 mA, so we take the looser figure: ±(0.18 + 0.03) = ±0.21 mA. Across a 16 mA span that is 0.4% of span on the Fluke against 1.3% on the Extech.

The same pattern holds on a 24.00 V control supply read on the 60 V range: the 87V allows ±(0.012 + 0.01) = ±0.022 V and the EX505A ±(0.12 + 0.05) = ±0.17 V. Both are fine for "is the supply up?". Only the Fluke is tight enough to judge a supply against a ±1% tolerance with margin.

For loop work specifically, our 4–20 mA loop testing picks include process meters that source as well as measure.

Washdown, rain and drops: what IP67 buys

The EX505A's strongest card is its housing. Extech says it is double-molded, rated IP67 (tested by Bureau Veritas, per the manual) and drop-tested to 2 m. IP67 means dust-tight and protected against temporary immersion. Fluke's comparison table lists the standard 87V at IP30, which means no rated water protection, and Fluke's own pages differ on its drop rating: 3 m in the comparison table, 1 m in the spec tab's shock line.

On a food plant's washdown floor, a wastewater site or an outdoor substation yard, that difference decides more than any accuracy figure. The EX505A also comes with a carrying case, magnetic hanging strap and Type K bead probe, though Extech rates that probe only to 250°C even though the meter reads to 760°C. If you want the 87V's filter and accuracy in a sealed body, Fluke's 87V MAX is IP67; we compare it in Fluke 87V vs 87V MAX.

CAT IV needs CAT IV leads and real PPE

Both meters are rated CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V, which covers a 480 V motor control center and the service entrance. The rating only holds with leads rated the same, and it doesn't replace arc-flash PPE, lockout/tagout or a qualified person. Before measuring capacitance on a drive, lock it out and confirm the DC bus has discharged per the drive maker's instructions.

Choose the Fluke 87V if…

  • ✓You measure variable-frequency drive outputs and need a low-pass filter
  • ✓You calibrate or verify 4–20 mA loops and 24 V DC supplies against tight tolerances
  • ✓You need peak capture for inrush and transients, or temperature to 1090 °C with a suitable probe

Choose the Extech EX505A if…

  • ✓The meter will get wet, dusty or dropped: it is IP67 with a 2 m drop rating
  • ✓Your work is 50/60 Hz mains, motors and controls rather than drive outputs
  • ✓You need capacitance beyond 10,000 µF, up to 99.99 mF

Neither if…

You need both drive-output accuracy and a waterproof meter. The Fluke 87V MAX keeps the 87V's filter and ±(0.05% + 1) DC accuracy in an IP67 body with a 4 m drop rating.

The bottom line

For industrial testing the Fluke 87V is the better meter: a low-pass filter for drives, 250 µs peak capture and DC accuracy ten times tighter make it the right tool for maintenance and instrumentation work. The Extech EX505A is the better buy where the meter takes abuse, with the same CAT IV 600V rating, IP67 sealing and a 2 m drop rating at roughly a quarter of the price, as long as you don't need to read drive outputs.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Extech EX505A have a low-pass filter?+

No. Extech's EX5xxA datasheet lists the low-pass filter and LoZ for the EX520A and EX530A and marks both as absent on the EX505A. The manual also specifies AC volts for non-sine waveforms at 50/60 Hz only, so drive outputs fall outside its accuracy specification. The Fluke 87V's filter is rated 3 dB at 1 kHz.

Is the Fluke 87V waterproof?+

Fluke's comparison table lists the standard 87V at IP30, which means protection against solid objects but no rated water protection. The Extech EX505A is IP67, dust-tight and protected against temporary immersion. If you want Fluke's measuring performance in a sealed meter, the Fluke 87V MAX is rated IP67 with a 4 m drop rating.

Is the EX505A accurate enough for 4–20 mA work?+

For checking that a loop is alive and roughly where it should be, yes. On a 12.00 mA signal, the manual's looser DC current figure allows about ±0.21 mA, or 1.3% of a 16 mA span. The Fluke 87V allows about ±0.064 mA on the same reading. For calibration against a tight tolerance, the Fluke is the better choice.

What is the difference between the Extech EX505 and EX505A?+

Extech lists the EX505A as the current model that replaces the discontinued EX505. The EX505A is a 6,000-count True RMS meter rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V with IP67 sealing and a 2 m drop rating. We compared only the EX505A here, using its 2024 datasheet and current manual; we didn't verify the older EX505's specifications.

Why does the Extech datasheet say 0.09% accuracy?+

The EX5xxA datasheet covers three models, and one feature bullet says 0.09% next to the EX505A heading. The same datasheet's specification table gives the EX505A ±0.5% basic accuracy and the EX520A ±0.09%, and the EX505A manual specifies DC volts at ±(0.5% + 5). We use the manual's figure and treat the bullet as an error.

Can the EX505A measure high temperatures?+

The meter reads Type K thermocouples from -20 to 760°C, but Extech rates the supplied bead probe only to 250°C and warns that exceeding it can damage the probe and the meter. For hotter surfaces you need a separately rated probe. The Fluke 87V reads -200 to 1090 °C, with the probe sold separately and its own rating applying.
