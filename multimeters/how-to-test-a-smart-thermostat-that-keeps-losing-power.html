How to Test a Smart Thermostat That Keeps Losing Power

A smart thermostat that keeps losing power, rebooting, dropping Wi-Fi or blanking its screen is nearly always short of the small 24 VAC supply it borrows from your heating and cooling system. The usual culprits are a missing or disconnected C (common) wire, a thermostat that relies on power stealing, a loose conductor at a terminal, a flat backup battery, or a blown low-voltage fuse at the furnace or air handler. Every check below is on the low-voltage side except the fuse, and that one is done with the equipment switched off.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 25, 2026

An AC volts reading between R and C is the single most useful number here. See our Fluke 117 review.

Stay on the low-voltage side

  • The wires at the thermostat carry about 24 VAC, which is low risk, but the furnace or air handler they come from also has 120 V or 240 V inside. Switch the equipment off at its service switch or breaker before opening any furnace or air-handler door.
  • Never defeat a door safety switch to watch the board run. Live testing inside the equipment is a job for an HVAC technician.
  • A burning smell, a melted connector or a scorched board means stop: keep the power off and call a professional.

Symptoms and likely causes

Note what the thermostat does and when. A screen that dies at the moment the furnace or compressor kicks on tells a different story from one that slowly fades over a week, and both differ from a thermostat that has been dead since the day it was mounted.

How a smart thermostat gets its power

A traditional mechanical thermostat needed no power at all; it was just a switch. A connected thermostat runs a screen, a processor and a radio around the clock, so it has to draw current continuously. In most forced-air systems that current comes from a step-down control transformer in the furnace or air handler. One side of the transformer secondary is the R terminal, the other is C. With a C wire in place, the thermostat simply connects across R and C and has a steady supply.

Older houses often have only two to four conductors in the thermostat cable, with no C. Many smart thermostats cope by power stealing: they trickle a small current through the heat or cool call wire while that circuit is idle, low enough that the relay or gas valve at the other end stays put. Some systems tolerate this well. Others react with a furnace that short cycles, a fan that hums, or a thermostat whose internal battery slowly drains because the trickle cannot keep up with the Wi-Fi radio.

Quick checks before the meter

  • Furnace switch and breaker: the light-switch style service switch near the furnace is easy to knock off. Confirm it is on and the furnace breaker has not tripped. Reset a tripped breaker once; if it trips again, leave it off and call for service.
  • Batteries: if your model takes replaceable cells, fit a fresh set of the type its manual specifies. If it has a built-in rechargeable cell, the settings menu usually shows battery level or charging state.
  • Condensate safety switch: many air handlers and high-efficiency furnaces have a float switch that interrupts the low-voltage circuit when the drain backs up. A full pan or a tripped float is a common reason for a dead thermostat in summer.
  • Wall plate: pull the thermostat off its base and look at each wire. Every conductor should be stripped clean, fully inserted and locked, with no copper strands touching a neighbor.

1Measure R to C at the wall plate

Leave the system powered, remove the thermostat from its base so the terminals are exposed, and set your meter to AC volts. Touch one probe to the R (or Rh/Rc) terminal and the other to C. A healthy residential control circuit is nominally 24 VAC, and readings in the mid-20s are typical; a transformer with little load on it often reads a little higher. Then take the same reading while someone raises the setpoint so the heat calls, or lowers it for cooling. A drop of a volt or so is normal as the gas valve or contactor pulls in. A collapse to single digits suggests a failing transformer, an overloaded secondary or a poor connection somewhere in the R or C path.

If there is no C terminal in use, measure from R to the W or Y terminal with the system idle. You should see roughly the full control voltage across the open call circuit, because the relay coil or gas valve at the far end completes the path. That confirms R is live, but it also confirms you have no dedicated return, which is the root problem for most smart thermostats. A meter with Min/Max recording, such as the HVAC meters in our roundup, helps catch a dip that only lasts a second or two while the blower ramps up. For the meter basics, see how to use a multimeter.

2Check the transformer and fuse at the furnace

If R to C reads zero, follow the cable back. Turn the furnace or air handler off at its service switch and at the breaker before removing the access door. Many control boards carry a small automotive-style blade fuse on the 24 V side, often rated around 3 A or 5 A; the exact value is printed on the board or in the wiring diagram inside the door. Pull it and check it with your meter on continuity or ohms. A good fuse reads close to zero ohms; an open one reads OL. Replace it only with the same rating.

A fuse that blows means something on the low-voltage circuit shorted: a staple through the thermostat cable, a nicked wire touching the metal cabinet at the outdoor unit, or R and C touching during the install. A new fuse that blows straight away is a technician job, because finding that short means tracing wiring through walls and the outdoor unit.

Checking the transformer itself, primary and secondary, is covered in how to check a 24V transformer with a multimeter. Measuring its live 120 V primary inside the cabinet is best left to a technician. A transformer that is undersized for the added draw of a smart thermostat, a zone panel or a humidifier will often read normal at idle and sag only under load, which is why the heat-call reading in Step 1 matters. A fuse you can check without pulling it is explained in how to test a fuse without removing it.

Common installation issues

  • C connected at the thermostat but not at the furnace: a spare conductor in the cable does nothing until it is landed on the board's C terminal too. Installers often find it folded back behind the cable jacket.
  • Rc and Rh jumper: systems with one transformer usually need Rc and Rh joined, either by a jumper or the thermostat's own setting. Separate heating and cooling transformers need them kept apart. Follow the thermostat maker's wiring guide.
  • Heat pump O/B and auxiliary wires: landing these on the wrong terminal makes the system behave oddly, and a wrongly configured thermostat may try to steal power through a circuit that won't tolerate it.
  • Short strip or overlong strip: too little bare copper gives a high-resistance contact that warms and loosens; too much risks touching an adjacent terminal and blowing the fuse.
  • Boilers, millivolt systems and line-voltage heat: many hot-water boilers, gas fireplaces and baseboard heaters don't use a standard 24 VAC thermostat circuit at all. Line-voltage baseboard thermostats switch 120 V or 240 V and need a thermostat made for them. Check compatibility before blaming the power supply.

When a C wire is missing, the fixes are a new multi-conductor cable, using an unused spare conductor, or an add-a-wire adapter kit approved by the thermostat maker. Each of those involves the furnace board and is a reasonable job to hand to an HVAC technician.

When to call an HVAC technician or electrician

Call an HVAC technician if the low-voltage fuse blows again, the transformer reads low under load, there is no C wire to use, or the system short cycles after the smart thermostat went in. Call an electrician if the furnace breaker keeps tripping or the furnace's 120 V supply itself is missing. Contact the thermostat maker if R to C reads a steady, normal voltage and the unit still reboots, since that points to the thermostat itself.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my smart thermostat go blank only when the heat or AC turns on?

That timing points to power stealing or a weak supply. Without a C wire the thermostat charges itself through a call wire while the system is idle, and when the call closes that trickle disappears. A sagging transformer or a poor connection on R or C can cause the same dip as the system load comes on.

Can I use the G (fan) wire as a C wire?

Some installers repurpose the G conductor as a common, but you lose independent fan control, and some heat pump or multi-stage setups need G. Check the thermostat maker's wiring guide for your system type before moving anything, or have a technician pull a new cable or fit an adapter kit the thermostat maker approves.

What voltage should I see between R and C?

Residential control transformers are nominally 24 VAC, and a reading somewhere in the mid-20s is common. A lightly loaded transformer often reads a few volts high. A reading far below 24, one that jumps around, or zero means the supply, wiring or fuse needs attention.

My thermostat uses AA batteries and still loses power. Why?

Battery-only models usually just need fresh alkaline cells, but some thermostats use the batteries only as backup for the clock and settings while the 24 VAC supply runs the display and radio. If fresh cells don't help, measure R to C to see whether the main supply is reaching the thermostat at all.

Why did my thermostat go blank after I changed the furnace filter?

Many furnaces have a door safety switch that cuts power to the control board, and with it the 24-volt supply to the thermostat, whenever the blower door is off. If the door is not fully seated after a filter change, that switch stays open and the thermostat goes dark. Push the door firmly into place so it presses the switch, then give the system a minute or two to restart.
