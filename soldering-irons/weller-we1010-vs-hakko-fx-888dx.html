Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX-888DX

Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX-888DX is the current-generation bench station match-up. Both are ESD safe digital stations with a 65–70W iron. The Hakko adds a 100W station, a wider 50–480°C range and five presets; the Weller (sold in the US as the WE1010NA kit) answers with standby and auto setback, password-protected settings and a published 28-second heat-up. Pick the Hakko for range, presets and T18 tips; pick the Weller for a shared bench where idle tips and fiddled settings cost money.

Weller WE1010 vs Hakko FX-888DX spec by spec

On raw numbers the Hakko leads on station power and on range at both ends, while the Weller is the only one of the pair with a published warm-up time. The two figures below show the gaps that actually change how the stations behave on a bench.

Reading the tolerance numbers fairly

Hakko's ±1°C describes how steady the tip holds when nothing is touching it. Weller's ±5°C says how close the displayed set point is to the real tip temperature, and its ±6°C covers drift. A smaller figure is better, but the makers aren't measuring the same thing, so treat the chart as a hint, not a verdict. A tip thermometer settles the question on your own bench.

Where the temperature ranges differ

Most electronics work happens well inside both ranges. Lead-free solder is commonly run somewhere around 320 to 380°C and tin-lead lower, so either station covers everyday joints. The extra Hakko range lives at the edges: settings under 100°C suit gentle jobs such as working with low-melt alloys near heat-sensitive plastic, and the top 30°C helps when a large tip has to push heat into a big ground pour.

Turning the iron up is rarely the best fix for a stubborn joint, though. A wider tip carries more heat at a lower setting and is kinder to pads. Our chart of soldering iron temperature by metal gives starting points by alloy.

What standby and setback actually save

A tip sitting at full heat in its stand oxidizes faster, and flux burns off between joints. Weller's standby mode with auto setback drops the tip to a lower temperature when the iron has been idle, then brings it back up when you pick it up again. Weller says this conserves energy and protects the equipment. Hakko doesn't state an equivalent feature for the FX-888DX, so on that station the habit falls to you: tin the tip and turn the dial down when you step away.

On a bench used all day by several people, those idle stretches add up to hours, which is where setback earns its keep. If your own iron seems to switch itself off or cool unexpectedly, read why a soldering iron keeps switching off; standby features are a common cause. Tip life tips are in our soldering iron tip care guide.

Use cases in practice

A home repair bench that moves from fine SMD rework to a chunky XT60 connector in one evening benefits most from the Hakko's presets: store a low, a middle and a high setting and switch with a press instead of spinning through 100 degrees. The FX-888DX's ability to drive other Hakko handpieces also gives a growth path without a second station.

A school lab, maker space or production line has different problems: settings that wander because someone turned them up, and tips left baking at full heat. The WE1010's password lock and auto setback answer both, and its published accuracy and stability figures make it easier to document a process.

Tip ecosystems matter more than the station

Over a station's life you'll buy many tips, so stay with the system you already own. Our guide to soldering iron tip types explains which shapes to start with, and soldering iron vs soldering station covers whether you need a station at all.

Safety: both tips reach 450°C or more

Either iron can burn skin instantly and ignite paper or flux-soaked rags, so keep the iron in its stand whenever it isn't in your hand and switch the station off at the end of a session. Plug both into a grounded three-prong outlet; without the earth connection the ESD path doesn't work. Ventilate or extract flux fumes, and wash your hands after handling leaded solder.

The bottom line

Choose the Hakko FX-888DX for more power, a wider range, presets and compatibility with FX-888D tips. Choose the Weller WE1010 for a shared or professional bench where standby, auto setback and password-protected settings matter more than raw range.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Weller WE1010 the same as the WE1010NA?+

Yes, in the US. WE1010NA is the North American 120V kit of the WE1010 station, supplied with the 70W WEP 70 iron and PH 70 safety rest. The specs on this page come from Weller's WE1010NA 120V product page, so check the voltage on any listing that isn't marked NA before you buy.

Which station heats up faster, the WE1010 or FX-888DX?+

Weller states about 28 seconds to go from 50 to 350°C. Hakko doesn't publish a heat-up time for the FX-888DX, so there's no fair way to compare them on paper. The Hakko's 100W station gives it more power in reserve, but warm-up also depends on the heater, tip size and control loop.

Are both stations ESD safe?+

Yes. Weller lists the WE1010 as an ESD-safe station with a heat-resistant silicone cable, and Hakko lists the FX-888DX as ESD safe with tip-to-ground resistance under 2 Ω. That protection only works when the station is plugged into a properly grounded three-prong outlet, not through a two-prong adapter.

Can I use FX-888D tips on the FX-888DX?+

Yes. The FX-888DX keeps the FX-8801 handpiece and T18 tips used by the FX-888D, so a drawer of existing tips carries straight over. That compatibility is the strongest reason for an FX-888D owner to stay with Hakko rather than switch to the Weller, whose WEP 70 iron takes ET-series tips instead.

Does the Hakko FX-888DX have a sleep mode?+

Hakko doesn't state a sleep, standby or auto-off mode for the FX-888DX on its product page, so assume it holds the set temperature until you turn it down. Weller lists standby with automatic setback for the WE1010, which lowers the tip temperature during idle periods to save energy and tip life.
