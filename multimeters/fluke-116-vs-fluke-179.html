Fluke 116 vs Fluke 179: Which Is Better for HVAC Troubleshooting?

For furnace and boiler service the Fluke 116 is the better HVAC meter, because it is the only one of the two that can read a flame-rod signal: its 600.0 µA range steps in 0.1 µA, while the 179's smallest current step is 0.01 mA, a hundred times coarser. The Fluke 179 wins everywhere else a tech pushes current through the leads, up to 10 A, and it adds CAT IV 600V, tighter DC accuracy and a lifetime warranty. Both read Type K temperature with the same published accuracy. This Fluke 116 vs 179 comparison maps each spec to an HVAC task so you can see which gap matters for your calls.

Six HVAC checks, two meters

A no-heat or no-cool call tends to run through the same handful of measurements. Here is how each meter handles them, using only Fluke's published figures. This is desk research against maker specs; we have not run either meter on a furnace ourselves.

Flame signal

116 reads it in 0.1 µA steps. 179 shows 0.00 mA on a typical signal.

Run capacitor

Both read it. 179's tolerance is a little tighter.

Supply / return air

Identical spec on both, 80BK probe included with each.

24 V control voltage

Same AC accuracy. 116 adds LoZ to sort ghost readings.

Inducer or blower amps

179 in series to 10 A. 116 can't; use a clamp.

Disconnect and panel

Both fine at CAT III. 179 also CAT IV 600V.

The current gap, drawn to scale

The single biggest difference in this Fluke 116 vs 179 comparison is where each current input lives on the scale. Plot both on a logarithmic axis and they barely overlap: the 116 covers roughly four decades at the very bottom, and the 179 covers six decades starting just above it.

Flame-rectification signals are small. Furnace makers generally specify them in single-digit microamps, and the exact minimum is printed in the equipment manual. Take an illustrative 2.5 µA reading. On the 116's DC µA line, the tolerance is 1.0% of 2.5 µA (0.025 µA) plus 2 counts of 0.1 µA, about ±0.23 µA, so the display shows something close to 2.5 and you can compare it with the manual. On the 179's 60.00 mA range one count is 10 µA, so the same 2.5 µA is a quarter of a single digit and the display reads 0.00. That is not a matter of precision: the 179 simply cannot see the signal.

Turn the problem around and the 116 fails just as completely. A thermostat heat anticipator, a damper actuator or a gas valve coil draws current measured in tenths of an amp, and small inducer and blower motors draw amps. The 116's input ends at 600 µA, well under one milliamp, so none of that can go through its leads. The 179 handles it on its 400 mA, 6 A and 10 A ranges. Our guide to measuring microamps covers the series hookup for flame rods.

Capacitors and temperature: closer than you'd think

On run and start capacitors both meters do the job. Work it through on an illustrative 45 µF label value, which falls on each meter's 100.0 µF range with 0.1 µF steps. The 116 allows 1.9% of 45 (0.855 µF) plus 2 counts (0.2 µF), so about ±1.06 µF. The 179 allows 1.2% of 45 (0.54 µF) plus 0.2 µF, about ±0.74 µF. A typical ±6% capacitor tolerance on 45 µF is ±2.7 µF, so either meter can tell a good part from one that has drifted out of spec. The 179's edge only matters when a reading lands right at the tolerance line.

Temperature is a genuine draw. Fluke publishes the same range, resolution and ±(1% + 10) tolerance for both, excluding probe error, and puts an 80BK bead probe in each box. Ten counts at 0.1 °C is 1.0 °C, so a 13 °C supply-air reading carries roughly ±1.1 °C on either meter. If you calculate temperature split, the probe and where you place it will move the result more than the choice between these two. See how to check a thermocouple with a multimeter for the pilot-generator side of temperature work.

Ghost voltage on control wiring

Thermostat cable often runs alongside line-voltage conductors, and an unused or open conductor can pick up a few volts by capacitive coupling. A standard high-impedance input reports that voltage faithfully, which can send you chasing a fault that isn't there. The 116's AutoVolt position drops the input to about 3 kΩ, enough load to collapse a coupled reading while a real 24 V source holds steady. Fluke lists no LoZ mode for the 179, so on that meter you have to judge suspicious readings by context, for example by checking whether the voltage stays put when the thermostat calls. Our page on voltage on a disconnected circuit explains the mechanism.

Same AC accuracy at 24 V

Both meters list True RMS AC volts at ±(1.0% + 3). On a 24.00 V control transformer reading that is 0.24 V plus 3 counts of 0.01 V, about ±0.27 V on either one. The LoZ input, not accuracy, is what separates them on control circuits.

Who should skip each one

Skip the 116 if you rarely see gas ignition problems, or if you want one meter that also handles general electrical work: with no milliamp or amp ranges it can't check a fuse-protected control circuit's draw, a doorbell transformer load or a small motor. Its CAT III 600V rating also rules it out for work at the service entrance. Skip the 179 if flame-sensor checks are a weekly job and you don't want a second tool, or if battery life matters: Fluke quotes about 200 hours against the 116's 400.

Already narrowing it down within one family? Compare the 116 with its siblings in Fluke 116 vs 115 for HVAC and Fluke 116 vs 117, or see whether you need the 179 at all in Fluke 177 vs 179, since the 177 drops only the thermometer.

A conflict on Fluke's 179 page

Fluke's own 179 product page disagrees with itself. Its model comparison table names microamps as an extra function and gives 9,999 µF and "400 °F", while the detailed spec table on the same page lists 0.01 mA as the best current resolution, 10,000 µF and an upper temperature of 400 °C. We follow the spec table, which matches the Fluke 170 Series datasheet. If microamps are why you are looking at the 179, treat that as unconfirmed and plan on the 116 or a µA clamp for flame work.

Live-circuit safety on HVAC equipment

Flame-rod checks are made with the appliance running, and the burner compartment has 120 V present even though the signal you read is tiny. Condensers and rooftop units may be fed at 208 to 480 V. Keep within each meter's rating (CAT III 600V for the 116), prove the meter on a known live source before and after a dead test, and never put test leads in the current jacks to read voltage. If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

The bottom line

Choose the Fluke 116 if gas heating calls are your bread and butter: it is the only one of the two that reads flame-sensor microamps, and its LoZ input helps on thermostat wiring, but plan on a clamp for motor amps. Choose the Fluke 179 if HVAC is part of a broader maintenance job: it measures current to 10 A, carries CAT IV 600V and a lifetime warranty, and reads temperature to the same spec, but it needs a µA clamp for flame rods.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 179 test a flame sensor?+

Not usefully. Its finest current step is 0.01 mA on the 60.00 mA range, which equals 10 µA, while flame-rectification signals are typically a few microamps. A healthy signal would display as 0.00 mA. Fluke's 179 comparison table does mention µA, but the spec table on the same page does not, so we don't rely on it.

Can the Fluke 116 measure blower motor amps?+

No. The 116's only current input is the 600.0 µA range, and Fluke lists no milliamp or amp ranges for it. Putting a motor in series with that input is outside its rating. Measure blower, inducer and compressor current with a clamp meter instead, which also avoids breaking into the circuit.

Do the Fluke 116 and 179 read temperature the same way?+

On paper, yes. Fluke lists -40 to 400 °C at 0.1 °C resolution with a ±(1% + 10) tolerance for both, excluding probe error, and each ships with an 80BK Type K bead probe. Fluke's 179 page has one table that says 400 °F, which conflicts with its own spec table; we follow the spec table.

Is CAT IV 600V on the Fluke 179 needed for HVAC work?+

Not for most of it. Furnaces, air handlers and condenser disconnects are downstream of the panel, where CAT III applies, and the 116's CAT III 600V rating covers that. CAT IV matters at the service drop, meter base and outdoor service conductors, so it helps techs who also do service-entrance electrical work.

Which has better battery life, the Fluke 116 or 179?+

The 116. Fluke quotes about 400 hours on its 9 V battery, against about 200 hours for the 179. Both use a single 9 V cell. If you keep the 179's backlight on in attics and crawl spaces, expect to change batteries more often than on a 116 used in the same conditions.

Is the Fluke 116 auto-ranging?+

Fluke's spec table for the 116 doesn't state it, and the meter has a manual RANGE button, so we list auto-ranging as not stated rather than guess. The 179 is listed as auto-ranging with manual override. In daily HVAC use most functions on both meters pick a sensible range without intervention.
