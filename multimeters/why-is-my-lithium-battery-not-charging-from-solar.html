Why Is My Lithium Battery Not Charging From Solar?

A lithium battery that won't charge from solar is usually being blocked on purpose: by its own BMS (because the cells are below freezing or it tripped on low voltage), by a charge controller set to the wrong battery type, or by a panel voltage too low for the controller to start. Less often the cause is a blown PV fuse, a loose connector or a failed controller. With a multimeter on DC volts you can find which one it is in about 15 minutes, by checking the voltage on each side of every link between the panel and the cells. This page covers small 12, 24 and 48 V off-grid systems with a separate charge controller and a LiFePO4 or other lithium battery.

Lithium batteries and solar wiring

  • Never try to force charge into a battery whose BMS has blocked it, for example by bypassing the BMS or charging cells directly. The BMS is protecting cells from fire and permanent damage.
  • Don't charge a swollen, leaking, hot or damaged lithium battery. Isolate it and contact the maker or a battery recycler.
  • Panels make voltage in daylight and can't be switched off. Open the PV breaker before touching PV conductors; strings over about 50 V DC are a shock hazard.
  • A short across battery terminals can release hundreds of amps. Remove jewelry, use insulated tools, and call a solar installer for roof or grid-tied systems.

Why a lithium battery refuses solar charge

A lead-acid battery has no electronics: whatever voltage the controller applies, it takes some current. A lithium battery has a battery management system (BMS) between its terminals and its cells, and the BMS has a switch that can open the charge path while leaving the discharge path working. It opens that switch for several reasons: cells too cold, a cell too high, a cell too low, too much current, or an internal fault. So "not charging" in a lithium system is often a decision, not a failure.

The charge controller adds its own decisions. It must recognize the battery voltage, it must have a working voltage from the panel, and it must be told what voltages the battery wants. If any link in that chain says no, the battery sits still in full sun. The fix is to find which link, which is a job of measuring volts on both sides of each one. If your battery charges but stops short of full, see why a lithium battery won't charge to 100%; this page is about batteries that take no solar charge at all.

1Check the battery temperature

Start here in cold weather because it's the most common cause and needs no tools beyond a thermometer. LiFePO4 makers commonly specify charging between about 0 °C and 45–55 °C (32 °F to 113–131 °F) while allowing discharge well below freezing. Below the lower limit the BMS blocks charge current entirely. Above the upper limit it may do the same.

The cell temperature is what counts, not the air. A battery in an uninsulated compartment can stay below 0 °C for hours after a cold night, even once the air warms up. If your battery has a Bluetooth app, it usually shows cell temperature and a charge-protection flag. Otherwise use a meter with a temperature probe taped to the case. If cold is the cause, charging resumes on its own once the cells warm up; nothing is broken.

2Read the battery terminals

Set the meter to DC volts and read directly on the battery posts with the PV disconnected. A 12 V LiFePO4 battery at a normal state of charge rests around 13.0–13.4 V. Three readings point to the BMS. Close to 0 V, or a few volts, usually means the BMS has disconnected the output after a deep discharge. A normal voltage that never rises when the sun is up suggests the charge switch is open. A reading that jumps straight up to the controller's output voltage the moment PV is connected, with no current flowing, suggests the same.

A battery that has tripped on low voltage needs to be woken. Many lithium batteries reactivate when a charger applies voltage to the terminals, but many solar controllers can't start without seeing battery voltage first. A lithium-capable AC charger with a wake or activation mode is usually the answer. Our guide on testing a battery protection circuit explains how to tell a tripped BMS from a dead one.

3Check the controller's battery profile

Open the controller's menu or app and confirm the battery type. Lead-acid profiles cause three kinds of trouble on lithium. Temperature compensation raises the charge voltage when it's cold, which can push a lithium battery into high-voltage cutoff. Equalization can run a 12 V system to about 15 V or beyond. And a lead-acid float stage holds the battery at a voltage that may not suit it. Any of these can make the BMS disconnect the charge path, after which the battery appears to refuse charge.

The opposite mistake causes trouble too. An absorption setpoint of 13.8 V, entered by mistake as the "charge" voltage, barely charges a LiFePO4 battery whose resting voltage is already 13.2–13.3 V. The difference of about half a volt drives very little current. Enter the battery maker's figures, not a generic chart's, and turn off temperature compensation unless the battery maker asks for it.

4Check the PV voltage at the controller

Read DC volts across the controller's PV terminals. For charging to start, the panel voltage must be comfortably above the battery voltage. A PWM controller can charge once PV exceeds the battery, but an MPPT controller often waits until PV is several volts higher, and then needs it to stay above. On a 24 V lithium bank (about 26.4–28.8 V while charging), a single 12 V-class panel with a Vmp around 18 V can never charge, however sunny it is.

Heat lowers panel voltage. Crystalline silicon panels typically lose roughly 0.3–0.4% of their voltage for every degree Celsius above 25 °C cell temperature, and cells on a summer roof run far above 25 °C. A string with little headroom at 25 °C can drop below the controller's start threshold on a hot afternoon. Partial shade on one panel of a series string can drag the whole string down too. If the PV reading is low even in clear sun, test each panel separately with the solar panel multimeter test.

5Check fuses, breakers and connectors

With everything connected and the sun up, read across each fuse or breaker in the PV line and in the battery line. A closed, healthy fuse shows nearly 0 V across it. An open one shows almost the whole source voltage. Do the same across each MC4 joint and each battery lug while the system is charging. A reading of more than a few tenths of a volt across a single joint means a bad connection, which also gets hot.

Replace a blown fuse only with the same type and rating, and find out why it blew: a reversed battery connection, a short, or an inrush current from a large inverter can all do it. If the controller's battery fuse keeps blowing, have the wiring checked before you try again.

6Confirm the charge reaches the cells

Once the earlier checks pass, turn off every DC load and the inverter, then watch the battery post voltage for 15 to 30 minutes in steady sun. A LiFePO4 battery in the middle of its range rises slowly, perhaps a few hundredths of a volt in that time, because its voltage curve is so flat. Near the top it rises faster. A battery app or shunt monitor that shows positive current is the clearest proof.

If the voltage doesn't move and no current is shown, but the controller reports output, the energy is going somewhere else, or the controller's reading is wrong. Measure the controller's output and the battery posts at the same time. If they match and the battery still won't rise, contact the battery maker: a BMS that won't close its charge switch under normal conditions needs their diagnosis.

A lithium battery can say no

Unlike lead-acid, a lithium battery has its own switch. Before blaming the panel or controller, ask whether the BMS has a reason to refuse: cold, a tripped low-voltage cutoff or a high cell.

Which multimeter to use for lithium solar faults

Lithium charging problems hide in small voltage differences, so pick a meter that resolves 0.01 V on the 20–60 V range. A temperature function saves a separate thermometer on cold mornings. A CAT rating matters if the same meter also goes near mains or high-voltage PV strings.

Frequently asked questions

Why won't my LiFePO4 battery charge in cold weather?

Charging lithium cells below freezing can plate lithium metal on the anode, which permanently damages them. Most LiFePO4 makers specify a charging range starting at about 0 °C (32 °F), and their BMS blocks charge current below that. The battery still discharges normally, so it seems fine but won't take solar. Self-heating batteries warm the cells first using charge power.

Can I use a lead-acid setting on a solar controller for lithium?

It's not a good idea. Lead-acid profiles often include temperature compensation, a high equalization voltage and a float stage that lithium batteries don't need. Equalization can push a 12 V battery toward 15 V or more, enough to trip the BMS. Use the controller's lithium or user profile and enter the battery maker's charge voltages.

Why does my solar controller say charging but the battery doesn't go up?

The controller may be charging into its own terminals while the BMS has disconnected the cells, or the current may be going straight to a running load. Measure the voltage at the battery posts while charging and compare it with the controller's battery screws. Then turn off loads and see whether the battery voltage rises over 15 to 30 minutes.

Will a solar controller charge a lithium battery from 0 volts?

Often not. Many controllers need to see battery voltage to power up and detect the system voltage, and a lithium battery whose BMS has cut off may show 0 V at its terminals. Some controllers and many lithium batteries have a wake-up or reactivation feature. Check both manuals; otherwise use a lithium-capable AC charger with a wake function to bring it back.

How many volts does a solar panel need to charge a 12V lithium battery?

Enough to exceed the battery's charge voltage with margin. A 12 V LiFePO4 battery charges to about 14.2–14.6 V, so the panel's working voltage must be higher. A 12 V-class panel with a Vmp around 17–19 V works with a PWM or MPPT controller. Many MPPT controllers also need the panel several volts above the battery before they start.
