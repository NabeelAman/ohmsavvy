Fluke 117 vs AstroAI DM6000AR: Professional Meter or Budget Alternative?

Buy the Fluke 117 if you test building wiring: both meters list CAT III 600V, but only the 117 adds LoZ ghost-voltage rejection, VoltAlert, tighter published accuracy and a 3-year warranty, and we bought and tested one ourselves. Buy the AstroAI DM6000AR if your work is electronics, cars and batteries: for a fraction of the price it adds microamp and milliamp ranges, temperature and duty cycle, none of which the 117 has. The rest of this page shows where each meter's published specs actually change the reading you get.

Key differences

  • →Safety rating: both list CAT III 600V. Fluke states compliance with IEC 61010-2-033, the part written for hand-held meters; AstroAI's Amazon spec table cites EN 61010-1 and adds CAT II 1000V, while its own product page states no category.
  • →Electrician features: only the 117 has LoZ, AutoVolt and VoltAlert; AstroAI's table marks NCV and LoZ absent.
  • →Small currents: the DM6000AR has 600 µA, 6,000 µA, 60 mA and 600 mA ranges; the 117's current starts at its 6 A range.
  • →Accuracy: Fluke's published DC volts spec is ±(0.5% + 2) against ±(0.8% + 3), and AC volts ±(1.0% + 3) against ±(1.2% + 6).
  • →Bench extras: the DM6000AR adds temperature to 1,370 °C, duty cycle and manual ranging; the 117 has none of the three.
  • →Working conditions: the 117 is specified from −10 °C, updates 4 times a second and has a fast bar graph; the DM6000AR is specified from 0 °C and samples twice a second.

CAT III 600V on both spec sheets: what still separates them

On paper, the safety gap is smaller than the price gap. The specification table in AstroAI's Amazon listing gives the DM6000AR EN 61010-1 CAT III 600V and CAT II 1000V, though AstroAI's own product page doesn't repeat it. Fluke states CAT III 600V for the 117 under IEC 61010-1 and IEC 61010-2-033, the part of the standard with particular requirements for hand-held meters. Both ratings cover receptacles, branch circuits and the panel. The DM6000AR's 1000 V DC range is covered only at CAT II, which is plug-in equipment, not distribution wiring.

What the rating doesn't tell you is everything else an electrician depends on. The 117 adds LoZ and VoltAlert, its published accuracy is tighter on every shared function, and it's specified to work from −10 °C. AstroAI's operating range starts at 0 °C, which rules out a winter attic or an unheated garage on paper. Fluke also backs it for three years, while AstroAI gives no warranty term.

Worked scenario: checking a 240 V water-heater circuit

At the disconnect, a 240.0 V reading on the 117's 600.0 V range carries ±(1.0% × 240.0 + 3 × 0.1) = ±2.7 V. On the DM6000AR's 600 V range the listed ±(1.2% + 6) gives ±(2.88 + 0.6) ≈ ±3.5 V. Both are fine for deciding whether a circuit is live. The difference starts to matter when you're judging voltage drop under load, where a volt either way changes the answer.

Accuracy side by side: is the AstroAI as accurate as a Fluke?

Close, but not equal. Every line AstroAI publishes for the DM6000AR is looser than the 117's matching line, typically by 0.3 to 0.6 percentage points plus a few more counts. That is a spec-sheet comparison, not a bench test. We haven't measured a DM6000AR, and a spec is the worst case the maker promises, not the error you should expect on every reading.

Ghost voltage: what the 117's LoZ mode solves

Electricians pay for the 117 as much for LoZ as for the CAT rating. When a dead conductor runs beside a live one in the same conduit or cable, stray capacitance couples a weak voltage onto it. A meter with a normal high-impedance input, more than 10 MΩ on the 117's regular volts setting, barely loads that source, so it can show a convincing phantom reading on a wire that can't power anything. The 117's AutoVolt LoZ input, about 3 kΩ per Fluke's datasheet, draws enough current to collapse the phantom toward zero while a real feed holds up.

A CAT III rating doesn't change this: AstroAI's comparison table marks VFD/LoZ and NCV detection as absent on the DM6000AR, so a DM6000AR owner has no quick way to sort a coupled reading from a live one. That doesn't matter on a breadboard, where there's no long parallel run to couple anything. It matters a great deal when you're deciding whether a switch leg in an old house is safe to cut. Our explainer on why a multimeter shows voltage on a disconnected circuit covers the effect in depth.

Microamps and milliamps: the DM6000AR's biggest advantage

The 117 was designed around 6 A and 10 A loads, so its smallest current step is 1 mA. The DM6000AR adds four low ranges, 600 µA, 6,000 µA, 60 mA and 600 mA, behind a 400 mA ceramic fuse, each listed at ±(0.8% + 5). For anything powered by a USB port or a coin cell, that turns a rough estimate into a usable number.

The arithmetic: 0.045 A on the 117 carries ±(1.0% × 0.045 + 3 × 0.001) ≈ ±3.5 mA, close to 8% of the reading. On the DM6000AR's 60 mA range it is ±(0.36 + 0.05) = ±0.41 mA, under 1%. Go lower and the 117 runs out entirely: a 150 µA sleep current shows as 0.000 A, while the DM6000AR's 600 µA range reads it to ±(1.2 + 0.5) = ±1.7 µA. The flip side is the 10 A jack. Fluke allows 20 A for 30 seconds; AstroAI doesn't state an overload limit or any AC current accuracy. Our guides to measuring standby current and parasitic battery drain show where those low ranges earn their place.

Where the DM6000AR is the better tool: the low-voltage bench

Turn to a microcontroller project and the comparison flips. The 117's current function begins at its 6 A range, so an Arduino drawing 45 mA shows as 0.045 A: one count is 1 mA, more than 2% of the reading before accuracy is even counted. The DM6000AR's fused 400 mA input carries its microamp and milliamp ranges, covered above. It also reads temperature and duty cycle, useful for checking a regulator heatsink or a PWM fan signal, and it lets you lock a range manually when auto-ranging hunts on a slowly changing voltage.

When the budget meter is the right call

If you never measure building wiring, much of what the 117 adds goes unused: LoZ, AutoVolt, VoltAlert and the cold-weather operating range are all about field work on mains. For a hobby bench, the DM6000AR's extra functions are the more useful set, and a meter that's cheap to replace is one you won't mind clipping into a circuit you might short. Our AstroAI DM6000AR review and the guide to the best multimeters for Arduino and circuit board testing go further.

A CAT III label isn't the whole safety story

Both meters list CAT III 600V, so neither belongs at the service entrance or meter base: those are CAT IV locations and a job for a licensed electrician. Whichever meter you use on mains, check it on a known live source before and after confirming a circuit is dead, keep the red lead out of the current jacks when measuring volts, and replace the leads if the insulation is nicked.

The refresh rate is the detail buyers overlook. Twice a second is fine for a steady rail, but when you're watching a voltage sag as a compressor starts, the 117's 32-per-second bar graph shows the movement the digits miss. On the other side, the DM6000AR's magnet comes in the box, and its battery and both fuses sit behind two screws under the kickstand, per AstroAI's own FAQ.

Temperature needs a caution. AstroAI lists −40 to 1,370 °C at ±(1–5% + 4), and astroai.com includes a K-type thermocouple in the box, but the Amazon listing's box contents name only the meter, a 9V battery, test leads, a multi-function socket and the manual. If you're buying for HVAC or heatsink checks, confirm the probe with the seller, or budget for a K-type bead probe. The 117 has no temperature function at all; see our HVAC multimeter picks if that matters.

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 117 if you test building wiring: the CAT III 600V rating is matched on paper, but LoZ, VoltAlert, tighter accuracy, a −10 °C operating floor and a 3-year warranty are not, and it's a meter we've tested ourselves. Buy the AstroAI DM6000AR for electronics, cars and batteries, where its microamp and milliamp ranges, temperature and duty cycle make it the more capable meter for a fraction of the price.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 117 worth it over the AstroAI DM6000AR?+

Yes, if you work on household or commercial wiring. The 117's LoZ ghost-voltage mode and VoltAlert detector are features the DM6000AR doesn't have, and its published accuracy is tighter. If you only work on hobby electronics and 12 V systems, the DM6000AR covers more of what you'll use for much less.

What CAT rating is the AstroAI DM6000AR?+

AstroAI's Amazon listing gives it EN 61010-1 CAT III 600V and CAT II 1000V. The astroai.com product page doesn't list a category. CAT III 600V matches the Fluke 117's rating, which Fluke states under IEC 61010-2-033 as well. Neither meter is rated CAT IV, so neither should be used at a meter base or service entrance.

Can I use the AstroAI DM6000AR on household outlets?+

Its Amazon listing states CAT III 600V under EN 61010-1, which covers receptacles and branch circuits, though AstroAI's own product page doesn't repeat it. It has no LoZ or non-contact detector, so it can't flag ghost voltage. For occasional outlet checks it's adequate; for regular work in boxes and panels, the Fluke 117's LoZ and VoltAlert are worth having.

Is the AstroAI DM6000AR as accurate as a Fluke 117?+

Not on paper. AstroAI lists DC volts at ±(0.8% + 3) and AC volts at ±(1.2% + 6); Fluke specifies ±(0.5% + 2) and ±(1.0% + 3). On a 120 V outlet that's about ±2.0 V against ±1.5 V. Both are close enough for live-or-dead checks. These are maker specs; we haven't bench-tested a DM6000AR.

Can the AstroAI DM6000AR measure microamps?+

Yes. AstroAI's listing gives DC current ranges of 600 µA, 6,000 µA, 60 mA and 600 mA at ±(0.8% + 5), plus 6 A and 10 A at ±(1.5% + 3). That's a real advantage over the Fluke 117, whose current ranges start at 6 A with 1 mA as the smallest step, so it can't read sleep currents.

Which meter is better for Arduino projects?+

The AstroAI DM6000AR. Its 60 mA range reads a 45 mA board to about ±0.4 mA, and its 600 µA range catches sleep currents the Fluke 117 can't see, since the 117's smallest step is 1 mA. It also adds temperature and duty cycle. On 3.3 V and 5 V rails both meters show three decimals.

Are both the Fluke 117 and AstroAI DM6000AR True RMS?+

Yes. Fluke specifies True RMS AC volts at ±(1.0% + 3) from 45 to 500 Hz. AstroAI's listing says the DM6000AR is True RMS on AC voltage and current and gives ±(1.2% + 6) on its 6 to 600 V AC ranges, but it doesn't state the frequency band or any AC current accuracy.

Is a thermocouple included with the AstroAI DM6000AR?+

AstroAI's sources disagree. The astroai.com product page lists a K-type thermocouple in the box, but the Amazon listing's box contents name only the meter, a 9V 6F22 battery, test leads, a multi-function socket and a manual. The temperature range is −40 to 1,370 °C. Check with the seller before relying on it for HVAC work.

Does the AstroAI DM6000AR have a low-impedance mode like the Fluke 117?+

No. AstroAI's own comparison table marks VFD/LoZ as absent on the DM6000AR, and it has no non-contact voltage detector either. The 117's AutoVolt LoZ input, about 3 kΩ per Fluke, loads a de-energized wire enough to collapse ghost voltage, which helps electricians decide whether a conductor is really live.

What is a cheaper alternative to the Fluke 117?+

The Fluke 115 keeps the same body, CAT III 600V rating and 3-year warranty but drops LoZ, AutoVolt and VoltAlert. Further down, the UNI-T UT139C pairs CAT III 600V with non-contact detection and temperature. The AstroAI DM6000AR is cheapest of all and suits benches and cars better than electrical work, mainly because it lacks LoZ.

Has OhmSavvy tested the Fluke 117 and the DM6000AR?+

We bought and tested the Fluke 117 on our own bench, which is why it carries our Hands-on Tested badge. The AstroAI DM6000AR is desk research from AstroAI's product page and its official Amazon listing.
