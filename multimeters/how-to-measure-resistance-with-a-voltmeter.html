HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Measure Resistance With a Voltmeter

To measure resistance with a voltmeter, put the unknown resistor in series with a resistor you already know, apply a steady DC voltage, and read the voltage across each one: because the same current flows through both, the unknown equals the known resistance multiplied by the ratio of the two voltages. If you can also read the current, Ohm's law does it directly: resistance is volts divided by amps. These methods matter when your meter has no ohms range, when the ohms range is broken, or when you need a part's resistance while it is actually carrying current. This guide works through three voltmeter methods with real arithmetic and shows where each one goes wrong.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 24, 2026

Any decent DC voltmeter works for these methods. A meter with a stable, high-resolution DC range, like the Fluke 117, keeps the ratio math accurate. For the dedicated ohms function, see how to measure resistance with a multimeter.

Low voltage only for the divider method

  • Build the reference circuit from a 9 V battery, AA pack or current-limited bench supply. Never wire a reference resistor across mains voltage.
  • Check the power each resistor will dissipate: P = V² ÷ R. At 9 V, a 100 Ω resistor would burn 0.81 W, more than a typical 1/4 W part can handle. Keep total resistance high enough that each part stays cool.
  • For live appliances, measure volts and amps only with a CAT-rated meter and insulated probes, and call an electrician if you would have to work inside a powered panel or appliance.

Why measure resistance with a voltmeter at all?

Most people searching for this have one of four problems. Their meter is a plain voltmeter, often an old analog panel meter or a cheap DC voltage tester, with no ohms range. Their multimeter's ohms range reads nonsense or has a blown protection part. They need the resistance of something that changes when it heats up, like a heater, a motor winding or a lamp, and want the hot value. Or they need to measure a very low resistance, such as a shunt, a cable run or a crimp, where a normal ohms range just shows test-lead resistance.

Every one of these is solved by the same idea. Resistance is voltage divided by current. If you can push a known current through a part, or compare it against a part whose value you know, a voltmeter is all you need to turn that into ohms. That is also exactly how your multimeter's ohms range works inside: a small current source and a voltmeter, with the division done for you.

1Pick a reference resistor and a DC source

You need a steady DC source and one resistor whose value you trust. A fresh 9 V battery is fine for quick checks; a bench supply is better because it doesn't sag. For the reference, a 1% metal-film resistor is ideal, and if any working meter is available, measure it first and use that real value instead of the color code.

Choose a reference close to the value you expect. The method is most accurate when the two voltages are similar, so each resistor drops roughly half the supply. If you have no idea, start with 10 kΩ, look at the split, and swap to a closer resistor for the final reading. Then check dissipation: with 9 V across 10 kΩ plus a similar unknown, current is about 0.45 mA and each resistor dissipates about 2 mW, which is nothing.

2Wire the series circuit

Connect the source positive to one end of the reference resistor, the other end of the reference to the unknown, and the far end of the unknown back to the source negative. A breadboard or a pair of alligator leads keeps the joints firm; a loose joint adds resistance that ends up in your answer. The unknown must be out of any other circuit, because a parallel path inside a board would carry part of the current and make the part look smaller than it is.

3Read the supply and the unknown's voltage

Set the meter to DC volts. First read Vs across the whole string, at the source terminals. Then move the probes to the two ends of the unknown and read Vx. Write both down with every digit the meter shows. If you use a battery, read Vs again afterward; if it changed, average the two or switch to a regulated supply.

4Calculate the resistance and sanity-check it

The current is the same everywhere in a series loop, so the voltages divide in proportion to resistance. The reference drops Vs − Vx, the unknown drops Vx, and therefore Rx = Rk × Vx ÷ (Vs − Vx). With the illustrative numbers above: 10.0 kΩ × 3.00 ÷ (9.00 − 3.00) = 10.0 kΩ × 0.5 = 5.0 kΩ. The current was 6.00 V ÷ 10.0 kΩ = 0.60 mA, and 3.00 V ÷ 0.60 mA gives the same 5.0 kΩ, which is a quick check that the arithmetic is right.

If Vx came out at 8.9 V or 0.1 V, the split is lopsided, and a small reading error turns into a large error in the answer. The chart shows why: the same ±0.01 V reading uncertainty produces a much bigger error at the extremes than at an even split.

Match the reference to the unknown

The divider method doesn't care what the supply voltage is, only how it splits. Choose a reference within about a factor of three of the unknown and your answer is limited mainly by the reference resistor's tolerance, not by the voltmeter.

5Live parts and low ohms: read volts and amps together

When the part is already carrying current, skip the reference resistor. Measure the voltage directly across the part and the current through it at the same moment, then divide. A 12 V heater pad drawing 2.0 A has an operating resistance of 12.0 ÷ 2.0 = 6.0 Ω. Read the current with the meter in series on its amps range, or with a clamp meter around one conductor, and keep the voltmeter probes on the part's own terminals so wiring drop isn't counted.

This is also the right way to measure very low resistances. Pass a known current through the part from a current-limited bench supply, then put the voltmeter probes directly on the part, inside the current connections. This four-wire hookup ignores lead and clip resistance because almost no current flows in the voltmeter leads. With 1.000 A flowing and 25.0 mV across a shunt, the shunt is 25.0 mV ÷ 1.000 A = 25.0 mΩ, a value most two-wire ohms ranges can't resolve. Our guide to multimeters for measuring low resistance covers meters with a REL zero for the two-wire version.

Expect hot and cold values to differ. A heating element's resistance changes as it warms, and a tungsten lamp filament reads many times higher when lit than on an ohmmeter at room temperature. The volts-and-amps figure is the one that predicts real current draw.

6High values: use the meter's own resistance

For resistances in the hundreds of kilohms and above, turn the voltmeter's weakness into the reference. Every voltmeter has an input resistance, Rm. Most digital meters are around 10 MΩ on DC volts (check your manual). Analog meters are rated in ohms per volt: a 20,000 Ω/V meter on its 10 V range has Rm = 200 kΩ.

First read the source directly: that's V1. Then put the unknown in series with one meter lead and read again: that's V2, lower because the unknown and the meter now share the voltage. The unknown is Rx = Rm × (V1 ÷ V2 − 1). On a 10 MΩ digital meter with V1 = 9.00 V and V2 = 4.50 V, Rx = 10 MΩ × (2 − 1) = 10 MΩ. On the 200 kΩ analog range with V1 = 9.0 V and V2 = 3.0 V, Rx = 200 kΩ × (3 − 1) = 400 kΩ. The answer is only as good as your Rm figure, so treat it as an estimate unless the manual states the input resistance precisely.

This is the same loading effect that spoils readings in analog multimeter work, used on purpose. It is useful for checking leakage paths, high-value resistors and dirty insulation on low-voltage circuits, but it is no substitute for an insulation tester on mains wiring.

How accurate is a voltmeter resistance measurement?

In the divider method, three errors add up: the reference resistor's tolerance, the voltmeter's accuracy on each reading, and loading by the voltmeter. With a 1% reference, a meter good to about 0.5% on DC volts, and the unknown close to the reference, the worst case lands around 2%. That is comparable to a basic multimeter's ohms range and good enough to tell a 4.7 kΩ resistor from a 5.6 kΩ one.

Frequently asked questions

Can a voltmeter measure resistance directly?

No. A voltmeter only reads the voltage difference between its probes, so it needs help to give you ohms. Add a known resistor in series and a steady DC source, and the two voltage readings give the resistance by ratio. Or read voltage and current together and divide. A digital multimeter's ohms range does the same thing internally with its own current source.

What is the formula for resistance from voltage?

With current known, R = V ÷ I, so 12 V across a part carrying 2 A means 6 Ω. With a known series resistor instead of an ammeter, Rx = Rk × Vx ÷ (Vs − Vx), where Vs is the supply voltage and Vx the voltage across the unknown. For example, 10 kΩ, 9 V supply and 3 V across the unknown gives 10k × 3 ÷ 6 = 5 kΩ.

What size reference resistor should I use?

Pick one close to the value you expect. The ratio method is most accurate when the two voltages are similar, roughly half the supply across each resistor. If you have no idea of the value, start with 10 kΩ, read the split, and swap to a closer resistor for a second reading. Use a 1% metal-film part and measure it first if you can.

Why does my calculated resistance come out too low on high-value resistors?

The voltmeter itself draws a little current. A typical digital meter has around 10 MΩ input resistance, which sits in parallel with whatever you probe. Across a 1 MΩ resistor that drags the reading about 9% low. Keep the unknown well below the meter's input resistance, or use the meter-resistance method, which turns that input resistance into the reference.

Can I measure the resistance of a heater or bulb while it is running?

Yes, and it's often the more useful number. Measure the voltage across the element and the current through it at the same time, then divide. A heater's hot resistance usually differs from its cold reading, and a tungsten bulb filament is many times higher when lit than when cold, so an ohmmeter reading on a cold part won't predict operating current.

Is this safe to do on mains-powered equipment?

Do the divider method only with a low-voltage battery or bench supply, never with mains. Measuring a live mains appliance's volts and amps needs a CAT-rated meter, a clamp meter for the current, and enough experience to work near exposed 120 V. If you're not comfortable with that, measure the part cold with the ohms range or ask an electrician.
