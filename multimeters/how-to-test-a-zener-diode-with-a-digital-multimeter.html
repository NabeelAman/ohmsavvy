How to Test a Zener Diode With a Digital Multimeter

To test a zener diode with a digital multimeter, start in diode mode: a good zener reads about 0.6–0.7 V forward and OL in reverse, just like an ordinary silicon diode. That proves it isn't shorted or open, but it can't show the zener voltage. To measure that, feed the zener from a DC supply through a series resistor and read the voltage across it on the DC volts range. This guide covers both tests and the maths for picking the resistor.

Keep the test at low voltage

  • Remove power before any diode-mode or resistance check, and discharge nearby capacitors in a power supply before you touch the board.
  • For the bench test, use a current-limited bench supply or a battery, never a mains-derived source without isolation. Always include the series resistor: a zener connected straight across a supply will overheat and fail.
  • Small zeners get hot if the resistor is too small. Check the power figures below before switching on.

What makes a zener different

In the forward direction a zener behaves like any silicon junction. The difference is in reverse: an ordinary rectifier blocks until it is damaged, while a zener is built to break down cleanly at a specified voltage (Vz) and hold close to it across a range of currents. Zeners serve as references, clamps and simple shunt regulators. Our general guide to testing a diode with a multimeter covers the forward and reverse diode-mode checks for rectifiers, LEDs and Schottkys. This page goes further and measures the zener voltage itself.

Vz

Zener (breakdown) voltage, the figure printed on the part, e.g. 5V1 = 5.1 V.

Izt

Test current at which the datasheet specifies Vz. Measure near it for a fair comparison.

Power

Common small glass zeners are 0.5 W; larger axial parts are 1 W or more.

1Do the diode-mode forward check

With the zener out of circuit, or with one leg lifted, select diode mode. Put the red probe on the anode and the black probe on the cathode (the end with the band). A healthy part typically shows around 0.6–0.7 V, the normal silicon forward drop at the meter's small test current. Swap the probes: the display should read OL for most zeners.

There is one exception. A low-voltage zener, say 2.4 V or 2.7 V, can break down at the meter's open-circuit test voltage and show a reading in reverse too. That is not a short: a shorted part reads close to zero both ways, usually with the continuity beeper sounding.

Why a multimeter can't show the zener voltage directly

Diode mode works by pushing a small, fixed current through the part and displaying the voltage that develops. The source inside the meter has a limited compliance voltage, commonly in the region of 2 to 3.5 V depending on the model, so it simply cannot reach the breakdown point of a 5.1 V or 12 V zener. The display reads OL because the meter has run out of voltage, not because the zener has failed.

Two tests, two questions

Diode mode answers "is the junction intact?" The bench test answers "does it break down at the right voltage?" A zener can pass the first and still fail the second if it has drifted.

2Measure the breakdown voltage with a supply and resistor

Build the circuit below on a breadboard or with clip leads. The zener is connected in reverse: the cathode (band) goes toward the positive side through the series resistor, the anode goes to the negative. Set the meter to DC volts and put it directly across the zener.

  1. Choose a supply voltage a few volts above Vz, for example 12 V for a 5.1 V part.
  2. Calculate the resistor (next section) and fit the nearest standard value at or above it.
  3. Switch on and read the DC voltage across the zener once it settles.
  4. Optional: raise the supply a few volts. The zener voltage should barely move while the voltage across the resistor takes up the difference. That flat response is the zener working.

If you want the actual current, measure the voltage across the resistor and divide by its value, the method explained in measuring voltage across a resistor.

Sizing the series resistor: the maths

The resistor drops whatever voltage the zener doesn't, so by Ohm's law:

R = (Vsupply − Vz) ÷ Iz

For a 5.1 V zener on 12 V at 5 mA: (12 − 5.1) ÷ 0.005 = 1,380 Ω. A standard 1.5 kΩ gives about 4.6 mA. The resistor dissipates 6.9 V × 4.6 mA, roughly 32 mW, and the zener about 23 mW, both well inside a 0.25 W resistor and a 0.5 W zener. For a precise comparison, aim for the Izt in the datasheet; 1 W parts are often specified at tens of milliamps, so recheck both power figures if you raise the current.

What you seeWhat it usually means
≈0.6–0.7 V forward, OL reverse; bench reading near VzGood zener
Near 0 V in both directions, beeper soundsShorted junction, a common failure after overload
OL in both directions; bench reading equals the supplyOpen zener: no current flows, so no drop across R
Forward check passes but bench reading is well off VzDrifted part, wrong part fitted, or test current far from Izt
Bench reading creeps down as the part warmsOften normal for higher-voltage zeners; heavy drift suggests overheating or damage

Judge closeness with your meter's accuracy in mind; our guide to multimeter accuracy for electronics repair explains how to read a ±(% + counts) spec.

Which multimeter you need to test a zener

Both tests need only diode mode and a DC volts range, which almost every digital meter has. A higher count display shows the zener voltage with more digits, which helps when comparing a reference zener against a tight tolerance. Frequently asked questions

Can I test a zener diode in circuit?

Partly. With the board powered, you can measure the DC voltage across the zener and compare it with its rating: a regulator zener should sit close to its marked voltage. Unpowered diode-mode checks in circuit are less reliable, because parallel parts can make a good zener look shorted. If a reading looks wrong, lift one leg before condemning it.

How do I read the markings on a zener diode?

Small glass zeners are often printed with the voltage directly, such as 5V1 for 5.1 V or 12 for 12 V, sometimes with a series prefix like BZX55C. Others carry a part number, for example 1N4733A, which you look up in a datasheet to get the zener voltage and test current. The band always marks the cathode.

Can I use a 9V battery to test a zener diode?

Yes, for zeners rated a few volts below 9 V. A 9 V battery and a series resistor of around 1 kΩ make a simple test source for a 5.1 V zener, giving a few milliamps. It can't test zeners above roughly 7 V, because the battery needs headroom above the zener voltage to push current through the resistor.

Why does my zener read lower than its rated voltage?

Zener voltage depends on current. The rated figure is specified at a test current on the datasheet, and at much lower current the reading is usually somewhat below it, especially for zeners under about 6 V, which have a soft knee. Tolerance matters too: a 5% part can legitimately read a little high or low.

What is the difference between a zener and a regular diode test?

The forward check is the same: both read about 0.6-0.7 V in diode mode. A regular rectifier is only expected to block in reverse, so the test ends there. A zener is designed to conduct in reverse at a set voltage, so a complete test also measures that breakdown voltage with an external supply and resistor.
