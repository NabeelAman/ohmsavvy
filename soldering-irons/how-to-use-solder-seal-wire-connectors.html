HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Use Solder Seal Wire Connectors

To use a solder seal wire connector, strip both wires, slide the connector on, overlap the bare strands so they sit under the solder ring in the middle, and heat the connector evenly with a heat gun until the solder melts and flows into the strands and the colored rings at each end melt and seal. Let it cool without moving it, then pull-test the joint. This guide covers the strip length, matching the connector to the wire gauge, heat gun versus lighter, how to tell a good joint from a bad one, and how to check the splice with a multimeter.

Heat, fumes and live wiring

  • Disconnect power first. On a vehicle, disconnect the battery negative before cutting into a harness, and never splice airbag (SRS) wiring, which is usually in yellow loom; that is a job for a dealer.
  • Heat guns reach several hundred degrees at the nozzle. Keep the gun moving, shield nearby plastic, wiring and fuel lines, and set the gun on its stand, nozzle up, when you put it down.
  • Never use an open flame near fuel, batteries or in an engine bay that may have fuel vapor. Work in fresh air; burning heat-shrink and flux give off irritating fumes.
  • Solder seal connectors are not approved for building wiring in walls and panels. Use listed connectors, and call a licensed electrician for house wiring.

What a solder seal connector is

A solder seal connector, also sold as a solder sleeve or solder splice, is a short length of clear heat-shrink tubing with three rings inside: a band of low-temperature solder with flux in the middle, and a ring of meltable sealant, usually colored, near each end. One heating does three jobs at once. The solder melts and joins the strands, the tube shrinks around the joint, and the end rings melt to seal out water.

The clear tube is the point. Unlike a crimp, you can see whether the solder flowed into the strands, so a good joint and a bad one look different. The low-melting solder is what lets a heat gun finish the joint without an iron, and it is also its limit: it is softer and melts at a lower temperature than the tin-lead or tin-silver-copper solder you'd use with an iron, so these joints don't belong next to an exhaust manifold or anywhere that runs hot.

1Match the connector to the wire gauge

Kits are color-coded by wire size, and the size is chosen by the combined bundle that goes inside. Many kits follow the familiar crimp-terminal colors, red for small, blue for medium and yellow for large wire, and some add white for very fine wire, but the exact gauges vary between brands, so use the chart on your kit. A connector too big for the wire leaves the end seals loose; one too small splits or won't slide over the insulation.

YellowAbout 12–10 AWGHeavier feeds; consider a crimp instead

2Strip the wires

Strip enough insulation that the two bare ends can overlap fully under the solder ring with the insulation still reaching under the end seals. On small connectors that is often about 1/4 to 3/8 inch (6 to 10 mm); check by holding the connector beside the wire before you cut. Use a proper stripper so you don't nick or cut strands. The copper should be bright; old, dark or green wire won't take solder well, so cut back to clean copper. Our guide to why solder won't stick to copper wire explains why oxidized wire causes dry joints.

3Slide on and overlap the strands under the ring

Slide the connector onto one wire first. Then lay the two bare ends side by side or interleave the strands, and give them a light twist so they stay together, keeping the bundle no thicker than it has to be. Slide the connector back so the solder band sits over the middle of the overlap and the insulation of each wire reaches under its end seal. This placement is the most common mistake: if the solder ring sits on insulation or only one wire, nothing joins. For an in-line splice where you can't pass the connector over a wire end, the overlap method works; for a butt joint, push the two ends in from each side until they meet under the solder.

4Heat it evenly until the solder flows

Start heating at the middle with a heat gun on a medium setting, a few inches away, and keep turning the wire or moving the gun so every side gets the same heat. The tube shrinks first. Keep going until the solder ring collapses and you see it wick into the strands, turning them silver, then move to each end in turn until the sealing rings melt and squeeze out slightly. A small connector takes only seconds.

Tin-bismuth, a common low-temperature solder, melts at 138 °C (281 °F), well below the 183 °C of traditional 63/37 tin-lead. The chart compares that melting point with the hot air settings a rework station offers.

A butane lighter will work in a pinch, but hold the connector above the tip of the flame, keep it moving and expect soot. Avoid the blue part of the flame and anything that blackens the tube, because you lose the ability to see the solder.

5Inspect and pull-test

Let the joint cool for a minute without flexing it. A good joint shows solder that has lost its ring shape and flowed through the strands, a tube shrunk smooth along its length, and both end rings melted onto the insulation. Then pull on both wires firmly, as hard as you'd expect the wiring to be tugged in service. If either wire slides, the strands weren't wetted; cut it out and redo with a new connector. Don't reheat a scorched connector hoping to save it.

6Check the splice with a multimeter

A continuity beep proves the wire is connected, not that the joint is good, because a few strands touching will still beep. With the circuit back on and carrying its normal current, set the meter to DC millivolts and measure across the splice, one probe on each wire just beyond the tube (pierce-probe or measure at the nearest connectors). A good splice drops very little. Divide by the current for the joint resistance: 10 mV at 5 A is 2 mΩ. Compare with the same length of plain wire to see what the joint itself adds.

A dry joint shows up as a much higher drop that changes when you flex the wire. The millivolt measuring guide explains the range setup and the heat math behind a bad connection.

Solder seal vs crimp vs soldered splice

MethodStrengthsWeaknessesBest for
Solder seal connectorOne step, sealed, visible joint, no ironLow-temp solder; needs even heat; stiff spotSmall repairs, trailer and lighting wires
Crimp + adhesive heat shrinkStrong, vibration-tolerant, repeatable with the right toolNeeds a proper crimper; joint is hiddenAutomotive, marine, higher current
Soldered splice + heat shrinkExcellent conductivity, compactNeeds an iron; solder wicks and stiffens wireElectronics, fine wire, bench work

The stiff spot matters. Solder wicks along the strands and makes that section rigid, and where rigid meets flexible, wire fatigues under vibration. Support a solder seal splice so it doesn't flex at the ends. If you'd rather use an iron, our guide on how to solder wires together covers the classic splice, and soldering without overheating insulation helps on short leads.

The clear tube is your inspection window

A solder seal joint is only as good as what you can see through the tube: solder flowed into both sets of strands, a smooth shrink and melted end rings. If you can't see that, the joint isn't done.

Frequently asked questions

Can you use a lighter on solder seal connectors?

You can, and many kits show it, but it is the least reliable heat source. A flame heats one side, can scorch the tube or the wire insulation before the solder melts, and leaves soot that hides whether the solder flowed. Keep the flame moving and use the hot air above the tip. A heat gun or hot air station gives even heat and a joint you can inspect.

Are solder seal connectors as good as crimping?

For low-current, low-vibration repairs they can work well, and the sealed tube keeps moisture out. They rely on low-temperature solder and the tube for strength, so they are not a substitute for a proper crimp where the wiring flexes constantly or carries high current. Many automotive and marine technicians prefer a quality crimp plus adhesive-lined heat shrink for those jobs.

How do I know if a solder seal connector is done?

The solder band in the middle has melted, lost its sharp edges and flowed into the strands so they look silver and wetted, the tube has shrunk tight onto the insulation along its length, and the colored rings at each end have melted and squeezed out slightly. If the solder still looks like a solid ring, keep heating.

What temperature melts solder seal connectors?

Makers rarely publish it, but the rings are a low-temperature solder so they melt below the point that damages ordinary wire insulation. Tin-bismuth, a common low-temperature alloy, melts at 138 °C (281 °F). A heat gun on a medium setting held a few inches away is plenty; full heat close up tends to scorch the tube before the solder flows.

Can you splice three wires with a solder seal connector?

Only if the combined wires fit the connector's gauge range and all the strands sit under the solder ring. Two wires from one side and one from the other usually needs the next size up. Overfilling a connector leaves strands dry and can split the tube. For multi-wire junctions, a crimp splice or a soldered joint under adhesive heat shrink is more dependable.
