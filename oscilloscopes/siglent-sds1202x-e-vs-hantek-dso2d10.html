Siglent SDS1202X-E vs Hantek DSO2D10

Siglent SDS1202X-E vs Hantek DSO2D10 is an unusually even match on paper: both are two-channel benchtops with 1 GSa/s and a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen. They part ways on what surrounds that core. Both include serial trigger and decode as standard. The Siglent doubles the bandwidth to 200 MHz and adds a published 100,000 wfm/s update rate and deeper memory; the Hantek adds a built-in 25 MHz waveform generator.

Spec by spec

With channels, screen and peak sample rate tied, the charts focus on what isn't: bandwidth, and two figures that follow directly from it under standard rules of thumb. The last chart shows where the sample rate goes once both channels are on.

One consequence of the Siglent's wide bandwidth is easy to miss. At 1 GSa/s it takes five samples per cycle of a 200 MHz sine; with both channels on, 500 MSa/s leaves two and a half, only just above the Nyquist minimum of two. Interpolation fills in the trace, but for work near the top of its range you get the most faithful picture with one channel on. The Hantek, at 1 GSa/s against 100 MHz, takes ten samples per cycle on one channel and five with both on. Our explainer on what GSa/s means covers why that ratio matters.

What each one can and can't measure

Bandwidth is specified at the frequency where a sine input reads 3 dB low, about 70% of its real amplitude, so a scope reads accurately only some way below its rating. That is why the 3–5x rule exists. Applied here, the DSO2D10 is comfortable up to roughly 20–33 MHz and the SDS1202X-E up to roughly 40–67 MHz. Both cover microcontroller GPIO, UART, I2C, typical SPI, PWM motor drives and audio. The difference appears on square edges, whose harmonics run far above the fundamental. A 25 MHz SPI clock or a fast MOSFET switching node still looks square on the Siglent but starts to round off on the Hantek, and overshoot and ringing measured near the Hantek's limit will understate the real thing. See what oscilloscope bandwidth is for the full reasoning.

Bandwidth is not the whole story for faults that come and go. A scope is blind while it processes one capture before taking the next, and the Siglent's published rate of 100,000 waveforms per second (or 400,000 in sequence mode) shortens that blind time, so a rare runt pulse or glitch is more likely to land on screen. Its 14 Mpts of memory on one channel also lets you capture a long stretch of a bus at a fast timebase. Hantek lists 8 Mpts on one channel (4 Mpts each with two), a little over half the Siglent's, and doesn't state an update rate for the DSO2D10.

What the Siglent can't do on its own is stimulate a circuit. Measuring a filter's cut-off, an amplifier's gain or how a sensor input responds needs a known signal going in. The DSO2D10 provides one from its own output, up to 25 MHz, with channel 1 on the input and channel 2 on the output. Siglent's Bode plot and AWG features belong to the four-channel models, so an SDS1202X-E owner needs a separate generator for the same test. The generator doesn't add channels, though: once it drives a circuit, both Hantek inputs are usually in use.

Mains and switching supplies

Both are mains-powered bench scopes, and on such scopes the probe ground clip is normally tied to mains earth. Clipping it to the primary side of a switching supply or any mains-referenced node can short that point to earth. Measure only the isolated secondary with a standard probe, and use a differential probe for anything mains-referenced.

The trade in one line

Buy the SDS1202X-E to see fast signals and rare glitches more completely; buy the DSO2D10 to make the test signal and see the response on one instrument. If you need four channels instead, neither fits; see Siglent SDS1104X-E vs SDS1202X-E and Hantek DSO2D10 vs Siglent SDS1104X-E.

More on the Hantek is in our Hantek DSO2D10 review. For every model we cover, see the beginner oscilloscope roundup and the oscilloscope buying guide.

Choose the Siglent SDS1202X-E if…

  • ✓You look at fast logic edges or switching nodes where 200 MHz of bandwidth earns its keep
  • ✓You hunt intermittent glitches and want a fast 100,000 wfm/s update rate
  • ✓You want the deeper memory: 14 Mpts on one channel against the Hantek's 8 Mpts
  • ✓You already own a signal source, or rarely need one

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓You need a scope and a test-signal source but have bench room for one instrument
  • ✓Your signals sit comfortably below 20–33 MHz, where 100 MHz is enough
  • ✓You test filters, amplifiers or sensor inputs by driving them and watching the result

The bottom line

If you don't need a built-in signal source, the Siglent SDS1202X-E is the stronger scope: twice the bandwidth, a published 100,000 wfm/s update rate and 14 Mpts of memory against 8 Mpts, on the same two channels and screen. Both decode I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN as standard. The Hantek DSO2D10 is the better buy when bench space is tight and your work means driving a circuit with a test signal, where its 25 MHz generator replaces a second instrument.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Siglent SDS1202X-E better than the Hantek DSO2D10?+

As a scope, yes on the published data: 200 MHz against 100 MHz, 14 Mpts against 8 Mpts of memory, and a stated 100,000 wfm/s update rate, with the same two channels and 800 × 480 screen. The Hantek is the better instrument if you need its built-in 25 MHz waveform generator.

Does the Siglent SDS1202X-E have a built-in signal generator?+

No. Siglent's AWG module, along with the Bode plot, MSO and Wi-Fi options, belongs to the four-channel SDS1000X-E models, not the SDS1202X-E. If you need a generator in the same box, the Hantek DSO2D10 has a one-channel output up to 25 MHz.

Do I need 200 MHz, or is the Hantek's 100 MHz enough?+

By the 3-5x rule, 100 MHz handles signals up to roughly 20-33 MHz, which covers most hobby microcontroller, audio and serial-bus work. 200 MHz, good to roughly 40-67 MHz, matters when you judge fast edges, ringing on switching nodes, or faster SPI clocks.

What sample rate does each scope give with both channels on?+

Siglent states 500 MSa/s per channel for the SDS1202X-E with both channels on, down from 1 GSa/s with one. Hantek lists the same split for the DSO2D10: 1 GSa/s on a single channel and 500 MSa/s with two.

Can either scope decode I2C or SPI?+

The SDS1202X-E includes I2C, SPI, UART, CAN and LIN trigger and decode as standard, per Siglent. Hantek lists the same five as standard on the DSO2D10 (UART/RS232, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN), with protocol triggers, so bus decoding doesn't separate them.
