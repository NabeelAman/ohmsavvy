FNIRSI DSO152 vs Siglent SDS1104X-E

FNIRSI DSO152 vs Siglent SDS1104X-E is not a close contest on specs: the Siglent has 500 times the bandwidth, 400 times the sample rate and four channels to the FNIRSI's one. The real question is which job you have. The DSO152 is a very cheap pocket scope for audio-range checks and learning; the SDS1104X-E is a 100MHz bench scope for microcontroller, digital and multi-signal debugging that you can grow into.

What each one can and can't measure

An oscilloscope's rated bandwidth is the frequency at which a sine wave is shown about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its true amplitude. That is why the usual advice is to buy three to five times the bandwidth of your signal. Applied to the DSO152's 200kHz, that gives comfortable, accurate readings up to roughly 40 to 65kHz. That range includes the whole audio band, mains-frequency waveforms on the low-voltage side of a transformer, slow sensor outputs, and PWM in the low tens of kilohertz. It excludes almost everything else in modern digital electronics. Our explainer on what oscilloscope bandwidth means covers the rule in more detail.

Square waves are where the gap becomes obvious. A digital edge is built from the fundamental plus many odd harmonics, so a scope needs far more bandwidth than the clock frequency to show a crisp edge. On a 200kHz scope, a 100kHz square wave keeps its period but loses its sharp corners, and anything in the megahertz range, such as a typical microcontroller clock or an SPI bus, is beyond what it can show meaningfully. The Siglent's 100MHz, by the same rule, is comfortable on signals up to roughly 20 to 33MHz, which covers many hobby microcontroller clocks and serial buses with headroom left over.

Sample rate sets a second limit. Nyquist says you need more than two samples per cycle to capture a frequency at all, and in practice a scope needs several times that to draw a waveform's shape faithfully. At 2.5 MSa/s the DSO152 takes about 125 samples per cycle of a 20kHz tone, which is plenty, but only 25 per cycle at 100kHz. The Siglent's 1 GSa/s, or 500 MSa/s per channel with all four on, is matched to its 100MHz front end. See what GSa/s means for how sample rate and bandwidth work together.

Channel count decides what kind of question you can ask. With one channel, the DSO152 can tell you whether a signal is present and roughly what shape it is. It cannot tell you whether an amplifier's output is distorted relative to its input, how much a signal is delayed through a circuit, or whether data changes at the right point on a clock edge, because each of those needs two traces on the same time base. The Siglent shows up to four at once.

Two different tools, not a good and bad version of one

The DSO152 is best treated as a pocket diagnostic and a cheap way to learn, and its own review makes the same point: it is not a scaled-down bench scope. The SDS1104X-E is a bench scope with headroom, and its main drawback for a pure beginner is that it is more instrument, and more money, than a first look at a waveform needs. Many people end up owning a pocket scope and a bench scope for exactly that reason.

Mains and high-voltage measurements

On a mains-powered bench scope such as the Siglent, the probe ground clip is connected to mains earth through the power cord. Clipping it onto a mains-referenced point in a circuit can create a short circuit through the scope. Measure mains-powered circuits through an isolation transformer or with a differential probe, and check the input voltage rating of whichever scope and probe you use before connecting to anything above low-voltage electronics.

Choose the FNIRSI DSO152 if…

  • ✓You want to see your first waveforms for very little money before committing to a bench scope
  • ✓Your signals are audio-range or slower, comfortably under roughly 40 to 65kHz
  • ✓You want a scope that fits in a pocket for quick checks away from the bench

Choose the Siglent SDS1104X-E if…

  • ✓You debug microcontrollers, clocks, serial buses or fast PWM edges
  • ✓You need to compare two or more signals at once, such as input against output or clock against data
  • ✓You want one bench scope you won't outgrow as your projects get more demanding

The bottom line

Buy the FNIRSI DSO152 if you want to see audio-range and other slow signals, or just learn what a scope does, for as little money as possible, and you accept a single channel and a 200kHz ceiling. Buy the Siglent SDS1104X-E if you work with microcontrollers, digital signals or anything that needs two signals compared: its 100MHz, four channels and 1 GSa/s cover far more work, and it is the one of the two you won't outgrow.

Frequently asked questions

Can the FNIRSI DSO152 replace a Siglent SDS1104X-E?+

No. The DSO152 has one channel and 200kHz of bandwidth, against four channels and 100MHz on the Siglent. It suits audio-range checks and learning the basics, while the Siglent handles microcontroller clocks, serial buses and multi-signal timing work the pocket scope cannot show.

Can the FNIRSI DSO152 show Arduino or SPI signals?+

Only the slowest ones, and only roughly. By the 3-5x bandwidth rule its 200kHz is comfortable up to about 40-65kHz, so a typical microcontroller clock in the megahertz range, or a fast SPI bus, is far beyond it. Slow PWM and low-rate serial lines will appear, but with rounded edges.

Is the Siglent SDS1104X-E too much scope for a beginner?+

For learning the basics, often yes, and it costs more than the Rigol DS1054Z. It makes sense if you already know your projects involve faster digital signals or multi-signal debugging, because 100MHz and four channels leave room to grow rather than forcing an upgrade later.

Does the sample rate gap between 2.5 MSa/s and 1 GSa/s matter?+

Yes, for anything beyond slow signals. A scope needs several samples per cycle to draw a waveform's shape. At 2.5 MSa/s the DSO152 gets about 125 samples per cycle of a 20kHz tone but only 25 at 100kHz. The Siglent's 1 GSa/s, or 500 MSa/s per channel with four on, matches its 100MHz bandwidth.

Is it worth buying a pocket scope before a bench scope?+

It can be. The DSO152 is a low-cost way to find out whether you will use a scope often. If you keep reaching for it and hitting its single-channel, 200kHz limits, that is a clear sign a bench scope such as the SDS1104X-E is worth the money. If it sits in a drawer, you learned that cheaply.
