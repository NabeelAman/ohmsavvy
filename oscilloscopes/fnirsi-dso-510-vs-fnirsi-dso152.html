FNIRSI DSO-510 vs DSO152: Portable Oscilloscopes Compared

Both of these are single-channel, pocket-sized FNIRSI scopes aimed at quick diagnostics rather than bench work. The DSO-510 is the more capable of the two — 50 times the bandwidth and a built-in signal generator — while the DSO152 undercuts it on price for the most basic checks.

Key differences

  • →Bandwidth: the DSO-510 is rated 10 MHz, 50 times the DSO152's 200 kHz — a large practical gap, not a marginal one.
  • →Sample rate: 48 MS/s on the DSO-510 against 2.5 MSa/s on the DSO152, keeping pace with the bandwidth difference.
  • →Signal generator: only the DSO-510 has one built in (0-50 kHz DDS, 13 waveforms); the DSO152 has none.
  • →Documentation: this Pick's DSO-510 sourcing (FNIRSI's own product page) is considerably more detailed than what's available for the DSO152, which is documented mainly through its own listing.
  • →Naming: the DSO-510's own listing carries an internal naming inconsistency (a marketing graphic calls it "DSO-153"); the DSO152 has no equivalent caution.

Spec by spec

These aren't close on the numbers that matter most for a pocket scope: bandwidth and sample rate. The DSO-510 isn't a modest step up from the DSO152 — it's a different class of tool entirely.

At 200 kHz, the DSO152 is really only suited to audio-range and basic low-speed logic signals. The DSO-510's 10 MHz opens up a much wider range of general electronics and automotive diagnostic work, and its built-in generator adds a capability the DSO152 simply doesn't have at any frequency.

Where each pocket scope runs out of road

A rated bandwidth is the frequency where a sine wave already displays about 30% short of its true height (the −3 dB point), so it marks the edge of the usable range rather than its middle. A common rule of thumb is to keep your signal at or below roughly a fifth to a third of that figure if you want amplitudes to read within a few percent. The map below plots both inputs on one log-frequency axis with that rule applied.

Read that way, the DSO152 is comfortable up to roughly 40–67 kHz: fine for the audio band, slow PWM and sensor outputs, but square waves lose their sharp corners well before that, because a clean edge needs several odd harmonics above the fundamental. The DSO-510's comfortable zone reaches roughly 2–3 MHz, enough to show the edges of a few-hundred-kilohertz PWM drive or a slow serial line with recognizable shape. The sample rates follow the same pattern: at 2.5 MSa/s the DSO152 gets about 25 points across one cycle of a 100 kHz signal, while the DSO-510's 48 MS/s gets 480.

Notice too where the DSO-510's generator sits: it covers the audio band and a little beyond, which is the right range for injecting a test tone into an amplifier stage or a filter and watching the result on the same screen. It isn't meant for clock or RF work. For more on how bandwidth and sample rate relate, see what GSa/s means.

FNIRSI DSO-510 vs DSO152: portable scopes compared

Despite both being marketed as pocket oscilloscopes, these serve different purposes once you look past the form factor.

If you need a genuinely useful diagnostic tool: the DSO-510

10 MHz bandwidth and a built-in generator make the DSO-510 workable for real diagnostic tasks — checking a sensor signal, a PWM line, or a basic automotive waveform — in a device that still fits in a pocket. The generator doubles as a way to inject a known test signal without separate equipment.

If you just want the cheapest possible single-channel scope: the DSO152

For strictly audio-range or very slow signals — and only when price is the deciding factor — the DSO152 still does the basic job of showing a waveform on a screen. It's a learning tool more than a diagnostic one at 200 kHz.

Check the actual model name before buying the DSO-510

Because the DSO-510's own listing contains a naming inconsistency — a seller comparison graphic calls the matching spec column "DSO-153" rather than "DSO-510" — it's worth a quick look at the current listing yourself before ordering, even though FNIRSI's site and the listing title agree on "DSO-510".

Both of these are pocket tools for quick checks, not replacements for a benchtop oscilloscope. If you need multiple channels, deep memory or serial decoding, look at a benchtop model like the Rigol DS1054Z instead.

Choose the FNIRSI DSO-510 if…

  • ✓You want to check signals well above basic audio range
  • ✓You want a built-in signal generator in the same pocket device
  • ✓You're doing automotive or general electronics diagnostics, not just toy projects
  • ✓You want USB-C charging and a lithium battery rated for about 4 hours

Choose the FNIRSI DSO152 if…

  • ✓Your signals are strictly low-frequency: audio, basic control logic
  • ✓Price is the only thing that matters for an occasional-use tool
  • ✓You want the smallest possible footprint and don't need a generator
  • ✓You're learning basic waveform concepts for the first time, not diagnosing a specific fault

The bottom line

The FNIRSI DSO-510 and DSO152 both fit in a pocket, but they're not really competing for the same job. The DSO-510's 10 MHz bandwidth, faster sampling and built-in generator make it the far more useful diagnostic tool; the DSO152 only makes sense if price is the only consideration and your signals are strictly low-frequency.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FNIRSI DSO-510 much better than the DSO152?+

Yes, substantially — 10 MHz bandwidth against 200 kHz (50x), and 48 MS/s against 2.5 MSa/s (about 19x), plus a built-in signal generator the DSO152 doesn't have. The DSO152 only makes sense when price is the deciding factor and your signals are strictly low-frequency.

Does the FNIRSI DSO152 have a signal generator?+

No. Only the DSO-510 includes one in this site's sourcing — a basic DDS generator covering 0-50 kHz across 13 waveform types.

Why does the DSO-510 have a naming caution on this site?+

The DSO-510's own Amazon listing includes a seller-made comparison graphic that labels the matching spec column "DSO-153" rather than "DSO-510", even though the listing title and FNIRSI's current product page both call it "DSO-510" with identical specs. It's worth checking the current listing yourself before buying.

Can either of these replace a benchtop oscilloscope?+

No — both are single-channel pocket tools meant for quick checks, not a substitute for a multi-channel benchtop scope with deep memory and serial decoding, such as the Rigol DS1054Z.

Is 200 kHz of bandwidth enough to see a square wave?+

Only a slow one. A square wave's sharp edges are built from odd harmonics well above its fundamental, so a 200 kHz scope like the DSO152 visibly rounds the corners once the fundamental climbs into the tens of kilohertz. The DSO-510's 10 MHz keeps edges recognizable into the hundreds of kilohertz, which is why it suits PWM and slow serial lines better.
