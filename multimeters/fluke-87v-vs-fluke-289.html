Fluke 87V vs Fluke 289: Which Is Better for Professional Use?

For most professional electricians weighing the Fluke 87V vs Fluke 289, the 87V is the better daily troubleshooting meter and the 289 is the better recording and documentation meter. Both are True RMS and carry the same CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating. The 289 adds a 50,000-count display, LoZ, a 50 Ω low-ohm range and TrendCapture logging; the 87V answers with peak capture, a published temperature range and a lifetime warranty.

Resolution and DC accuracy side by side

The two meters share a safety rating, so the first measurable gap is on the display. The 87V normally runs at 6,000 counts and can switch to a 19,999-count high-resolution mode. The 289 shows up to 50,000 counts all the time, roughly two and a half times the 87V's best. The basic DC volts figure follows the same pattern: 0.025% for the 289 against ±(0.05% + 1) for the 87V.

Turned into volts on a 24.00 V DC control circuit, the 87V's worst case is 0.012 V from the percentage plus one count of 0.01 V, about ±0.022 V. The 289's percentage term on the same reading is 0.006 V; our data doesn't give its digit term, so its full allowance isn't something we can state. Either way, both meters are far tighter than a panel check needs. Where the extra digits pay off is in spotting slow drift: a supply creeping from 24.012 V to 24.031 V shows plainly on 50,000 counts. This is arithmetic from published specs, not a measurement.

Logging and documentation: the 289's real advantage

The Fluke 289 can record a circuit over time and plot the readings on its own screen, a feature Fluke calls TrendCapture. You leave it connected, come back later, and scroll a graph instead of hoping you were looking at the right moment. For an electrician answering "the lights flicker sometimes" or "the drive trips once a shift," that record turns a hunch into evidence you can show a customer, a supervisor or the utility.

The 87V takes a different approach to intermittent faults. Its Peak Capture catches events as short as 250 µs, and its 32-segment bar graph refreshes 40 times a second, so you see a value swing while you watch. That is real-time troubleshooting rather than unattended recording: great for catching a contactor chatter while you stand at the panel, less useful for a fault that shows up at 3 a.m. Our 87V review lists no logging memory or trend graph for it.

Logging is a workflow, not just a feature

If your job includes commissioning reports, warranty claims or proving a power-quality complaint, the 289 saves a second trip and a separate logger. If you mostly diagnose a fault and fix it on the spot, the 87V's instant reading and bar graph fit that rhythm better. Our guide to checking for voltage fluctuations shows what a logged reading is good for.

Electrical-floor features: LoZ, drive filtering and safety

Two features matter on every commercial and industrial job. The first is LoZ, a low-input-impedance mode that drains the capacitively coupled "ghost" voltage you see on an open conductor running beside a live one. The 289 has it. Fluke lists no LoZ on the 87V, so an 87V user who meets an unexpected reading on a supposedly dead circuit has to reason it out or reach for another tester.

The second is a low-pass filter for variable-frequency drive outputs. Both meters have one, so both can read the fundamental a motor responds to rather than a figure inflated by switching edges. Fluke publishes the 87V's filter at 3 dB down at 1 kHz; our 289 data confirms the filter but not its cutoff. Both meters are True RMS, which matters on LED drivers, switch-mode supplies and drive inputs.

Same rating, same precautions

A CAT IV 600V rating covers the service entrance and meter base, but it doesn't make live work safe on its own. Wear rated PPE, follow your site's lockout and arc-flash procedures, and prove the meter on a known live source before and after you verify a circuit is dead. Drive DC bus capacitors can hold charge after power is removed. Leave utility-side work to the utility or a licensed electrician.

Low ohms and microamps

The 289's 50 Ω range resolves 1 mΩ using a 10 mA test current, and relative mode subtracts the test-lead resistance. That lets you compare contact resistance across breaker poles, check a bolted busbar joint or confirm a current shunt without a separate milliohm meter. It is still a two-terminal measurement, not true 4-wire Kelvin, so clip placement and lead zeroing affect the result. Our 87V data lists resistance up to 50 MΩ at ±(0.2% + 1) but no dedicated low-ohm range.

On microamps the meters are closer. Both resolve 0.01 µA DC, enough for HVAC flame-rod current. The 289 also publishes its burden voltage, about 102 µV per µA on the 500 µA range: at an illustrative 5 µA flame signal the meter drops roughly 5 × 102 µV ≈ 0.5 mV, small next to a flame circuit. Our 87V data doesn't state its burden figure.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 289 better than the Fluke 87V for electricians?+

Not for most daily work. Both carry CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V and True RMS, and the 87V's fast bar graph, peak capture and temperature input suit live troubleshooting. The 289 is better when you need to log a circuit, use LoZ on a dead conductor, or check contact resistance down to 1 mΩ.

Does the Fluke 87V have data logging?+

Not according to the data behind our 87V review, which lists Peak Capture down to 250 µs and a 32-segment bar graph updating 40 times a second, but no logging memory or on-screen trend graph. If you need to leave a meter connected and review readings later, the Fluke 289's TrendCapture is built for that job.

Do the Fluke 87V and 289 have the same safety rating?+

Yes. Both are rated CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V, so either can be used from the service entrance down to branch circuits and on 480 V three-phase equipment. The rating protects against transients; it doesn't replace PPE, lockout procedures or proving the meter on a known live source before and after testing.

Which is more accurate on DC volts, the 87V or the 289?+

On paper the 289. Our data lists 0.025% basic DC volts accuracy for the 289 against ±(0.05% + 1) for the 87V, and the 289 shows 50,000 counts against the 87V's 19,999 in high-resolution mode. On a 24 V control circuit both are far tighter than troubleshooting needs; the gap matters most when you track slow drift or calibration work.

Does the Fluke 289 have a low-pass filter for VFDs?+

Yes, our 289 data lists a low-pass filter alongside LoZ, so it can read a drive's output fundamental like the 87V can. Fluke publishes the 87V's filter as 3 dB down at 1 kHz; our data doesn't give the 289's cutoff. For drive work the two are roughly equal, and the decision comes down to logging versus field speed.

How long do the batteries last in the Fluke 87V and Fluke 289?+

Fluke rates the 87V at about 400 hours, per the specifications behind our review. Our data for the 289 doesn't state a battery life, so we can't compare them directly. If you plan long unattended logging runs on a 289, check Fluke's manual for its battery figure and carry spares, since a flat battery ends the recording.
