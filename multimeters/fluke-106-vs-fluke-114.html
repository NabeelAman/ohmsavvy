Fluke 106 vs Fluke 114: Which Compact Multimeter Should You Buy?

The Fluke 106 vs 114 differences run in opposite directions. The 106 is the genuinely compact one, a 200 g palm-sized meter that reads amps and capacitance but isn't True RMS. The 114 is a full-size 110-series body that does less, measuring only volts, ohms and continuity, yet does those with True RMS, an AutoVolt low-impedance input, a bar graph and Min/Max/Average. Pick by the measurement you can't do without, not by size.

  • →The 114 is True RMS and has AutoVolt, a low-impedance 600 V position that picks AC or DC for you; the 106 is average-responding with a conventional high-impedance input only.

Only one of these is really compact

Searchers often lump the 114 in with Fluke's small meters because it does so few things, but physically it shares the same 167 × 84 × 46 mm housing as the 115 and 117. The 106 belongs to a different family: the 101/106/107 pocket line, built around two AAA cells. Drawn to the same scale, the gap is obvious.

The bulk buys the 114 a 9 V battery rated for twice the hours, a bar graph that refreshes 32 times a second and a backlight. The 106 trades all three for something you can drop in a shirt pocket. If "compact" is your top requirement, the honest answer is the 106, and the real question becomes whether you can live without True RMS and LoZ.

Two ways to be wrong about a dead wire

The biggest practical gap is how each meter behaves on an open conductor that runs beside a live one. The 106's volts input is over 10 MΩ, which is what you want on electronics, but it also means a disconnected wire picking up voltage capacitively from its neighbor can show a sizable AC reading with almost no current behind it. You then have to decide whether that number is real.

The 114's AutoVolt position puts a low resistance across the leads. A stiff source such as a real 120 V feed barely notices; a weak, coupled voltage collapses toward zero. As a hedged illustration, a coupled wire might read tens of volts on a high-impedance meter and close to nothing on LoZ, while a genuinely energized circuit reads about the same on both. That contrast is the diagnostic. Fluke doesn't publish the LoZ input resistance on the 114 page we checked; the same feature on the 113 and 117 is specified at roughly 3 kΩ.

The 106 workaround, and its limits

With a 106 you can still sort ghost voltage from a live feed by confirming with a second method, such as a known load or a plug-in tester, but that is an extra step and an extra tool. The 114 does it in one dial position, and it picks AC or DC on its own, which saves a mistake when you meet a DC control circuit in a panel. Our Fluke 113 vs Fluke 115 page works through the loading arithmetic for a low-impedance input in detail.

Where the 106's current and capacitance ranges win

Current is the job the 114 simply can't start. The 106 reads up to 10 A in series, AC or DC, at ±(1.5% + 3), with AC current specified from 40 to 200 Hz. That covers a 12 V accessory in a car, a bench supply feeding a project or a low-voltage lighting run, as long as the load stays within 10 A. It has no milliamp range, so best resolution on the 4 A range is 1 mA and small standby currents are out of reach; see Fluke 17B+ vs Fluke 106 if you need finer current.

Capacitance follows the same pattern. The 106 reads from its 50 nF range up to 1,000 µF, though Fluke notes readings can take up to 18 seconds to settle. That handles a furnace blower's run capacitor or a power-supply filter cap. The 114 lists no capacitance function at all, so an HVAC or appliance user who needs that test should not start with it; our Fluke 114 vs Fluke 116 page covers the 110-series model that adds it.

Buy the Fluke 106 if you're a homeowner, car owner or hobbyist who wants a tough, small meter that covers volts, amps, ohms and capacitance, and you mostly measure clean utility power or DC. Skip it if you work on building wiring for a living, because it has no LoZ, no True RMS and no backlight listed. Buy the Fluke 114 if your day is spent proving circuits live or dead in panels and boxes. Skip it if you'll ever need amps or capacitance, since the more complete 115 and 117 share its body; compare it with the Fluke 115 first.

Both stop at CAT III 600V

Neither meter is rated for the service entrance, meter base or overhead drop, which are CAT IV locations. Use leads in good condition, test your meter on a known live source before and after checking a circuit you plan to touch, and leave anything on the utility side to a licensed electrician. On GFCI-protected circuits, a low-impedance reading taken hot to ground can draw enough current to trip the device; Fluke warns about this for its LoZ meters, so measure hot to neutral there.

Where the published specs disagree

Fluke's 114 overview mentions "voltage and current", but its own spec table lists no current range and the meter has only two jacks, so we treat it as voltage and resistance only. The same page carries a temperature footnote without any temperature function. Amazon gives the 114's weight as 0.51 kg against Fluke's 550 g. On the 106, our product summary mentions data hold, yet Fluke's 106 page doesn't list hold or a backlight, so treat both as unconfirmed. Fluke's 106/107 documentation gives a two-year warranty that the 106 product page itself doesn't repeat. A 1 m drop rating is published for the 107; for the 106 and 114 it is not stated in the sources we cite.

Choose the Fluke 106 if…

  • ✓You need to read current, up to 10 A, as well as volts
  • ✓You want to check capacitors from 50 nF to 1,000 µF
  • ✓The meter has to live in a glovebox, kitchen drawer or small pouch

Choose the Fluke 114 if…

  • ✓You check building wiring and want a low-impedance input to rule out ghost voltage
  • ✓You need True RMS readings on dimmers, drives or electronic loads
  • ✓You log a fluctuating voltage with Min/Max/Average and want a fast bar graph

Neither if…

You need current/capacitance and AutoVolt/True RMS/bar graph together — neither meter covers both sets, and the Fluke 117 does.

The bottom line

Choose the Fluke 106 if you want a truly pocketable meter that reads current and capacitance and you mostly measure clean mains or DC. Choose the Fluke 114 if you test building wiring and value True RMS, AutoVolt LoZ, a bar graph and Min/Max/Average over amps. If you need both sets of features, look at the Fluke 117 instead of compromising.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 114 a compact multimeter?+

Not physically. It uses the same 167 × 84 × 46 mm, 550 g housing as the Fluke 115 and 117. It is compact only in its feature list: volts, resistance and continuity. The Fluke 106 is the small one here at 142 × 69 × 28 mm and 200 g.

Can the Fluke 106 detect ghost voltage?+

Not directly. It has a high-impedance volts input of over 10 MΩ and no low-impedance mode, so an induced voltage on an open wire can show up as a real-looking reading. You can confirm with a second method, such as a plug-in tester or a known load. The Fluke 114's AutoVolt position is built for exactly that check.

Does the Fluke 114 measure amps or capacitance?+

No. Fluke's spec table lists neither, and the meter has only two input jacks, COM and V/Ω. Fluke's overview text mentions current, which conflicts with its own table, but there is no current input on the meter. For amps on this body you need the Fluke 115 or 117; for capacitance and temperature, the 116.

Which is more accurate, the Fluke 106 or 114?+

On clean sine-wave AC and on DC they are close: both list AC volts at ±(1.0% + 3), and DC volts at ±(0.5% + 3) on the 106 versus ±(0.5% + 2) on the 114. The real difference is waveform. The 114 is True RMS, so it stays correct on distorted AC from dimmers and drives, where the average-responding 106 can read off.

Which battery lasts longer, the Fluke 106 or 114?+

Fluke rates the 114 at 400 hours on one 9 V battery without the backlight, and the 106 at a minimum of 200 hours on two AAA cells. The 106 also switches itself off after 20 minutes of inactivity, which helps if it lives in a toolbox and sometimes gets left on.

Do the Fluke 106 and 114 have the same continuity beeper?+

Not quite. Per Fluke's documents, the 106 beeps below 70 Ω, while the 114 beeps below 20 Ω and stays silent above 250 Ω. So a corroded joint measuring 50 Ω beeps on the 106 but not on the 114, which matters when you are confirming a clean connection rather than just checking that a fuse isn't blown.
