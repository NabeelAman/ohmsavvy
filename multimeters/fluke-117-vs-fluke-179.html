Fluke 117 vs Fluke 179

Both are True RMS Fluke meters with a strong reputation among electricians, but they're built for different tiers of work. The 117 is tuned for residential and light commercial wiring, with VoltAlert non-contact voltage detection and a low-impedance AutoVolt input as its standout features. The 179 is the full-featured member of Fluke's 170 series: CAT IV 600V, temperature, frequency to 100 kHz, tighter DC accuracy and a lifetime warranty, aimed at industrial maintenance, HVAC and electronics as much as building wiring.

Fluke 117 vs 179 spec by spec

On capacitance and AC voltage the two are level: Fluke quotes ±(1.0% + 3) AC volts for both, and both handle motor-run and power-supply capacitors. The 179 pulls ahead on DC accuracy and frequency reach; the 117 answers with twice the quoted battery life.

What does the DC gap look like in volts? On a 24 V control supply, read on the 60.00 V range, the 117's spec allows ±(0.12 + 0.02) = ±0.14 V and the 179's allows ±(0.022 + 0.02) = ±0.042 V. That's arithmetic from Fluke's published figures, not a measurement. For checking that a 24 V supply is alive, either is plenty; for setting a sensor reference or comparing two readings a few tens of millivolts apart, the 179 is the better instrument.

CAT III 600V vs CAT IV 600V: where it matters

A measurement category describes how big a transient the meter is built to survive at a given point in the installation. Closer to the utility transformer, a fault or lightning-induced surge carries far more energy, so the IEC 61010 test levels rise. CAT IV covers the origin of the installation: the service drop, meter base and main disconnect. CAT III covers distribution panels, feeders and fixed equipment.

For a residential electrician, almost all troubleshooting happens in the CAT III and CAT II zones, where the 117 is fully rated. The 179 earns its rating when you test at the meter base, on the line side of a main breaker, or on outdoor service conductors. Our multimeter buying guide explains the categories in more depth.

A rating is not permission

Line-side and service-entrance conductors can't be switched off by the main breaker. Even with a CAT IV meter, that work calls for arc-rated PPE and training. If you're a homeowner, leave the meter base and service conductors to a licensed electrician or your utility.

What the 117 does that the 179 can't

The 117's two electrician features are about telling real voltage from false alarms. VoltAlert, a sensor in the top of the meter, beeps and lights near an energized AC conductor with no probe contact, a quick first check before you touch anything. AutoVolt drops the input to about 3 kΩ, which drains the phantom voltage a disconnected wire picks up from live neighbors in the same conduit. A high-impedance meter like the 179 will happily display that phantom as a convincing reading. Our explainer on voltage on a disconnected circuit shows how to unmask it without LoZ.

Fluke 117 vs 179 for HVAC technicians

An HVAC service call asks a meter for four things: capacitance for run and start capacitors, temperature, a clean voltage reading on long control runs, and microamps for the furnace flame sensor. The 117 and 179 split those four between them, and neither covers all of them, which is the most useful thing to know before buying either for HVAC work.

Capacitors first, since they are the most common failure on a no-cool call. Take a 45 µF run capacitor as an illustrative example. The percentage term alone allows about ±0.86 µF on the 117 and ±0.54 µF on the 179, before the two-count term. Run capacitors are commonly printed with a ±5% or ±6% tolerance, roughly ±2.3 to ±2.7 µF on a 45 µF can, so both meters give a clear pass or fail. Capacitance is not a reason to choose one over the other.

Flame sensors are the trap. Flame-rectification current is typically a few microamps, and a useful reading needs a µA range with 0.1 µA resolution. The 117's finest step is 1 mA and the 179's is 0.01 mA, which is 10 µA, so on a sensor running at, say, 3 µA both meters would show zero. If you service furnaces, plan on a meter with a microamp range, such as the Fluke 116 or the Fluke 87V. Our best multimeter for HVAC technicians ranking is built around exactly that requirement.

On safety ratings, both cover the work. Rooftop units, condensers and air handlers are fixed equipment fed from the building's distribution, which is CAT III territory, and both meters are rated there at 600 V or above, enough for 208, 240 and 480 V equipment. The 179's extra CAT IV rating matters to an HVAC tech only if they also probe the service entrance. Where the 117 earns its place is low-voltage controls: a 24 V thermostat cable run beside line-voltage wiring can show a phantom reading on a high-impedance meter like the 179, and the 117's LoZ position collapses it.

Temperature measurement compared

This comparison is one-sided: the Fluke 117 has no temperature function at all. Fluke's 110 Series datasheet gives the thermocouple input to the Fluke 116 only, so if you need temperature in that body, the 116 is the model to buy. The 179 measures temperature with a thermocouple supplied in the box.

For HVAC, −40 °C to 400 °C on the 179 comfortably spans supply and return air, refrigerant line temperatures for superheat and subcooling, and heat-exchanger surfaces. Two caveats. First, the range above is the meter's; a thermocouple probe carries its own rating, and we haven't confirmed the rating of the probe Fluke ships with the 179, so check the probe before measuring anything hot. Second, with one input you take readings in sequence: for a temperature split across a coil (commonly targeted around 15 to 20 °F on cooling), read return air, move the probe, then read supply air. The bottom line

For most residential and light commercial electricians, the 117 is the more practical choice: its CAT III 600V rating covers standard panel work, VoltAlert and LoZ solve everyday false readings, and it runs twice as long on a battery. The 179 earns its higher price for work that needs CAT IV protection, temperature, tighter DC accuracy or a lifetime warranty: industrial maintenance, HVAC and electronics more than routine residential troubleshooting.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 179 simply a better version of the 117?+

Not exactly. It trades VoltAlert and LoZ for a higher CAT rating, temperature, tighter DC accuracy and a wider frequency range. Both are True RMS with the same AC volts accuracy, and each has features the other lacks, so the choice depends on the work you do rather than which meter is newer or dearer.

Do residential electricians actually need CAT IV 600V?+

Usually not. CAT III 600V, the 117's rating, covers distribution panels, branch circuits and fixed equipment, which is most residential and light commercial work. CAT IV matters at the origin of the installation: the meter base, service drop and line side of the main disconnect.

Why doesn't the 179 have non-contact voltage detection?+

Fluke doesn't give a reason. It lists VoltAlert as a 117 feature and leaves it off the 170 series, which it positions as general troubleshooting meters. If you choose the 179, carry a separate non-contact voltage tester for quick checks, and use the meter's probes to confirm before touching anything.

Can the 179 do everything the 117 can, plus more?+

Almost, but not entirely. It lacks VoltAlert non-contact detection and the low-impedance AutoVolt input, so it can't drain a ghost voltage the way the 117 does. Beyond those, the 179 extends further in DC accuracy, current resolution, frequency and temperature, and adds CAT IV 600V.

Which Fluke has the better warranty, the 117 or the 179?+

The 179. Fluke lists a lifetime warranty for it against three years for the 117. Over a long career that's a real difference, though both are built for daily field use. Choosing the 117 for VoltAlert and LoZ means accepting the shorter term; choosing the 179 for CAT IV and temperature brings the longer one with it.

Is the Fluke 117 or 179 better for HVAC work?+

For most HVAC techs, the 179, because it measures temperature and reads capacitance slightly more accurately, ±(1.2% + 2) against ±(1.9% + 2). The 117 wins only on control wiring, where its LoZ position clears ghost voltage on long thermostat runs. Neither has a microamp range, so furnace flame-sensor checks need a third meter such as the Fluke 116 or 87V.

Can the Fluke 179 test a furnace flame sensor?+

Not usefully. Its finest current step is 0.01 mA, which is 10 µA, and Fluke lists no microamp range. Flame-sensor current is typically a few microamps, so the 179 would read zero or a single count. Use a meter with a µA range and 0.1 µA resolution, such as the Fluke 116 or Fluke 87V, in series with the flame rod lead.

Does the Fluke 117 measure temperature?+

No. Fluke's 110 Series datasheet gives the thermocouple input to the Fluke 116, not the 117. The 179 reads −40 °C to 400 °C with a thermocouple in the box. If you want a 110 Series body with LoZ plus temperature and microamps, the 116 is the HVAC model in that family.

What temperature probe comes with the Fluke 179?+

Fluke says the 179 ships with a thermocouple, and the meter reads −40 °C to 400 °C. We haven't confirmed the supplied probe's type or its own temperature rating from Fluke's specifications, so treat that as not stated. A probe can be rated lower than the meter, so check the marking on the probe before measuring hot surfaces or flue gas.
