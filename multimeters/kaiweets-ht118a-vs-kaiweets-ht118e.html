KAIWEETS HT118A vs HT118E: Do 20,000 Counts Beat 6,000?

KAIWEETS HT118A vs HT118E looks like an old model against its upgrade, and on DC it is: the HT118E reads 20,000 counts, ±(0.08% + 5) on DC volts and a 200 µA range. Read the rest of KAIWEETS's tables and it isn't that simple. The 6,000-count HT118A has the tighter AC volts spec, capacitance to 100 mF instead of 2 mF, and a True RMS claim on its Amazon listing that KAIWEETS never makes for the HT118E. Both are CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V with NCV, lit jacks and a 3-year warranty.

KAIWEETS HT118A vs HT118E: where the extra counts pay off

A meter's accuracy spec has two parts: a percentage of the reading, and a number of digits added on top. More counts shrink the size of each digit, but if the maker also raises the digit count, the gain can vanish. That is exactly what happens between these two meters. On DC the HT118E wins clearly. On AC it loses.

The DC math: on its 20.000 V range the HT118E adds 0.08% of 12.600 V, which is 0.0101 V, plus 5 digits of 1 mV, so ±0.015 V. The HT118A on its 60.00 V range adds 0.5%, which is 0.063 V, plus 3 digits of 10 mV, so ±0.093 V. The AC math runs the other way. At 120 V the HT118E uses its 200.00 V range: 1.0% is 1.2 V, plus 25 digits of 10 mV is 0.25 V, so ±1.45 V. The HT118A on its 600.0 V range: 0.8% is 0.96 V, plus 3 digits of 0.1 V is 0.3 V, so ±1.26 V.

The microamp math, for an illustrative 150 µA load: the HT118E reads it on its 200.00 µA range at ±(0.5% + 5), so 0.75 µA plus 0.05 µA, ±0.80 µA. The HT118A reads it on its 600.0 µA range at ±(1.0% + 3), so 1.5 µA plus 0.3 µA, ±1.8 µA. Above 200 µA the E switches to its 2000.0 µA range and the gap narrows. KAIWEETS publishes no burden voltage for either meter; our guide to measuring microamps explains why that matters on a 3.3 V board.

The True RMS question

KAIWEETS's own product pages don't use the words True RMS for either meter. The HT118A's Amazon title says “TRMS”, and our Pick's photo shows “TRUE RMS” printed on the meter. Neither the HT118E's listing nor its product page makes that claim.

It matters on non-sine AC. Variable-speed fans, LED drivers, dimmers and inverter outputs produce distorted waveforms, and an average-responding meter can misread them. On a clean 120 V outlet, either meter will do. For HVAC blower motors on variable drives or a cheap inverter in an RV, buy the meter that claims True RMS, which here is the HT118A. Our do I need a True RMS multimeter guide explains when the difference shows up.

Read both terms of the accuracy spec

±(1.0% + 25) on a 20,000-count meter can be looser than ±(0.8% + 3) on a 6,000-count meter, because 25 small digits add up to more than 3 large ones. Work the number for the signal you measure before paying for counts.

For car and battery work, the HT118E is the better meter of the two. For house, HVAC and appliance work, the HT118A is. If you split your time, the HT118A's True RMS claim and 100 mF range cover more situations where the other meter can't give an answer at all, while the HT118E's advantages are about a tighter answer on readings both meters can take.

The trust question: KAIWEETS's pages disagree with themselves

The HT118A's product page says it measures “up to 600V AC/DC” in one paragraph and lists 1000 V DC and 750 V AC in the spec table, which also matches the Amazon listing. It gives the display as 2.9 inches in one place and 2.7 inches in another, and its package list says 2 × AAA while the manual cited in our Pick says 2 × AA. The HT118E's page is cleaner but has a typo in its capacitance ranges, and neither page publishes AC bandwidth, burden voltage or battery life.

None of that makes either meter a bad buy at its price. It does mean you should treat the spec table as the best guide, check the voltage printed by the input jacks, and not assume a feature exists because a related model has it.

1000 V ratings on budget meters

Both meters claim CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V. Inspect the leads and case before high-energy work, keep fingers behind the lead guards, and test on a known live source before you trust a zero reading. For work inside a service panel, hire a licensed electrician. See CAT III vs CAT IV for what those ratings cover.

The bottom line

The KAIWEETS HT118E is the better DC meter: 20,000 counts, ±(0.08% + 5) on DC volts and a 200 µA range make it the pick for batteries, cars and sleep current. The KAIWEETS HT118A is the better AC and all-round meter: a tighter ±(0.8% + 3) AC spec, a True RMS claim on its listing, capacitance to 100 mF and temperature down to -40 °C.

Frequently asked questions

Is the KAIWEETS HT118E an upgrade over the HT118A?+

On DC, yes: 20,000 counts against 6,000, ±(0.08% + 5) DC volts against ±(0.5% + 3), and a finer 200 µA range. On AC and capacitance, no. The HT118A has a tighter ±(0.8% + 3) AC volts spec, reads capacitors to 100 mF instead of 2 mF, and its Amazon listing claims True RMS, which KAIWEETS doesn't claim for the HT118E.

Is the KAIWEETS HT118E True RMS?+

KAIWEETS doesn't say so. Its HT118E product page and Amazon listing describe 20,000 counts, the ranges and the safety rating, but neither uses the words True RMS. The HT118A's Amazon title does say TRMS. If you measure distorted AC, such as variable-speed motors or inverter output, treat the HT118E as average-responding until KAIWEETS states otherwise.

Which KAIWEETS meter is better for a car battery?+

The HT118E. On a 12.6 V battery its 20.000 V range shows 1 mV steps and KAIWEETS's ±(0.08% + 5) spec gives about ±0.015 V. The HT118A shows 10 mV steps with about ±0.093 V. Both are good enough to tell a charged battery from a flat one, but the HT118E shows small voltage drops across cables and grounds more clearly.

Do both KAIWEETS meters have the same safety rating?+

Yes. KAIWEETS rates both CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, with double ceramic fuses and thermistor protection on all ranges. One caution on the HT118A: its product page text also says 600V AC/DC, which conflicts with the 1000 V DC and 750 V AC in its own spec table and on Amazon. We use the spec table.

Which KAIWEETS meter reads larger capacitors?+

The HT118A. It reads capacitance up to 100 mF, at ±(5.0% + 3) on that top range and ±(4.0% + 3) below it. The HT118E stops at 2 mF with ±(4.0% + 50). That covers most filter capacitors in power supplies and amplifiers on either meter, but a 1 farad car-audio capacitor is beyond both, so use a dedicated tester for one.
