Fluke 289 vs Fluke 287: Which Logging Multimeter Is Better?

The Fluke 289 and Fluke 287 are the same logging multimeter with three differences, all in the 289's favor: a 50 Ω low-ohm range that resolves 0.001 Ω, a LoZ input for ghost voltage, and an AC low-pass filter for inverters and drives. Everything else matches in Fluke's 287/289 manual, from the 50,000-count display and 0.025% DC accuracy to the 0.01 µA microamp range, TrendCapture logging memory and CAT IV 600V rating. For electronics repair, the 289 is better only if you measure resistances below about an ohm; otherwise the 287 does the same job for less.

The second chart is where the 289 earns its place. On the 287's 500 Ω range, the floor of 10 counts at 0.01 Ω is ±0.10 Ω before the percentage term, so a 0.100 Ω part could read anywhere from 0.00 to 0.20 Ω. On the 289's 50 Ω range, 20 counts at 0.001 Ω is ±0.020 Ω, plus 0.15% of 0.1 Ω (0.00015 Ω): a reading between roughly 0.08 and 0.12 Ω. That's the gap between "near zero" and an actual number.

How the 289's low-ohm range works

Per the manual, the 50 Ω function has a single range, sources up to 10 mA at an open-circuit voltage up to 20 V, and allows only relative and relative-percent modes. More current through a small resistance gives a bigger voltage to measure, which is how it reaches 0.001 Ω steps. It's still a two-terminal measurement, not a four-wire Kelvin setup, so the leads and probe contacts sit in series with the part.

Fluke 287 vs 289 for electronics repair

For board-level repair the two meters are closer than the price gap suggests. The functions a repair tech leans on every day are identical in Fluke's tables: the 500 µA range at 0.01 µA for sleep and standby current, capacitance from 1 nF to 100 mF, frequency to 999.99 kHz, duty cycle and pulse width for PWM lines, a 3.1 V diode test, 50 nS conductance for leakage, temperature for a hot regulator, and the same logging memory to catch a fault that only appears after an hour.

Microamp burden is the same on both, so here's what it means in practice. A device sleeping at 300 µA drops 300 × 102 µV ≈ 31 mV across the meter. On a 3.3 V rail that's under 1%, usually harmless; on a coin-cell circuit near its brown-out threshold, it can matter, and neither meter has an advantage there. Our guide to measuring standby current covers ways to work around burden.

The 50 Ω range is the one 289 feature that changes repair outcomes. Hunting a shorted capacitor on a power rail means comparing tiny resistances from point to point: the closer you get to the short, the lower the number. With 0.001 Ω steps, a few milliohms of copper between test points show up as a gradient you can follow. With 0.01 Ω steps and a 10-count error floor, the 287 tends to read near zero across the whole rail. Our guide to checking a PCB for a short circuit walks through the method.

LoZ is not a bench feature

The 289's LoZ position puts about 3 kΩ across the leads, and Fluke cautions against using it on circuits that low impedance could damage. On a logic board it can load a signal and mislead you. For electronics, LoZ is a reason to pick the 289 only if the same meter also goes to mains wiring. On the bench, the 287 gives up nothing by lacking it.

The low-pass filter and LoZ outside the lab

The 289's other two extras point at field work. The low-pass filter blocks AC content above 1 kHz, which Fluke says improves readings on the composite waveforms from inverters and variable-frequency drives; with it on, the meter switches to manual ranging. LoZ reads AC volts on a fixed 1000 V range through a 3.2 kΩ input, which collapses the phantom voltage a disconnected conductor picks up from live neighbors. Both are aimed at solar, motor-control and building work, not bench electronics.

Logging on the 289 and 287: the same recorder

If logging is the reason you are shopping in the 280 Series, the choice between these two doesn't affect it. The shared manual gives both meters the same recording memory (up to 10,000 sample intervals, with events filling the rest of a 15,000-record budget), the same on-screen trend graph, the same real-time clock and the same infrared link to FlukeView Forms on a PC. Battery life is listed as 100 hours minimum and 200 hours in logging mode on both. None of the three 289 extras changes what or how long either meter records.

Safety: discharge before ohms, and test live first

Both meters are rated CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, but resistance and low-ohm modes are for de-energized circuits only: discharge capacitors before measuring, because the 289's 50 Ω range sources current and a charged capacitor can damage the meter or the board. On mains equipment, Fluke warns not to use the low-pass filter to check whether voltage is present, since it can hide some of it; measure unfiltered first. Never use LoZ on circuits that a 3 kΩ load could damage.

Choose the Fluke 289 if…

  • ✓You check shunts, PCB traces, connector contacts or motor windings well below an ohm
  • ✓You also work on building wiring and want LoZ to expose ghost voltage
  • ✓You measure inverter or drive outputs and want the 1 kHz low-pass filter

Choose the Fluke 287 if…

  • ✓Your work is bench electronics: sleep current, rails, sensors and logging a drifting supply
  • ✓0.01 Ω resolution on the 500 Ω range is fine for the resistances you meet
  • ✓You want the 280 Series logging and accuracy without paying for industrial extras

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 289 if you measure resistances below an ohm, such as shunts, rails, busbars and windings, or if the meter also goes to solar, drive or building work where LoZ and the low-pass filter help. Buy the Fluke 287 for bench electronics and logging: it shares the 289's 50,000-count accuracy, 0.01 µA microamp range, capacitance, frequency and recording memory, and gives up only features that matter in the field.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Fluke 287 and 289?+

Three functions, all on the 289: a 50 Ω low-ohm range with 0.001 Ω resolution, a LoZ AC volts position for ghost voltage, and an AC low-pass filter that blocks content above 1 kHz. Fluke's shared manual gives both meters the same 50,000-count display, accuracy, microamp range, capacitance, frequency, logging memory, battery, weight and CAT IV 600V rating.

Is the Fluke 287 good enough for electronics repair?+

Yes, for most of it. It resolves 0.01 µA on the 500 µA range, measures capacitance from 1 nF to 100 mF, reads frequency to 999.99 kHz and logs readings over time. Its weak spot is sub-ohm work: the 500 Ω range's 10-count error floor is ±0.10 Ω, too coarse for tracing a shorted rail or checking a current-sense shunt.

Does the Fluke 287 have LoZ?+

No. Fluke's 287/289 manual marks LoZ as a model 289 feature. On the 289 it reads AC volts through a 3.2 kΩ input on a fixed 1000 V range, which drains capacitively coupled ghost voltage. On a 287 you can still unmask a phantom reading by adding a suitable load across the leads, but there is no dial position for it.

Is the Fluke 289's 50 Ω range a four-wire measurement?+

No, it's two-terminal. The meter sources up to 10 mA through the same leads it measures with, so lead and contact resistance add to the reading. Short the probes and press REL to subtract them, then keep contact pressure consistent. For true four-wire Kelvin measurements you need a dedicated milliohm meter.

Do the Fluke 287 and 289 log the same amount of data?+

Yes. The shared manual gives both up to 10,000 recorded sample intervals, with events filling the rest of a 15,000-record budget. Both view logged data as a graph on the meter, have a real-time clock, and transfer to a PC through the infrared link with FlukeView Forms. Battery life is listed as 100 hours minimum, 200 hours in logging mode.

How long can the Fluke 289 or 287 log on one set of batteries?+

Fluke's manual lists 200 hours in logging mode on six AA cells for both meters, a little over eight days. The memory of 10,000 intervals usually runs out first: at one sample a minute it lasts about 167 hours, and at one a second under three hours. Pick the interval so the memory and the battery figure both cover the run you need.

Which has the better microamp range, the 287 or 289?+

Neither: they're identical. Both have a 500 µA range resolving 0.01 µA with DC accuracy of ±(0.075% + 20) and a burden of 102 µV per µA, plus a 5,000 µA range at 0.1 µA. A 300 µA sleep current drops about 31 mV across either meter, which is usually harmless on a 3.3 V rail.
