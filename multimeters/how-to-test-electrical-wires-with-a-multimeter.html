How to Test Electrical Wires With a Multimeter

Most wire problems come down to one of two faults: an open (a break somewhere along the run) or a short (an unintended connection to another wire or ground). A multimeter finds both, and the same continuity-mode technique works whether you're tracing a wire in household wiring or troubleshooting a digital multimeter on a car.

Any meter with a continuity or resistance mode can do this — see our full multimeter roundup.

De-energize the circuit before you test

Continuity and resistance mode send the meter's own small test current through the probes, and that only gives a meaningful reading — or a safe one — on a circuit with no external power applied. Switch off the breaker or disconnect the battery before testing a wire this way, and confirm with a voltage check first if there's any doubt. Testing building wiring also means your meter needs the right CAT rating for the circuit you're working on — see our buying guide if you're not sure what that means for your meter.

1Finding an open wire with continuity mode

With the circuit de-energized, set the dial to continuity mode and touch one probe to each end of the wire you're testing. A beep (and a near-zero reading) means the wire is intact end to end. No beep means there's a break somewhere along its length — a corroded connector, a pinched or cut section, or a failed splice. If you can access the wire at a midpoint, testing from each end to that midpoint narrows down which half of the run actually has the break.

2Finding a short to another wire or to ground

A short shows up the opposite way: continuity (a beep) where you don't expect any. With the circuit still de-energized and the suspect wire disconnected at both ends, touch one probe to the wire and the other to the chassis, ground, or the neighboring wire you suspect it's shorting to. A beep here means they're connected when they shouldn't be — the fault is somewhere in the insulation between those two points.

3How resistance measurement actually makes this work

Continuity mode is really just resistance mode with a beeper threshold built in, and the underlying multimeter resistance measurement principle is the same either way: the meter applies a small, known current from its own internal battery through the probes and measures the resulting voltage drop, then calculates resistance using Ohm's law (R = V ÷ I). That's why it only works on a de-energized circuit — an external voltage source would overwhelm the meter's own tiny test current and produce a meaningless or dangerous reading. A healthy wire has a resistance low enough that the meter reads it as near-zero; a break shows up as near-infinite resistance (no current can complete the loop at all).

How low is "near zero"? Copper wire resistance is well documented. Using standard AWG table values at 20 °C, 100 ft of 14 AWG branch-circuit wire is about 0.25 Ω, and even 100 ft of thin 22 AWG hookup wire is only about 1.6 Ω. Most meters' own test leads add a few tenths of an ohm, so on a household or automotive run a sound wire reads within a fraction of an ohm of the shorted-leads value. Touch the probe tips together first and subtract that figure.

Use the chart as a sanity check. Scale the value by the length you're testing: a 25 ft run of 12 AWG should be about 0.16 × 0.25 = 0.04 Ω, which no handheld meter can tell apart from zero. So if that run reads 3 Ω or 40 Ω, the wire is not the copper you think it is: a loose terminal, a corroded splice or a few surviving strands is adding resistance. That reading can still beep in continuity mode, because many meters beep below a threshold of some tens of ohms, which is why the number on the display matters more than the beep on a suspect run.

4Testing a digital multimeter on a car

Automotive electrical systems are 12V DC nominal, which is far less dangerous than household mains, but the same continuity principle applies, with a few things specific to cars:

  • Testing a fuse — pull it and run a continuity check across the two legs. A beep means it's good; no beep means it's blown. Never test a fuse for continuity while it's still in the circuit and the circuit is live.
  • Checking the charging system — with the engine running, DC voltage at the battery terminals should read noticeably higher than the battery's resting voltage (typically in the 13.5–14.5V range for a healthy alternator); a reading at or below resting battery voltage with the engine running points to a charging problem.
  • Tracing a wire in a harness — the same continuity technique from Step 1 works, but disconnect the wire from both the battery and any module it connects to first, since most of a car's wiring uses the chassis itself as the ground return path, which can create unexpected continuity paths if you don't isolate the wire you're actually testing.

Automotive-specific cautions

Keep the multimeter leads clear of moving parts — belts, fans, pulleys — whenever the engine could start or is running. Disconnect the battery's negative terminal before working on wiring you'll be touching with bare hands or metal tools, since a dropped wrench across the battery terminals or chassis can cause a direct short with enough current to cause burns or start a fire, even at only 12V. If a wiring problem is inside an airbag, ABS, or other safety-critical module's harness, that's worth leaving to a qualified technician rather than troubleshooting it yourself.
