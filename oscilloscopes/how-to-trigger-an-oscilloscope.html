How to Trigger an Oscilloscope

Learning how to trigger an oscilloscope is what turns a smeared, drifting trace into a waveform that sits still long enough to measure. The trigger tells the scope exactly where in the signal each capture should line up. This guide covers edge triggering, level and slope, the Auto, Normal and Single modes, holdoff, and a quick fix list for the most common "my waveform won't stop moving" problems.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 20, 2026

Safety before you clip on

Triggering is a front-panel task, but you can only practice it on a live signal, so set up safely first. On a bench scope the probe's ground clip is normally tied to mains earth through the power cord. Clipping it to a point that isn't at earth potential, such as anywhere on a mains-connected, non-isolated circuit, creates a short through the ground lead that can cause damage, fire or injury. Never remove or defeat the scope's earth to work around this; use an isolation transformer on the device under test, a differential probe, or an isolated-channel or handheld scope rated for the job. Stay within the CAT rating and maximum input printed on your probe and scope. Standard 1x/10x probes are not for mains work unless their rating explicitly covers it. For practice, the scope's own probe-compensation output or a function generator is ideal.

Why an untriggered waveform rolls

A scope doesn't draw a signal once; it captures it over and over and redraws the screen each time. If each capture starts at an arbitrary point in the signal, every redraw shows the waveform shifted by a different amount. Your eye sees that as a trace drifting sideways, a blur of overlapping copies, or a picture that never settles. The trigger fixes this by waiting for one specific, repeatable event, for example "the signal rising through 1 V", and lining up every capture on that event. Because the event happens at the same place in every cycle of a repeating signal, every redraw lands on top of the last one and the waveform appears to stand still.

The one-sentence version

An edge trigger says: "line up each capture at the moment this channel crosses this voltage, going in this direction." Source, level and slope are the three answers you have to give it.

The trigger controls, in plain terms

Names and menu layouts vary between brands, but almost every digital scope gives you the same core set. On most bench scopes there's a dedicated Trigger section on the front panel with a level knob and a menu button.

On the panel above, the level knob is the one you will touch most; source, type, slope, mode and holdoff usually live in the menu that the trigger section's button opens. Here is what each of those settings does.

Source

Which channel (or external input) the trigger watches. Usually the channel showing your signal.

Type

What kind of event to look for. Edge is the default and covers most everyday work.

Level

The voltage the signal must cross. Shown as a marker or dashed line on screen.

Slope

Rising, falling, or on some scopes either edge. Picks which crossing direction counts.

Mode

Auto, Normal or Single: what the scope does while it waits for a trigger.

Holdoff

A minimum wait after each trigger before the scope will accept the next one.

1Pick the trigger source

Set the source to the channel carrying the signal you want to hold steady, usually CH1 when you're starting out. A very common beginner mistake is probing on CH2 while the trigger is still watching an empty CH1: the scope never sees a crossing, so nothing locks. If you're viewing two related signals, trigger on the cleaner or more regular of the two and the other will be shown in the correct time relationship to it.

2Use an edge trigger and choose the slope

Leave the trigger type on Edge. Then choose the slope: rising means the event happens when the signal crosses the level going upward, falling means going downward. For a simple repeating wave either will give a stable picture; they just line up the capture on opposite halves of the cycle. When you care about a specific transition, such as the moment a logic line goes high, pick the slope that matches it so that edge sits at the trigger point.

3Set the trigger level inside the signal

The level must sit somewhere the signal actually crosses. A good starting point is roughly halfway between the waveform's lowest and highest points, where the edges are steepest and least affected by noise. Many scopes have a button or knob-press that sets the level to the middle of the signal automatically (often labeled 50% or similar; check your manual). If the level is above the top of the signal or below the bottom, there is no crossing to find and the trigger cannot lock.

4Choose Auto, Normal or Single mode

The mode decides what the scope does while no trigger event is happening. Most scopes default to Auto, which is the right choice while you're still finding the signal. Switch to Normal or Single once you know what you're looking for.

ModeWhat it doesUse it when
AutoTriggers on the event if it happens; if nothing triggers for a short time, it captures anyway so you always see a trace.Finding a signal, checking DC levels, or when you are not sure what is there yet.
NormalOnly updates the screen when a valid trigger occurs. Between events the last capture stays on screen.Infrequent or irregular signals, where Auto would keep drawing untriggered junk.
SingleArms, waits for one trigger, captures it, then stops.One-off events: power-up behavior, a switch or button bouncing, an occasional glitch.

Two things catch beginners out here. In Auto mode a trace that rolls or jitters usually means the scope is not actually triggering and is free-running instead. In Normal mode a screen that stops updating usually means the level or source is wrong, not that the signal disappeared. Many scopes show a status indicator (for example Trig'd, Auto or Wait) that tells you which situation you are in.

5Use holdoff for complex repeating signals

Some signals cross the trigger level several times in one repeating pattern, such as a burst of pulses followed by a gap, or a data stream. An edge trigger may then lock on a different crossing each time, giving a picture that jumps between two or three alignments or looks like several waveforms overlaid. Holdoff tells the scope to ignore any trigger events for a set time after each one it accepts. Increase it gradually until it is a little longer than the burst itself but shorter than the full repeat period, and the scope will lock on the first edge of every burst. For simple sine and square waves you can leave holdoff at its minimum.

Fixing a rolling or unstable waveform

If you are not sure where to start, check the four core settings in the order below. Each one depends on the one before it: a perfect level is no use if the trigger is watching the wrong channel, and no mode can help if the level never meets the signal.

If you would rather start from what you see on screen, work down this list in order; the first two fixes solve most cases.

SymptomLikely causeFix
Trace drifts sideways in AutoLevel is outside the signal, so the scope free-runsMove the level to mid-signal, or use the set-to-50% function if your scope has one
Nothing locks at allTrigger source is a different channel from the one you are probingSet the source to the active channel
Screen frozen in NormalNo valid trigger events are happeningCheck level and source, or switch to Auto to find the signal first

Stable but not the edge you wantWrong slope selectedSwitch between rising and falling

Beyond edge triggering

Edge is enough for most hobby and repair work, but many digital scopes add more specific trigger types, such as triggering on a pulse that is wider or narrower than a set time, on a video signal's sync, or on a pattern across several channels. Some models can also trigger on serial bus data. Which types are available varies a lot by model and sometimes by installed options, so check your scope's datasheet rather than assuming. How triggering fits into a measurement

A stable trigger is the step that makes everything else possible. Once the waveform holds still, you can read the screen division by division, measure voltage with cursors, and measure frequency from the period. If you're brand new to scopes, start with what an oscilloscope is and the first-session walkthrough in how to use an oscilloscope. And if the signal you're chasing is a steady DC level rather than something that changes over time, you may not need a trigger at all: see oscilloscope vs multimeter.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my oscilloscope show Auto instead of Trig'd?

On scopes with a status indicator, a reading of Auto in Auto mode generally means no valid trigger event has been found, so the scope is capturing on its own timer and the trace is not locked. Check that the source is the channel you are probing and that the level sits between the top and bottom of the signal. Once it locks, the status should change to Trig'd or similar.

How do you capture a one-time event on an oscilloscope?

Use Single mode. Set the source to the channel carrying the event, choose the slope that matches it (rising for a line going high, falling for one going low) and put the level partway through the expected change. Pick a time/div slow enough to hold the whole event, then press Single. The scope arms, waits, captures one trigger and stops, leaving the result on screen to measure.

Should I trigger on the rising or falling edge?

For a simple repeating wave either gives a stable picture; the choice only moves which half of the cycle sits at the trigger point. It matters when you care about one transition, such as a reset line releasing or a logic signal going low. Pick the slope that matches that transition so the edge you want to study lands at the trigger point every time.

Can you trigger on one channel and view another?

Yes. The trigger source does not have to be the channel you are studying. A common approach is to trigger on a clean, regular signal, such as a clock or a control line, and view a noisier related signal on another channel. Both traces are then shown in their correct time relationship, which is how you check delays or the order in which signals change.

Why does my waveform jitter even when it is triggered?

Small left-right jitter usually comes from noise near the trigger level, which makes the exact crossing moment vary slightly from capture to capture. Move the level to the steepest part of the edge, where noise shifts the crossing time least. Many scopes also offer noise-reject or HF-reject trigger coupling. If the jitter stays, the signal itself may genuinely vary in timing.
