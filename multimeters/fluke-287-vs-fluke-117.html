Fluke 287 vs Fluke 117: Advanced Meter or Electrician's Meter?

Buy the Fluke 117 if you work on building wiring; buy the Fluke 287 if you work on circuits, sensors and anything you need to record over time. The 117 is built for electricians: VoltAlert non-contact voltage detection, an AutoVolt low-impedance input that exposes ghost voltage, a 6,000-count display and a compact 550 g body that runs about 400 hours on a 9V battery. The 287 is an advanced bench and logging meter with 50,000 counts, ±(0.025% + 2) DC accuracy, a microamp range, temperature and 10,000-interval recording, plus a higher CAT IV 600V rating. Neither replaces the other: each lacks something the other was designed around.

Key differences

  • →Purpose: the 287 is a 280 Series logging meter aimed at electronics and diagnostics; the 117 is a 110 Series meter Fluke built around electricians' everyday checks.
  • →Field features: only the 117 has VoltAlert non-contact detection and an AutoVolt LoZ input (about 3 kΩ). Fluke's manual reserves LoZ for the 289, so the 287 has neither.
  • →Precision: the 287 shows 50,000 counts with ±(0.025% + 2) DC accuracy; the 117 shows 6,000 counts at ±(0.5% + 2), twenty times wider on the percentage term.
  • →Small currents: the 287 has a 500 µA range resolving 0.01 µA. The 117 has no mA or µA range and steps in 1 mA.
  • →Extra functions on the 287: temperature, frequency to 999.99 kHz, duty cycle, capacitance to 100 mF, 250 µs peak capture and 10,000-interval logging.
  • →Rating and warranty: the 287 is CAT III 1000V / CAT IV 600V with a lifetime limited warranty; the 117 is CAT III 600V with 3 years. The 117 is lighter (550 g vs 871 g).

Our testing

117 owner-tested; 287 compared on Fluke's specs.

The Fluke 117 carries our Hands-on Tested badge: the owner has bought and hands-on tested one. We haven't tested the 287, so its side of this page comes from Fluke's manual and product data. The 287 is the more expensive meter, and most of that difference buys bench precision and recording that an electrician on a service call rarely needs. Equally, the 117 leaves out the functions an electronics bench depends on. Our Fluke 117 review covers the electrician's meter in depth.

What the 117 does on a job site that the 287 can't

An electrician's most common question is "is this wire live?", and the 117 answers it in two ways the 287 doesn't. VoltAlert lights and beeps when the top of the meter is held near an energized AC conductor, a quick screen before you open a box. AutoVolt picks AC or DC automatically and measures through a low-impedance input of about 3 kΩ, which matters because of how switched-off wiring behaves.

A conductor that is switched off but runs alongside live ones picks up voltage through stray capacitance. The 287's 10 MΩ input draws almost no current from that weak source, so it can display a convincing voltage that disappears the moment any real load is connected. The 117's LoZ position loads the wire enough to drain the coupled charge, so a ghost reading collapses while a real supply holds up. For an electrician in a remodel full of shared conduits, that one function prevents a lot of wasted time. Our explainer on voltage on a disconnected circuit shows how to check without LoZ.

A higher spec isn't a better electrician's meter

The 287 beats the 117 on almost every number, but it has no NCV and no LoZ, weighs 871 g and runs on six AA cells. Those are the things an electrician notices every day; the extra digits mostly aren't.

What the 287 does at the bench that the 117 can't

Turn the question around and the gaps are just as large. The 117 has no temperature function, no microamp or milliamp range, and reads frequency only to 50 kHz. The 287 covers all three and adds recording.

The microamp gap is the decisive one for electronics. A microcontroller board in sleep, a sensor loop or a furnace flame sensor all run at currents the 117 can't see: anything under 1 mA reads as zero or a single count. The 287's 500 µA range resolves 0.01 µA, with Fluke publishing its burden voltage at 102 µV per µA so you can work out how much the meter disturbs the circuit. Our guide on how to measure microamps explains the setup.

Precision in practice: a 120 V outlet

Electricians mostly measure mains, so here is what the accuracy gap means there. On a 120 V AC reading, the 117 uses its 600.0 V range (0.1 V per count) and Fluke allows 1.0% plus 3 counts: 1.2 V + 0.3 V = ±1.5 V. The 287 uses its 500.00 V range (0.01 V per count) with 0.3% plus 25 counts at 45–65 Hz: 0.36 V + 0.25 V ≈ ±0.61 V. This is arithmetic on Fluke's published lines, not a measurement.

For deciding whether an outlet has 120 V, a breaker has tripped or a voltage drop exceeds a few percent, ±1.5 V is plenty. The 287's tighter figure helps when you document readings for a report or compare them over time, which is where its logging comes in.

Recording: Min/Max/Avg vs a full log

Both meters can be left on a circuit. The 117's Min/Max/Avg reports the extremes and average since you started, which is enough to confirm that a voltage sagged during a motor start. The 287 records up to 10,000 intervals with a real-time clock, shows the run as a graph on its screen and transfers it to a PC over an infrared link with FlukeView Forms. That turns "the lights flicker sometimes" or "the board resets overnight" into a timeline you can line up against loads and events. Battery life reflects the different jobs: Fluke quotes 100 hours minimum, 200 in logging mode, on six AA cells for the 287, and about 400 hours typical on one 9V for the 117. The two figures are on different bases (minimum against typical), so treat the comparison as rough.

Match the rating to the work, and verify the meter

The 117 is rated CAT III 600V: panels, feeders and branch circuits, but not the meter base or service drop. The 287 adds CAT IV 600V for those locations; its Amazon listing says Category I/II, which conflicts with Fluke's rating, and we use Fluke's. Whatever you carry, test the meter on a known live source before and after checking a circuit you plan to touch, and leave service-entrance work to a licensed electrician or your utility if you aren't trained for it.

Choose the Fluke 287 if…

  • ✓You design, repair or calibrate electronics and need microamps, fine resolution or 0.025% DC accuracy
  • ✓You track down intermittent faults and want a time-stamped record with a graph on the meter
  • ✓You measure temperature or frequencies above 50 kHz
  • ✓You need CAT IV 600V for work at the service entrance as well as at the bench

Choose the Fluke 117 if…

  • ✓You're a residential or light commercial electrician working panels, outlets and lighting circuits
  • ✓You want a non-contact voltage check built into the meter you already carry
  • ✓Ghost voltage on switched-off wiring regularly wastes your time
  • ✓You want a lighter meter with long battery life for all-day field use

The bottom line

For electricians, the Fluke 117 is the better meter: VoltAlert, LoZ, a CAT III 600V rating that covers panel work, 550 g and about 400 hours on a 9V battery, and it's one of the meters the owner has tested hands-on. For electronics, diagnostics and anyone who needs to record a fault over time, the Fluke 287 is in a different league, with 50,000 counts, ±(0.025% + 2) DC accuracy, 0.01 µA resolution, temperature, 10,000-interval logging, CAT IV 600V and a lifetime warranty.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 287 better than the Fluke 117?+

On specifications, yes: more counts, tighter accuracy, microamps, temperature, logging and a CAT IV 600V rating. For electricians, no. The 287 has no non-contact voltage detection or LoZ input, weighs 871 g and runs on six AA cells. The 117's VoltAlert and AutoVolt LoZ solve everyday wiring problems the 287 wasn't designed for.

Does the Fluke 287 have LoZ or VoltAlert?+

No. Fluke's 287/289 manual marks LoZ as a Model 289 feature, and no non-contact voltage function is listed for the 287. The Fluke 117 has both: VoltAlert detects AC voltage without probe contact, and AutoVolt measures through an input of about 3 kΩ that drains ghost voltage from switched-off conductors.

Can the Fluke 117 measure microamps or temperature?+

No to both. The 117's current ranges are 6 A and 10 A with a finest step of 1 mA, and it has no temperature input; in the 110 Series that's the Fluke 116. The 287 has a 500 µA range resolving 0.01 µA and reads temperature from -200 to 1350 °C, excluding the probe.

Is the Fluke 287 safe to use on electrical panels?+

Yes. Fluke rates it CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, higher than the 117's CAT III 600V, so it is rated from the service entrance inward. The 287's Amazon listing describes it as Category I/II, which conflicts with Fluke; we go by Fluke. Its weaknesses on panels are practical, not safety-related: no LoZ, no NCV and a bulkier body.

Which has the better warranty, the Fluke 287 or 117?+

The 287. Fluke gives the 280 Series a lifetime limited warranty, against three years for the 117. That fits the price difference and the 287's role as a long-term bench instrument. The 117's shorter term is still typical for Fluke's electrician meters, and it's the meter we've tested hands-on.

Should an electronics hobbyist buy the 117 or the 287?+

Neither is an obvious hobby buy. The 117 lacks the µA range and temperature most electronics benches use, and the 287 costs far more than a hobby bench needs. Meters with 0.01 µA resolution at lower prices are ranked in our best multimeters for electronics repair guide; choose the 287 when logging and Fluke's accuracy specs matter.
