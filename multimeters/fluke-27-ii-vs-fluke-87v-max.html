Fluke 27 II vs Fluke 87V MAX

The Fluke 27 II and 87V MAX are both sealed to IP67, both run about 800 hours on three AA cells and both carry CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V and a lifetime warranty, but they measure AC in different ways. The 87V MAX is a True RMS industrial meter with a low-pass filter for variable frequency drives, 250 µs peak capture, temperature and a 19,999-count mode, and it is rated for a 4 m drop. The 27 II is average responding, so it reads clean sine waves correctly but not drive outputs or electronic loads; in exchange it carries MSHA approval, which Fluke doesn't list for the MAX.

Key differences

  • →AC method: the 87V MAX is True RMS; the 27 II is the average-responding "AVG" model and is only accurate on sine waves.
  • →Drive tools: the 87V MAX lists a low-pass filter (3 dB at 1 kHz) and 250 µs peak capture. Fluke lists neither for the 27 II.
  • →Extra functions on the MAX: temperature with an included probe, a 19,999-count mode and Input Alert for leads in the wrong jack.
  • →Accuracy: the MAX halves the DC volts percentage and prints accuracy for ohms, capacitance and frequency; the 27 II prints only ranges for those.
  • →Toughness: both IP67 with the same cold rating, but the MAX is drop-rated to 4 m against 3 m. The 27 II is MSHA approved; the MAX page doesn't list MSHA.

Rugged meets rugged, with different AC brains

People shortlist these two for the same reason: both survive rain, dust and cold that would kill an ordinary meter. What separates them is not the shell but the measuring method. The 87V MAX carries the measuring set of Fluke's industrial 87 line into a sealed case. The 27 II carries an average-responding front end, which Fluke and Amazon both label "AVG", into a sealed case. On a plant with drives, that one word decides the purchase.

What a drive puts on the motor leads

A variable frequency drive rectifies the incoming AC to a DC bus, then switches that bus on and off thousands of times a second. The motor leads see full-height pulses whose widths rise and fall in a sine pattern. The motor's inductance smooths the current, so the motor responds mainly to the low-frequency fundamental, but a meter connected across those leads sees every switching edge.

With its low-pass filter on, the 87V MAX attenuates content above about 1 kHz, so its True RMS reading tracks the fundamental the motor actually uses. The 27 II has two problems on the same leads. Its averaging assumes a sine, which a pulse train is not, and it lists no filter while accepting AC up to 30 kHz, so the switching edges reach the converter. Its band also starts at 40 Hz, so a drive turned down below that, for a slow conveyor or fan, sits outside its published range.

Where the 27 II still earns its place

Not every harsh site has drives on every circuit. A mine's mobile equipment shop, a farm, a quarry or an irrigation district may spend most of its meter time on 12 and 24 V DC systems, fuses, solenoid coils, continuity and line-fed motors. Averaging doesn't affect DC, resistance or continuity at all, and on a utility sine the 27 II's AC volts line, 0.5% + 3, is actually tighter on paper than the MAX's 0.7% + 4. Add MSHA approval and a case that is 1.7 cm shorter, and it is a sensible meter for that work.

The catch is that a meter tends to outlive the job it was bought for. If there is any chance you'll be asked to check a drive, a UPS or a panel of LED lighting, the average-responding front end becomes a liability, and that is a hard thing to remember in the moment.

Decide by the waveform, then by the paperwork

If you will ever measure AC from power electronics, buy the 87V MAX: True RMS and the filter are not optional for that work. If your site requires MSHA-approved instruments, the 27 II is the one of these two Fluke lists, and if you also need True RMS on a mine site, the Fluke 28 II combines both.

Safety: drive circuits stay dangerous after the motor stops

A drive's DC bus capacitors can hold a lethal charge after the disconnect is opened; wait the discharge time on the drive's label and confirm zero volts DC on the bus before touching anything. Both meters are rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, so use leads with at least the same rating, test the meter on a known live source before and after proving dead, and wear the arc-flash PPE your site requires. Don't trust an average-responding reading to tell you a drive output or a loaded neutral is within limits. Neither meter is intrinsically safe, so keep both out of classified areas. When in doubt, stop and call a qualified electrician.

Buy the Fluke 87V MAX for industrial work: True RMS with a 1 kHz low-pass filter makes it trustworthy on drives and electronic loads, it adds temperature, peak capture and a hi-res mode, it publishes accuracy for every core function and it is rated for a 4 m drop. Buy the Fluke 27 II only if you need an MSHA-approved sealed meter and your AC work is line-fed sine waves, or most of your readings are DC.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 27 II good for VFD work?+

No. It is average responding and Fluke lists no low-pass filter for it, so readings on a drive's output leads can be well off. Its AC band also starts at 40 Hz. The 87V MAX is True RMS with a filter that rolls off at about 1 kHz, which is the setup Fluke designs for drive outputs.

Is the Fluke 87V MAX MSHA approved?+

Fluke's 87V MAX page lists CE and CSA approvals and doesn't mention MSHA, while the 27 II and 28 II pages both list MSHA approval. If your mine's electrical program specifies MSHA-approved instruments, buy one of those two or get written confirmation from Fluke before choosing the MAX.

Which is tougher, the Fluke 27 II or the 87V MAX?+

On paper the 87V MAX, by one drop-test meter: 4 m against 3 m. Both are IP67, so dust-tight and protected against temporary immersion, and both share the −15 to 55 °C operating range with 20-minute excursions to −40 °C. They weigh the same 698.5 g in their holsters.

Does the Fluke 27 II measure temperature?+

Fluke doesn't list a temperature function for the 27 II. The 87V MAX reads −200 to 1090 °C at the meter and ships with the 80BK-A bead probe, which covers −40 to 260 °C. If you check bearings, enclosures or pipework, that saves carrying a separate thermometer.

Why does the 27 II have a higher AC bandwidth than the 87V MAX?+

Fluke lists the 27 II's AC band as 40 Hz to 30 kHz and the 87V MAX's as 20 kHz. A wider band only helps on clean sine signals, because the 27 II is average responding. On drive outputs, a wider band without a filter lets in more switching noise, which is why the MAX adds a low-pass filter.

Do the Fluke 27 II and 87V MAX use the same batteries?+

Yes. Both take three AA cells and Fluke rates both at about 800 hours; for the 87V MAX that figure is without the backlight. Standard AA cells are easy to find on a remote site, and the long run time means battery changes are rare on either meter.
