Rigol DS1054Z vs Hantek DSO5102P for Arduino

Rigol DS1054Z vs Hantek DSO5102P for Arduino work comes down to how many pins you need to watch at once. The Rigol has four inputs at 50 MHz; the Hantek has two at 100 MHz, with built-in math, FFT, 32 automatic measurements and a pulse-width trigger, but a short record length. Arduino signals are slow enough for either scope's bandwidth, so inputs and memory decide this one more than megahertz.

  • →Bandwidth: 100 MHz on the Hantek against 50 MHz on the Rigol. Both are far above what a 16 MHz Arduino Uno produces.
  • →Both quote 1 GSa/s. Rigol's figure is for one channel and falls to 250 MSa/s per channel with three or four on; Hantek quotes 1 GSa/s real-time without a per-channel breakdown in our data.
  • →Hantek lists a record length of 24K points in its datasheet and 40K on its product page; the sources disagree, and either is short for long bus captures. Spec by spec

Each maker's published figures, charted. Where a figure is missing from one side, it is left out rather than estimated.

The last chart explains why record length matters more on an Arduino bench than the bandwidth headline. To show a whole 9600-baud UART byte, the Hantek has to slow its sampling well below 1 GSa/s, and to show a 20-byte message (about 21 ms) its 24K to 40K points work out to roughly 1.1 to 1.9 MSa/s. That is still over a hundred samples per UART bit, so slow serial is fine; the squeeze comes with long bursts of fast SPI. Rigol lists 12 Mpts standard for the DS1054Z on its product page (24 Mpts as an option; the 2020 datasheet says 24 Mpts), hundreds of times the Hantek's record, so it can hold that 21 ms message at well over 100 MSa/s. Background on these figures: what GSa/s means and what oscilloscope bandwidth is.

For Arduino projects: which pins each scope can show you

A classic Arduino Uno runs from a 16 MHz clock, and the signals on its header are slower still. By the usual rule of buying three to five times the signal frequency, the DS1054Z is comfortable up to roughly 10 to 17 MHz and the DSO5102P up to roughly 20 to 33 MHz. Square-wave edges carry harmonics well above the fundamental, so faster is never wasted, but typical Arduino buses fit inside both scopes' range:

Rigol DS1054Z: the multi-pin view

Most Arduino bugs are timing bugs between pins: a chip select that rises one clock too early, a stepper driver that misses steps because direction changes too close to a step pulse, an interrupt that fires before the sensor has finished its I2C reply. Seeing those means seeing three or four pins in one capture, and that is what the DS1054Z is for. With all four inputs on it still samples each at 250 MSa/s, which is over 30 samples per cycle of an 8 MHz SPI clock. Its limits for Arduino work are the menus, which take time to learn, and the 50 MHz ceiling, which only starts to matter if you move to much faster boards. More in our DS1054Z review.

Hantek DSO5102P: two pins, measured for you

The DSO5102P suits sketches where two pins tell the whole story. I2C needs exactly two inputs, and a UART link needs at most two. Its 32 automatic measurements save cursor work when you are tuning PWM frequency and duty cycle, the pulse-width trigger can wait for a pulse shorter or longer than you expect (a handy way to catch a missed servo pulse), and FFT shows noise from a motor or a switching regulator on an analog input. What it can't do is show SPI's four lines together, decode any bus into bytes, or keep a high sample rate across a long transaction, given its 24K to 40K point record.

Count wires before megahertz

For Arduino work, write down the bus or pins you'll probe most. If the answer is SPI, several stepper or motor-driver lines, or anything plus a trigger pin, four inputs win. If it is I2C, serial or a single PWM output, two inputs are enough and the Hantek's measurements earn their keep. Our Arduino oscilloscope guide covers other options.

Relay modules and mains

The DSO5102P runs from mains, and on any earthed bench scope the probe ground clip is normally connected to mains earth. That is harmless on a USB-powered Arduino, but never clip it onto the AC side of a relay module or a non-isolated power supply. Use a differential probe or isolation rated for mains if you must measure there.

  • ✓You debug timing between several pins, such as a stepper driver's step, direction and enable lines

The bottom line

For most Arduino benches the Rigol DS1054Z is the better buy: four inputs show SPI and multi-pin timing in one capture, 50 MHz is ample for an Uno, and it carries our Hands-on Tested badge. The Hantek DSO5102P makes sense if your projects are mostly I2C, serial and PWM on two pins, you value its automatic measurements and pulse-width trigger, and it costs clearly less when you buy.

Frequently asked questions

Is 50 MHz enough for Arduino, or do I need the Hantek's 100 MHz?+

50 MHz is enough for a classic Arduino. An Uno runs at 16 MHz and its SPI clock tops out at 8 MHz, inside the DS1054Z's comfortable range of roughly 10 to 17 MHz. The DSO5102P's 100 MHz becomes useful on faster 32-bit boards whose clocks and SPI buses run at tens of megahertz.

Can either scope decode Arduino serial, I2C or SPI?+

Hantek lists no serial decoding for the DSO5102P, so you would read bits from the waveform by eye. Rigol's DS1000Z datasheet lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C and SPI decoding for the DS1054Z, but its product page describes bus trigger and decode as an option, so check what is enabled on the unit you buy. Either way, both scopes show the raw signals, which is enough to spot wrong timing or missing pulses.

How many channels do I need to debug SPI on an Arduino?+

Four to see the whole bus: clock, MOSI, MISO and chip select. The DS1054Z can show all four in one capture. On the two-input DSO5102P you would probe two lines at a time, typically clock with one data line, and repeat the capture for the others.

Is the DSO5102P's short memory a problem for Arduino work?+

Sometimes. Hantek lists 24K points in its datasheet and 40K on its product page. That holds 24 to 40 microseconds at 1 GSa/s, so longer captures force a lower sample rate. Slow UART and PWM still look fine, but long bursts of fast SPI or I2C traffic may be under-sampled.

Which one is hands-on tested?+

The Rigol DS1054Z carries our Hands-on Tested badge. The Hantek DSO5102P assessment is desk research from Hantek's datasheet and product page, which disagree on record length, and it has no full OhmSavvy review yet. Treat its verdict here as a spec-sheet comparison.
