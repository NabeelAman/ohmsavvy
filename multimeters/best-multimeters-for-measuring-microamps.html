What microamp measurement asks of a meter

Microamps show up when a product is supposed to be doing nothing: a microcontroller in deep sleep, a remote on standby, a sensor node between readings, or a car module that should have shut down. A meter has to put itself in series with that current, resolve it in fractions of a microamp, and avoid disturbing the circuit with its own voltage drop. Most general-purpose meters fall short on at least one of those, and some can't read below milliamps at all. Our step-by-step guides to measuring microamps and measuring standby current cover the hookup.

This ranking puts the lowest current range, its resolution and the published burden voltage first, then logging, because a sleep-current problem rarely reveals itself in the ten seconds you spend watching a display. Every figure comes from the maker's spec tables or manual. If you're trying to find out why a board or appliance pulls more than it should, start with a circuit drawing too much current; for microcontroller boards, see measuring an Arduino's current consumption.

Current ranges and fuses

On a current range the meter is close to a short circuit. Never touch the leads across a battery, power supply or outlet while they sit in the µA/mA or A jack: the fuse will blow at best, and on mains a meter without proper high-breaking-capacity fuses can fail violently. Switch off, break the circuit, connect the meter in series, then power up. Move the red lead back to the voltage jack as soon as you finish.

How the ten compare on paper

What to look for in a microamp meter

A dedicated µA range

Look for a range of a few hundred microamps. A meter whose smallest current range is in milliamps can't see sleep current at all.

Resolution at that range

0.01 µA steps separate 3.20 µA from 3.27 µA; 0.1 µA steps round both to 3.2 µA.

Published burden voltage

The voltage the meter itself drops per microamp. Lower is better, and a figure in the spec means you can correct for it.

Range headroom

Devices wake up and draw milliamps for a moment. A µA range that tops out too low forces a range change mid-test.

Logging or a PC link

Sleep current often changes over minutes or hours. Recording beats watching the display.

Lead and jack layout

A separate µA/mA jack, and a fuse you can replace easily, matter when you're probing current every day.

Burden voltage in practice

A burden of 100 µV/µA sounds tiny, and at 5 µA it is: half a millivolt. But if the device wakes and pulls 2 mA while the meter is still on its µA range, the same shunt would want to drop 200 mV, enough to brown out a 1.8 V or 3.3 V part. That's why careful microamp work uses a bypass switch, and why a published burden figure is worth more than another digit of resolution. For more on reading accuracy specs, see multimeter accuracy for electronics repair; for car standby drain, which usually sits in milliamps, see measuring parasitic battery drain.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Read the current table, not the dial

Model families share dials and marketing photos, so a µA symbol on a picture doesn't prove a given meter has the range. Fluke's 117 and 179, for example, have no microamp range at all. Look in the maker's specification table for the lowest current range and its resolution, and check the burden voltage line while you're there.

Plan for the wake-up spike

A sleeping device can draw a few microamps and then pull tens or hundreds of milliamps when it wakes to transmit. On a µA range that surge can blow the meter's fuse or drop enough voltage across the shunt to reset the device. Bridge the meter with a switch during start-up and radio bursts, or use a higher range first and step down once the device is quiet.

Decide whether you need a record

If you only confirm that a product sleeps below a target, any meter here will do. If you're tuning firmware or chasing a battery that dies after a week, a recording meter such as the Fluke 287, UNI-T UT181A or OWON B41T+ shows the pattern over time. That trade decides more of the ranking than raw resolution does.

Keep mains work in mind

Several meters here, including the Fluke 87V and Brymen BM235, are also built for electrical panels, while the OWON models don't state a CAT rating on the maker's page. If the same meter will go into a distribution board, weigh the safety rating as heavily as the microamp spec.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Can any multimeter measure microamps?+

No. Many electrician's meters, such as the Fluke 117, start their current measurement at amps, and others begin at tens of milliamps. To read microamps you need a meter with a dedicated µA range, usually a few hundred microamps full scale. Check the spec table for a range labeled in µA, not just a µA symbol on the dial of a sibling model.

What is burden voltage and why does it matter for microamps?+

Burden voltage is the drop across the meter's internal shunt while current flows through it. It is specified per microamp, so a meter rated at 100 µV/µA drops 10 mV at 100 µA. That rarely matters at sleep current, but when the circuit wakes and draws much more, a large drop can starve a low-voltage device or trigger a brown-out reset during the test.

How do I measure the sleep current of an Arduino or ESP32 board?+

Break the supply line and put the meter in series, with the red lead in the µA/mA jack. Start on a milliamp range so the boot current doesn't blow the fuse, then switch to the µA range once the board is asleep. Many people bridge the meter with a switch or jumper during wake-up and open it only for the sleep reading.

Is 0.1 µA resolution enough for low-power electronics?+

For confirming that a board sleeps at tens of microamps, yes. For comparing firmware changes that move standby current by fractions of a microamp, 0.01 µA resolution is easier to read. Remember that the accuracy spec adds a fixed number of digits, so the last digit on any meter is less certain than the display suggests.

Why does my multimeter read zero on the microamp range?+

Usually the meter isn't actually in series, the red lead is still in the voltage jack, or the µA/mA fuse has blown, which often happens after measuring a wake-up surge on a low range. Check the fuse with the meter's continuity mode, confirm the lead positions, and make sure the circuit's current really flows through the meter and not around it.

Do I need a logging multimeter to measure standby current?+

Not for a spot check, but standby current often changes as radios wake, timers fire or batteries settle. A meter that records readings, such as the Fluke 287, UNI-T UT181A or OWON B41T+, shows those changes over hours. Without logging, you only see whatever happens while you're watching the display.
