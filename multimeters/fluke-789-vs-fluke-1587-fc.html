Fluke 789 vs Fluke 1587 FC: Process Calibration or Insulation Testing?

Pick the Fluke 789 if your work is 4–20 mA instrument loops, and the Fluke 1587 FC if your work is proving that motors, cables and windings are still insulated. They aren't rivals so much as two specialist tools that happen to share a body size, a CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating and a 3-year warranty. The 789 sources, simulates and powers loop current at 0.05% of span; the 1587 FC applies 50 to 1000 V DC and reads insulation up to 2 GΩ. If you do neither job, a general multimeter with a 10 A input will serve you better than either.

Key differences

  • →Purpose: the 789 is a loop calibrator built into a multimeter; the 1587 FC is an insulation tester built into a multimeter. Neither can do the other's specialist job.
  • →Output: the 789 drives up to 24 mA at 24 V compliance into a loop; the 1587 FC drives about 1 mA at 50 to 1000 V DC into insulation.
  • →mA reading: the 789 resolves 0.001 mA at 0.05% + 2 and shows % of span; the 1587 FC reads mA at ±(0.2% + 2) with no span display.
  • →Meter extras: only the 1587 FC lists capacitance, temperature, a low-pass filter and built-in Fluke Connect.
  • →Shared: CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, True RMS AC, four AA cells, the same 50 × 100 × 203 mm footprint, no 10 A input and a 3-year warranty.

A loop on one side, a winding on the other

It helps to picture where each meter gets clipped on. An instrument technician puts the 789 in series with a running loop, or on the terminals of a transmitter sitting on a workbench, and the circuit stays energized the whole time because the 24 V loop is the thing being checked. An electrician uses the 1587 FC the other way around: the feeder gets switched off, locked and proven dead, and only then does the meter put its own high voltage onto the winding or cable. One tool works with the power on; the insulation function only works with it off.

We haven't hands-on tested either tool. The 789 carries a 4.7 and the 1587 FC a 4.6 from desk research, and every number here comes from Fluke's data sheets, product pages and 789 manual, or the official Amazon listings, as recorded in our existing Fluke 789 and 1587 FC comparisons.

What each tool puts out

Both meters push current into something, which is exactly why they can't stand in for each other. The 789 is a precise current source with a modest voltage behind it. The 1587 FC is a high-voltage source with a deliberately small current limit. Plotted together, the voltage gap is about forty to one.

Two details are worth knowing. The 1587 FC's test voltage tolerance is +20% / −0%, so the 1000 V setting is allowed to land anywhere from 1000 V up to 1200 V, never below. And the 789's compliance figure depends on which Fluke document you read: the data sheet and product page say 24 V, while the 789 manual says 28 V with a healthy battery. Either is enough for typical loop loads; Fluke rates the source at 1,200 Ω at 20 mA.

Can the 1587 FC check a 4–20 mA loop?

Partly. Its 60 mA range will read loop current if you break the loop and wire it in series, so it can tell you a transmitter is outputting roughly 12 mA. What it can't do is generate a signal, so there's no way to drive a PLC input to 4, 12 and 20 mA and watch the scaling, stroke a valve, or power a transmitter that has no supply. Precision differs too. Here is the worst-case error on a 12 mA reading, from each meter's published accuracy:

Can the 789 tell you a motor is safe to restart?

No. Its resistance function tops out at 40 MΩ and uses the meter's own small test voltage, which can show a dead short between a winding and the frame but says little about insulation that only breaks down under stress. A proper insulation test applies hundreds of volts precisely because weak insulation often looks perfect at a few volts. The 1587 FC does that at five settings, reads up to 2 GΩ, and runs timed PI and DAR tests whose results land in the Fluke Connect app with temperature compensation.

The 789 also lacks the motor-side extras Fluke lists for the 1587 FC: capacitance for run capacitors, temperature with the included K-type probe, and a low-pass filter for reading a drive's output. For the winding-test procedure itself, see how to check a motor winding for a short, and for how the 1587 FC compares with Fluke's simpler insulation model, read Fluke 1577 vs Fluke 1587 FC.

Who should buy neither

Most people who search for these two model numbers don't need either one. If your day is outlets, breakers, lighting, HVAC controls or motor running current, both specialists come up short in the same place: the 789 tops out at 1 A (0.44 A continuous) and the 1587 FC at 400 mA, with no 10 A jack on either. You would be paying for a calibrator or a megohmmeter you never switch on, and still need a clamp for load current.

Where the sources disagree

Fluke gives the 789's weight as 600 g in the data sheet and 610 g on the product page, and its sourcing battery life as 10 hours at 12 mA in one and at 20 mA in the other. The 789 manual lists 28 V source compliance against 24 V elsewhere. For the 1587 FC, Amazon lists an item weight of 1645 g against Fluke's 550 g, and Fluke's page calls the included case both soft and hard. Fluke doesn't state a drop rating for the 1587 FC or an IP rating for the 789. Amazon stock of the 789 came from a third-party seller when we checked.

Safety: insulation testing means up to 1200 V on your leads

The 1587 FC deliberately generates up to 1000 V DC, and its tolerance lets that reach 1200 V. Run an insulation test only on equipment that is switched off, locked out, tagged and proven dead with a voltmeter you have checked on a known live source. The meter's 30 V lockout refuses to start on a live circuit, but it is a backstop, not a substitute for lockout. Disconnect drives, PLC cards, transmitters, surge suppressors and anything electronic, because a test voltage meant for a winding can destroy them; never run an insulation test on a 4–20 mA loop with instruments still connected. Keep clear of probe tips and conductors during the test, warn people nearby, and let the meter discharge long cables and large motors before you touch them. Work on the 789's loops can also put you in cabinets with 120 V or higher control power, so get permission and put loops in manual first. Both meters are rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V and no higher. If you aren't trained and authorized for this work, call a qualified electrician or instrument technician.

Neither if…

Your work isn't specifically loop-current/HART or insulation testing — both of these are specialist instruments; a general multimeter with a 10 A input, such as the Fluke 87V, serves everyday electrical work better.

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 789 for 4–20 mA work: it measures loop current at 0.001 mA, sources and simulates at 0.05% of span, and powers transmitters with its own 24 V supply and HART resistor. Buy the Fluke 1587 FC for insulation work: 50 to 1000 V tests to 2 GΩ, PI and DAR through Fluke Connect, plus temperature and capacitance. Plant technicians who cover both instruments and motors need both tools. Everyone else is better served by a general multimeter with a 10 A input, such as the Fluke 87V.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 789 or the 1587 FC better for an industrial maintenance technician?+

It depends on which assets you look after. Instrument loops, transmitters and analog inputs call for the 789; motors, cables and switchgear call for the 1587 FC. A technician covering both usually carries both, since neither does the other's specialist job. If you mostly troubleshoot live equipment, a Fluke 87V with a clamp may cover more of your day.

Can the Fluke 1587 FC source 4–20 mA?+

No. Fluke lists no current output, simulate mode or loop supply for the 1587 FC. It can measure loop current on its 60 mA range if you wire it in series, at ±(0.2% + 2), but it can't drive a PLC input, stroke a valve or power a 2-wire transmitter. Those jobs need a loop calibrator such as the 789.

Can the Fluke 789 do an insulation resistance test?+

No. The 789's resistance range reaches 40 MΩ using a low internal test voltage, which finds hard shorts but not insulation that fails under stress. Insulation testing needs hundreds of volts and readings into the gigohms, which the 1587 FC provides with 50, 100, 250, 500 and 1000 V settings and a 2 GΩ ceiling.

Do the Fluke 789 and 1587 FC have the same safety rating?+

Yes. Fluke rates both CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, so either can measure voltage at a service entrance up to 600 V or on 1000 V-class circuits inside a building. The rating covers their voltmeter inputs; it doesn't make insulation testing on live circuits acceptable, which the 1587 FC blocks above 30 V anyway.

Can either meter measure motor running current?+

Not at typical motor loads. The 789's current input is rated 1 A, with 0.44 A continuous, and the 1587 FC stops at 400 mA. Neither has a 10 A jack. Use a clamp meter for running and inrush current; Fluke sells 1587 FC kits that include its i400 clamp, but the bare meter on Amazon doesn't.

Which one connects to the Fluke Connect app?+

The 1587 FC has Fluke Connect built in over a 2.4 GHz radio, and uses it to graph PI and DAR tests, store readings and apply temperature compensation. Fluke lists Fluke Connect logging for the 789 only through the optional IR3000FC infrared adapter, so out of the box the 789 has no wireless link.
