HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test a Golf Cart Battery With a Multimeter

To test a golf cart battery with a multimeter, charge the cart fully, let it rest, then measure each battery on its own posts with the meter on DC volts and compare the readings. In a healthy pack every battery reads within a small spread of the others; one battery that sits clearly lower at rest, or drops faster when you drive, is the one dragging the whole cart down. This guide gives resting voltages for 6, 8 and 12 V batteries and full-pack totals for 36 and 48 V carts, then shows how to confirm the weak battery under load and with a hydrometer.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 15, 2026

A 6,000-count meter like the UNI-T UT139C reads each battery to the hundredth of a volt, which is the resolution you need to compare batteries in a pack. See our UNI-T UT139C review.

Golf cart packs can hurt you

  • A 36 or 48 V pack can deliver hundreds of amps. A wrench across two posts will arc and can weld or explode a terminal. Remove rings and watches and use insulated tools.
  • Flooded batteries vent hydrogen, especially while charging, and contain sulfuric acid. Wear eye protection, keep sparks away, and test in a ventilated space.
  • Put the cart in tow or maintenance mode, or set the run switch to off, before touching cables, and keep the drive wheels off the ground or the brake set during any load test.
  • Lithium packs have their own BMS and are sealed. Don't open one; take faults to the cart dealer or the battery maker.

How a golf cart battery pack is wired

Most lead-acid golf carts run a 36 V or 48 V pack made by chaining smaller deep-cycle batteries in series, positive of one to negative of the next. A 36 V cart typically uses six 6 V batteries. A 48 V cart uses six 8 V batteries, four 12 V batteries, or eight 6 V batteries. Series wiring adds the voltages but keeps the capacity of a single battery, which is why one weak battery limits the whole cart: the pack can only deliver what its weakest member holds.

Each flooded lead-acid battery is itself a stack of 2 V cells. A 6 V battery has three cells, an 8 V has four, and a 12 V has six. A fully charged cell rests at about 2.12 V, so a full 6 V battery reads about 3 × 2.12 = 6.37 V, an 8 V about 4 × 2.12 = 8.49 V and a 12 V about 6 × 2.12 = 12.73 V. Those per-cell numbers are the key to every reading on this page.

1Charge the pack fully and let it rest

Let the cart's charger finish a complete cycle. Then unplug it and leave the cart parked, key off, for at least six hours; overnight is better. A freshly charged lead-acid battery carries surface charge that inflates the reading by a few tenths of a volt per battery, which hides a weak one. If you can't wait, switching the headlights on for a few minutes removes most of the surface charge on carts that have lights. Check the electrolyte level before charging, and top up with distilled water after charging if the plates are exposed.

2Measure the whole pack

Set the meter to DC volts on a range above 60 V, or auto. Put the black probe on the main negative post, the one where the heavy cable leaves the pack toward the controller, and the red probe on the main positive. A rested, full 36 V pack of six 6 V batteries reads about 38.2 V, and a full 48 V pack about 50.9 V. A pack well below that after a full charge has at least one battery that won't take or hold a charge.

3Measure each battery on its own posts

Now measure every battery individually, probes on that battery's own positive and negative posts, not on the cable lugs. Write the numbers down in order. In a healthy pack they cluster tightly. The illustrative readings below come from a 48 V pack with one tired battery; notice how number 4 stands out even though the pack total looks only a little low.

A battery 0.2 V or more below its neighbors after the same full charge is the suspect. Divide by the number of cells to see how serious it is: battery 4 is about 0.21 V low across four cells, roughly 0.05 V per cell, which lines up with roughly a quarter less charge. A battery about 2 V low, for example an 8 V battery reading near 6.4 V, very likely has a dead or shorted cell.

4Load test by driving the cart

Resting voltage shows state of charge, not the ability to deliver current. To see that, load the pack. The simplest load is the cart itself: with a helper driving up a slope, or the brake firmly set and the motor pulling on a hill, read each battery in turn. Every battery sags under load; the weak one sags more. In an illustrative pack, five 8 V batteries might read about 7.9 V while one falls to 7.2 V. That 0.7 V difference under the same current is the signature of high internal resistance or lost capacity.

Measure again after a normal round. The battery that ends a round lowest, and especially one whose voltage keeps sliding while the others hold steady, is the one to replace. Dedicated golf cart discharge testers do this more precisely by pulling a fixed current for a timed run, but a careful drive test with a meter finds the same culprit in most packs.

5Confirm with a hydrometer (flooded batteries)

A battery hydrometer reads the specific gravity of the acid in each cell, which a meter can't reach. On a fully charged flooded battery, expect about 1.265 to 1.280, corrected for temperature per the hydrometer's instructions. Battery makers commonly treat a spread of more than about 0.050 between cells in the same battery as a sign of a bad cell. Test only after charging, and only on flooded batteries with removable caps; sealed AGM and gel batteries can't be checked this way.

Compare, don't just measure

A golf cart pack is only as strong as its weakest battery. The absolute numbers matter less than how each battery compares with its neighbors after the same charge and under the same load.

6Check cables and terminals under load

Sometimes the batteries are fine and a connection isn't. While the cart pulls on a hill, put one probe on a battery post and the other on the cable lug clamped to it. A clean connection reads a few hundredths of a volt at most; anything over about 0.1 V means corrosion or a loose nut, and that joint will run hot. Clean the post and lug, retorque to the battery maker's figure, and replace any lug that has discolored or melted. The same technique is explained in more detail in how to check voltage drop on a car, and white or green crust on the posts is covered in why a battery terminal corrodes.

Deep-cycle batteries last far longer when you recharge them before they fall below about 50%. Running a cart until it crawls home regularly takes the pack into the bottom rows, and that is a common reason packs fail early. If the pack won't charge at all, the problem may be the charger rather than the batteries; our guide to testing a battery charger with a multimeter walks through the output checks.

Testing a lithium golf cart battery

Many newer carts use a single lithium (usually LiFePO4) pack with a built-in battery management system. A common 48 V class LiFePO4 pack uses 16 cells in series, about 51.2 V nominal, and reads roughly 53 to 54 V when full and rested; check the label or manual, as packs vary. You can only measure the pack terminals, not the cells, and the voltage curve is flat through most of the charge, so voltage is a poor fuel gauge. If the pack reads 0 V or far below nominal, the BMS has likely disconnected it to protect the cells; use the pack's app or display and contact the maker rather than forcing a charge. For cell-level diagnosis on LiFePO4, see the 48 V LiFePO4 imbalance test.

Which multimeter to use to test golf cart batteries

You need a DC voltage range that covers the whole pack, around 60 V, and two-decimal resolution to compare individual batteries. A backlit display helps under a cart seat. Any of these does the job; cheaper meters are fine for battery voltage, which is low-energy, careful work.

Frequently asked questions

What should a 48 volt golf cart battery pack read when fully charged?

A rested 48 V pack of six 8 V flooded batteries typically reads about 50.9 V when full, roughly 8.49 V per battery. A pack of four 12 V batteries reads about 50.9 V too, and eight 6 V batteries about 50.9 V. Straight off the charger it reads higher because of surface charge, so let it rest several hours before judging.

How do I find a bad battery in my golf cart?

Fully charge the pack, rest it, and measure every battery on its own posts. Then drive the cart up a hill or for a few minutes and measure again with the motor pulling. The bad battery is the one that reads lowest at rest or falls furthest under load. A hydrometer then confirms which cell inside it is weak.

Can one bad battery ruin the others in a golf cart?

It can shorten their life. The charger reads the whole pack, so a weak battery that charges and empties faster pulls the pack voltage down, and the healthy batteries are overcharged or deeply cycled to compensate. Replacing the single weak battery in a young pack is often fine; in an old pack, makers usually recommend replacing the set.

What voltage is too low for a 6 volt golf cart battery?

A rested 6 V flooded battery near 6.0 V is only about a quarter to a third charged, and below about 5.85 V it is effectively flat. Regularly running batteries that low shortens their life sharply. If a 6 V battery won't rise above about 6.2 V after a full charge and rest, it has likely lost capacity or has a failed cell.

Is my golf cart charger or battery the problem?

Measure the pack before and during charging. A working charger raises the pack voltage several volts above its resting value within minutes. If the pack voltage barely moves, test the charger output and the charge receptacle. If the pack charges to a normal total but one battery reads low, the charger is fine and that battery is the problem.

Should I test golf cart batteries with the cart on or off?

Resting voltage should be taken with the key off, the charger unplugged, and the cart parked for several hours. The load test is the opposite: the cart driving, with a helper reading the meter or you reading it safely while stopped on a hill with the brake on and the motor pulling. Both readings are needed.
