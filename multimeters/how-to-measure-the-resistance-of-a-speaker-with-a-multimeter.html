How to Measure the Resistance of a Speaker With a Multimeter

To measure the resistance of a speaker with a multimeter, disconnect it from the amplifier, set the meter to its lowest ohms range, short the probes to note the lead resistance, then touch the probes to the speaker terminals and subtract. A healthy 8 Ω speaker typically reads about 6 to 7 Ω, and a 4 Ω speaker about 3 to 3.5 Ω, because the meter sees DC resistance, not impedance. OL means an open voice coil; a reading near zero suggests a short.

Amplifier off, speaker disconnected

  • Switch the amplifier or head unit off before touching the speaker wires. A resistance range cannot measure a connected, powered load, and an amplifier output can damage the meter.
  • Many amplifiers hold charge in large supply capacitors after switching off. Wait before working on terminals inside the amplifier itself, and keep to the speaker side of the wiring.

Speaker DC resistance vs nominal impedance

A speaker's rating of 4, 8 or 16 Ω is its nominal impedance, the opposition it presents to an AC audio signal. It changes with frequency and includes effects from the coil's inductance and the cone's motion. A multimeter instead passes a small direct current through the coil and reports the resistance of the copper or aluminum wire, often written as Re or DCR on spec sheets. That figure is always lower than the nominal rating.

1Disconnect the speaker

With the amplifier off, remove at least one wire from the speaker terminals. Left connected, the meter reads the driver in parallel with the amplifier's output stage and any other speakers on the same channel. In a car or home cabinet, getting at the driver's own tabs gives the clearest answer; measuring from the far end of the cable also includes the wire, which is useful for spotting a damaged run. The methods in testing electrical wires help separate a cable fault from a driver fault.

2Zero the test lead resistance

On low-ohm readings the probe leads matter. Select the lowest Ω range, press the probe tips firmly together and read the display. Typical leads read a few tenths of an ohm. Either subtract that from every speaker reading or, if your meter has a relative (REL or Δ) button, press it with the probes shorted so the display zeroes out the leads.

Why a few tenths matter

On a 4 Ω driver, 0.4 Ω of lead resistance is more than 10% of the real reading, enough to make a healthy coil look borderline. Clean probe tips and a firm contact on the terminals keep that error small and repeatable.

3Measure the voice coil

Hold one probe on each speaker terminal. Polarity does not matter for the reading. Let the display settle, then subtract the lead value. While the probes are on, press the cone gently in and out by a few millimeters: the reading should stay steady.

Open, shorted or good: reading the result

Reading (after subtracting leads)Likely condition
About 75–90% of the nominal rating, steadyCoil intact
OLOpen voice coil or broken lead-out wire; driver needs replacing or reconing
Near 0 Ω, or far below the typical bandShorted turns from overheating, or a short in the wiring
Flickers as the cone movesFraying tinsel lead or a cracked joint at the terminal
Normal reading but distorted soundCoil rubbing or a torn cone; not visible to a meter

A blown speaker from overpowering often fails open, so OL is the most common bad result. Partial shorts are less frequent but show up as a reading well below the expected band, for example around 2 Ω on a driver marked 8 Ω.

Speakers with crossovers: why to disconnect

Multi-way speakers split the signal with a passive crossover. Tweeters are fed through a series capacitor, which blocks the meter's DC test current entirely, and woofers through an inductor that adds its own winding resistance. Measured at the cabinet's input, you mostly see the woofer plus its inductor, and nothing of the tweeter.

Tweeters typically read a little lower than their nominal rating, just like woofers. If the driver checks out and the tweeter is still silent, the crossover capacitor itself may have failed; our guide to testing a capacitor with a multimeter covers that next step.

Measuring speakers wired in series or parallel

When several drivers share one amplifier channel, measuring across the combination tells you the load the amplifier sees. Series connections add; parallel connections combine as in our guide to measuring resistance in a parallel circuit. Typical combined readings, using drivers that each read 6.5 Ω or 3.25 Ω DC:

WiringNominal loadTypical DC reading
Two 8 Ω in series16 Ω≈ 13 Ω
Two 8 Ω in parallel4 Ω≈ 3.3 Ω
Four 8 Ω, series-parallel8 Ω≈ 6.5 Ω
Two 4 Ω in parallel2 Ω≈ 1.6 Ω

A combined reading that is too high usually means one driver in a parallel set is open; too low suggests a short or an unintended extra connection. Check that the final nominal load suits the amplifier's rated minimum impedance.

Which multimeter is good for speaker checks

You need a resistance range with 0.1 Ω resolution and a meter whose leads and jacks make solid contact. Most auto-ranging digital meters manage this. The specification that matters is low-ohm accuracy, explained in multimeter accuracy for electronics repair. New to the dial? Start with how to use a multimeter.

A voice coil reads only a few ohms, so the lead offset matters. These meters give clear low-ohm readings, and one can subtract the leads for you.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my 8 ohm speaker read 6 ohms?

Because the 8 Ω rating is nominal impedance, an AC figure that includes the voice coil's inductance and its motion in the magnet gap across the audio band. A multimeter measures only the DC resistance of the coil wire, which is typically around 75 to 90 percent of the nominal rating. A reading of about 6 to 7 Ω is normal for an 8 Ω driver.

Can I tell a speaker's impedance from its resistance?

Roughly. Measure the DC resistance and pick the nominal value just above it: around 3.2 Ω usually means a 4 Ω driver, around 6.5 Ω usually means 8 Ω, and around 13 Ω usually means 16 Ω. Treat this as a good guess for an unmarked driver, not a specification, and confirm with the label or datasheet when you can.

Will a multimeter damage my speaker?

No. A multimeter's resistance range drives only a small test current through the coil, far less than an amplifier delivers. On some woofers you may hear a faint click or see the cone twitch as the probes connect, which is harmless. Do make sure the amplifier is off and disconnected, both for the meter's sake and for an accurate reading.

Why does my speaker read OL through the crossover?

If you are measuring a tweeter, or a whole speaker cabinet with the tweeter path, a crossover capacitor in series blocks DC completely, so the meter sees an open circuit even when the tweeter is fine. Disconnect the driver from the crossover and measure across its own terminals to get a meaningful reading.

What does a scratchy sound with a normal resistance mean?

The voice coil is probably intact but rubbing in the magnet gap, often from a sagging suspension, heat damage or debris. A multimeter cannot detect that. Press the cone gently and evenly with your fingertips; scraping or uneven movement confirms it. Readings that flicker while you move the cone suggest a fraying lead wire instead.
