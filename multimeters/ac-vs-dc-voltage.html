AC vs. DC Voltage: What's the Difference and When Is Each Used?

The difference between AC and DC voltage is direction. DC (direct current) voltage keeps the same polarity all the time, so current flows one way, as it does from a battery. AC (alternating current) voltage reverses polarity many times a second, so current flows back and forth, as it does at a wall outlet. Both are measured in volts, but they behave differently, they come from different sources, and a multimeter needs to be told which one it is looking at.

How AC and DC voltage differ

Plot voltage against time and the difference is obvious. DC is a flat line above zero: a 9V battery sits at 9 volts, falling slowly only as it discharges. AC traces a sine wave that rises to a positive peak, falls through zero to an equal negative peak, and comes back again. How often it repeats is the frequency, measured in hertz: mains electricity runs at 60 Hz in North America and 50 Hz in the UK, Europe and most of the rest of the world.

Where each one is used

As a rule, anything that plugs into the wall is fed AC, and anything that runs from a battery, or from an adapter or charger, works on DC internally. Typical examples:

Why mains is AC but electronics run on DC

AC won for power distribution because transformers can step it up to very high voltages for long-distance lines, where lower current means less energy lost as heat, and step it back down near your home. That only works with a changing voltage. Electronics, on the other hand, need a steady supply: chips, LEDs and batteries all work on DC. So every phone charger, laptop brick and TV power supply converts AC to DC with a rectifier and regulator. The reverse job, turning battery or solar DC into household AC, is done by an inverter.

RMS and peak: what an AC reading means

Because AC is always changing, a single number has to summarize it. The standard is RMS (root mean square): the DC voltage that would deliver the same heating power to a resistor. When an outlet is described as 120V or 230V, that is the RMS value, and it is what a multimeter shows. The wave actually peaks higher. For a pure sine wave, peak = RMS × 1.414.

120 V RMS

Peaks at about 170 V, so about 340 V peak to peak.

230 V RMS

Peaks at about 325 V, so about 650 V peak to peak.

24 V RMS

A thermostat transformer peaks at about 34 V.

Those peak figures are one reason meter and probe voltage ratings matter. They also explain why an oscilloscope, which shows the whole waveform, can display a bigger number than a meter on the same outlet; our article on why oscilloscope and multimeter voltage readings differ goes into that.

Measuring each with a multimeter

Set the dial to V (a solid line over a dashed line, or VDC) for DC sources and to V~ (a tilde, or VAC) for AC. The leads go in COM and VΩ for both. With DC, polarity matters: red on the positive terminal gives a positive reading, and reversed leads give the same number with a minus sign, which is harmless; see why a multimeter shows negative voltage. With AC there is no fixed positive side, so lead orientation doesn't change the reading. For AC from motor drives, dimmers or LED drivers, whose waveforms are far from a clean sine, a True RMS meter gives an accurate figure where an average-responding meter can read noticeably off.

Mains AC needs a meter rated for it

Measuring an outlet or panel puts the full mains voltage across your meter and leads. Use a meter and probes with a CAT rating that covers the location (CAT II for plug-in loads, CAT III for fixed wiring and panels), hold the probes behind their finger guards, and connect one probe at a time, black first.

What happens on the wrong setting

A battery measured on V~ (AC) usually reads zero or close to it, because there is no alternating part for the AC circuit to respond to. An outlet measured on V (DC) shows a small number that drifts around zero instead of 120V or 230V. Neither mistake normally harms a modern meter, since both settings are voltage ranges with a high input impedance, but either one can convince you a live circuit is dead. If a reading makes no sense, the dial setting is the first thing to check, before the circuit.

The one-line version

DC holds one direction (batteries, USB, cars, electronics); AC reverses many times a second (wall outlets, transformers). Match the meter's DC or AC voltage setting to the source, and remember that an AC reading is an RMS value, so the real peak is higher.

Frequently asked questions

Is AC or DC more dangerous?

Both can kill, and the risk depends mainly on the voltage, the current path through the body and the duration of contact. Household AC is a familiar hazard because it is everywhere and the frequency used for mains can disturb the heart's rhythm; high-voltage DC, such as in EV battery packs and solar arrays, is equally serious and can sustain an arc that is hard to break. Treat any voltage above about 50V as hazardous whichever kind it is.

Can I measure AC voltage on the DC setting?

Not usefully. On the DC setting, the meter averages the input, and a symmetrical AC wave averages to roughly zero, so you see a small, wandering number instead of the real voltage. Switch to V~ for any outlet, transformer or mains-powered circuit.

Why does my 120V outlet measure 118V or 124V instead of exactly 120V?

Nominal mains voltage is a target, not a fixed value. Utilities allow a tolerance band around it, and the voltage at your outlet also drops a little under heavy household load. A reading a few volts either side of nominal is normal.

What is the difference between AC and DC current, as opposed to voltage?

Voltage is the push, current is the flow it causes. AC voltage drives AC current and DC voltage drives DC current, so the same direction rule applies. A multimeter has separate settings for each (A~ and ADC), and measuring current needs the leads in the current jacks and the meter in series with the load.

Does a USB charger output AC or DC?

A USB charger takes AC from the wall and outputs DC. Inside it, a rectifier and switching regulator turn the 120V or 230V AC input into a steady DC supply, nominally 5V on a standard port and higher on USB-C fast-charging profiles once the phone negotiates them. Measure it on the DC voltage setting with the red lead on the positive pin.
