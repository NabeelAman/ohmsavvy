Fluke 113 vs Fluke 115: Which Multimeter Is Better?

These two Flukes answer different questions. The Fluke 113 is a two-jack utility meter built around VCHEK, a low-impedance voltage test that turns into a continuity check when the circuit is dead; it has no current ranges and its resistance stops at 60 kΩ. The Fluke 115 is a general-purpose True RMS meter with 6A and 10A current, high-impedance volts, resistance to 40 MΩ and frequency. For most buyers the 115 is the better multimeter; the 113 wins only when fast live-or-dead checks on power wiring are the whole job.

What VCHEK actually does

VCHEK is the reason the 113 exists. Touch the leads to a conductor and the meter presents roughly 3 kΩ across them. If a supply is there, you get a voltage on the 6, 60 or 600 V range. If nothing is there, the same position switches to a continuity test and beeps for anything under 20 Ω. One turn of the dial answers both "is it live?" and "is this the conductor I think it is?"

The low impedance is what defeats ghost voltage. A disconnected wire run beside an energized one picks up a charge through stray capacitance, and a 10 MΩ input like the 115's barely disturbs it, so the display shows tens of volts on a wire that can't power anything. At 3 kΩ that charge is drained and the phantom collapses toward zero. Our guide to voltage readings on a disconnected circuit walks through the physics; with a 115 you would need to load the circuit by other means to get the same answer.

The cost of a 3 kΩ voltmeter

LoZ is a strength on mains wiring and a liability almost everywhere else. Because the 113 has no high-impedance volts position, every reading it takes draws current from whatever it measures. A stiff 120 V supply doesn't care. A sensor output, a thermostat signal or a resistor divider in a control board does.

Worked example: a 5 V signal behind 10 kΩ of internal resistance, read with the 113, forms a divider of 10 kΩ and 3 kΩ. The meter sees 5 × 3 ÷ 13, about 1.15 V, and you would wrongly conclude the sensor is dead. The 115's 10 MΩ DC input sees 5 × 10,000 ÷ 10,010, which rounds to 5.00 V. Fluke also notes that the 113's autorange needs at least 3 V DC to register, so small signals are out of reach anyway.

The second cost is ground-fault devices. At the nominal 3 kΩ, Ohm's law puts about 40 mA through the meter on a 120 V hot-to-ground check, while a US GFCI receptacle is designed to trip at only a few milliamps of leakage (roughly 4 to 6 mA). Fluke's 113 page warns that LoZ measurements phase-to-earth can trip a GFCI or RCD. Test hot-to-neutral on protected circuits, or expect to walk back and reset the device. See why a GFCI keeps tripping before blaming the outlet.

Current: the 115's deciding feature

The single biggest gap is amps. The 115 has a fused A jack with 6.000 A and 10.00 A ranges for AC and DC, True RMS on AC, and tolerates 20 A for 30 seconds. That covers a blower motor, a well pump control circuit, a 12 V accessory draw on a vehicle or a small appliance. The 113 can't measure any of it, and nothing in its spec tab suggests a workaround short of buying a separate clamp meter.

Where the 113 earns its place

Fluke sells the 113 as a utility meter, and its feature set suits a narrow but common workload. A lineworker, a facilities electrician tracing circuits in an older building, or a service tech verifying that a disconnect really isolated a load spends the day on two questions: is there voltage, and is this the right conductor. VCHEK answers both without a dial change, and the LoZ input means a shared conduit full of phantom readings doesn't slow the job down.

The rating matters here too. Fluke lists the 113 under IEC 61010-2-033 at CAT IV 600V, and the terminals on the listing photo are printed CAT IV 600V. That category covers the origin of the installation, such as service drops and the line side of the meter base, where the 115's CAT III 600V does not reach. If your work genuinely happens there, the 113 is the one of these two built for it.

Its capacitance range is a pleasant surprise for a meter this simple. It reads from 1,000 nF up to 9,999 µF, so motor run and start capacitors are within reach, though Fluke widens the tolerance to ±(5% + 20) on the top 9,999 µF range. The 115 starts lower, at 1 nF, which matters if you also touch small electronics capacitors. Our guide to testing a capacitor with a multimeter applies to either.

Where the sources disagree

Three facts to read carefully

First, the safety rating: Fluke's spec tab gives IEC 61010-2-033 CAT IV 600V for the 113 and the meter is printed CAT IV 600V, but one bullet on the Amazon listing says CAT III to 600 V and CAT IV to 300 V. We follow Fluke. Second, True RMS: the Amazon title and the meter's front label say True RMS, but Fluke's spec tab mentions it only in a generic footnote, so treat it as a label claim. Third, warranty: Fluke shows 3 years only for the reconditioned R-FLUKE-113 and states no term for a new unit, while the 115's 3 years is published.

Safety: LoZ checks and protected circuits

A low-impedance test draws real current, so hot-to-ground checks with the 113 can trip GFCI and RCD devices, and Fluke warns about exactly this. A reading near zero on VCHEK is still not proof of dead: confirm with the live-dead-live sequence on a known source before you touch a conductor. Keep the 115 off service drops and meter bases, where its CAT III 600V rating doesn't apply, and replace either meter's leads if the insulation is nicked. If you are not qualified to work inside panels, call a licensed electrician.

Choose the Fluke 113 if…

  • ✓Your work is utility, service or facilities wiring where most checks are live or dead
  • ✓You want one dial position that reads LoZ volts and then confirms continuity
  • ✓You value Fluke's CAT IV 600V rating and never need to measure amps

Choose the Fluke 115 if…

  • ✓You need to measure current, up to 10 A in series
  • ✓You test electronics, sensors or controls where a 3 kΩ input would drag the reading down
  • ✓You want resistance above 60 kΩ, frequency, or tighter DC voltage accuracy

The bottom line

The Fluke 115 is the better multimeter for almost everyone: it measures current, reads volts without loading the circuit, covers resistance to 40 MΩ and adds frequency. Choose the Fluke 113 only if your work is verifying power wiring, where its VCHEK low-impedance test, continuity fallback and Fluke-stated CAT IV 600V rating are exactly what you need and the lack of amps never matters.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 113 measure current?+

No. The Fluke 113 has no current ranges at all and only two input jacks, COM and V/Ω. Fluke's spec tab lists voltage through VCHEK, resistance, capacitance, continuity and diode test. If you need amps, the Fluke 115 measures 6 A and 10 A AC and DC in series, or pair the 113 with a clamp meter.

Is the Fluke 113 True RMS?+

The meter's front label and the official Amazon listing both say True RMS. Fluke's own spec tab for the 113 only mentions true-RMS meters in a general footnote about open-lead readings and never states it as a feature. The Fluke 115 is clearly specified as True RMS on AC volts and AC amps on Fluke's datasheet.

What is VCHEK on the Fluke 113?+

VCHEK is a single dial position that tests for voltage through a low input impedance of about 3 kΩ. If voltage is present it shows the reading on the 6, 60 or 600 V range; if not, it runs a continuity check that beeps below 20 Ω. The low impedance stops ghost voltage on disconnected wires from showing as a real reading.

Why does the Fluke 113 trip GFCI outlets?+

Its VCHEK input is only about 3 kΩ, so a hot-to-ground check draws roughly 40 mA on a 120 V circuit by Ohm's law. A GFCI is designed to trip at a few milliamps of leakage to ground. Fluke warns that LoZ measurements phase-to-earth can trip GFCI and RCD devices. Measure hot to neutral on protected circuits instead.

Is the Fluke 113 CAT IV rated?+

Fluke says yes: its spec tab lists IEC 61010-2-033 CAT IV 600 V, and the terminals in the listing photo are printed CAT IV 600V. One Amazon bullet instead says CAT III 600 V and CAT IV 300 V. We follow Fluke's published rating. The Fluke 115 is rated CAT III 600V, so it is not intended for service drops or meter bases.

Can I use the Fluke 113 for electronics or automotive work?+

It's a poor fit. Every voltage reading goes through the 3 kΩ VCHEK input, which pulls down weak signals such as sensor outputs, and Fluke notes the autorange needs at least 3 V DC. Resistance stops at 60 kΩ and there is no current range for drain tests. The Fluke 115's high-impedance inputs and 10 A range suit both jobs better.
