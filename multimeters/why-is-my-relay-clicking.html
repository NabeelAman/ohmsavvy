Why Is My Relay Clicking? Causes, Checks and Fixes

A relay that keeps clicking or chattering is almost always getting a coil voltage that dips below its hold-in level and then recovers, so the armature drops out and pulls in again over and over. The usual culprits are a weak battery or supply, a high-resistance ground, a thin or corroded feed, a driver transistor that is not switching fully, or a missing flyback diode that resets the controller. This guide shows why your relay is clicking and how to prove which cause it is with a multimeter, using a car relay and a 5 V Arduino relay module as worked examples.

Before you probe a chattering circuit

  • A car battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short. Take off rings and watches, and keep the meter probe tips away from anything that could bridge a fused feed to the body.
  • Many relay modules switch 120 V or 230 V loads. Unplug the mains side before you touch the board, and never probe the contact side of a mains relay with a meter rated below CAT II for that voltage.
  • Chattering contacts get hot. If a relay or its socket is discolored, smells burnt or has melted plastic, stop and replace the relay and socket. For building wiring, call a licensed electrician.

How relay chatter happens

A relay coil is an electromagnet. It needs a certain voltage to pull the armature in (the pick-up or must-operate voltage) and much less to keep it there. If the voltage at the coil falls below the drop-out level, the spring wins and the contacts open. On their own, slow voltage sags just make a relay hesitate. Chatter happens when the act of switching causes the sag: the contacts close, the load pulls current from the same supply, the voltage at the coil collapses, the relay releases, the load current stops, the voltage recovers and the cycle repeats. That loop can run several times a second, which is the buzzing click you hear.

In a car, the classic case is a starter solenoid or a fan relay on a battery that is nearly flat. In electronics, it is a 5 V relay module powered from a USB port or a small regulator, where the coil current and the load surge are enough to reset the microcontroller that drives the relay. Same physics, different supply.

1Measure the coil voltage at the relay while it clicks

Measure at the relay, not at the battery or the supply. Set the meter to DC volts and back-probe the two coil terminals with the relay in place: pins 85 and 86 on a standard automotive relay, or the coil pins on a module (often next to the transistor and diode). A meter display updates a few times per second, so a chattering coil reads as an unstable number. Watch for the lowest values. A bar graph or MIN/MAX recording catches the dips far better than the digits.

If the coil holds within about 10% of its rated voltage the whole time but still chatters, suspect the relay itself and bench test it. If the coil voltage dips, the relay is a victim, and the next three steps find where the volts are going.

2Check the battery or supply under load

On a car, measure across the battery posts (on the lead posts themselves, not the clamps) with everything off. About 12.6 V is a full lead-acid battery, about 12.4 V is roughly 75% and 12.0 V is about half. Then have someone operate the circuit that chatters. A healthy battery should hold above roughly 10 V during cranking and barely move for a fan or lamp load. If it collapses toward 6 to 8 V, the battery cannot support the relay and the load at the same time. Charge it and load-test it with our 12 V battery load test. A starter that just clicks has its own guide: why a car starter clicks but won't start.

On a 5 V relay module, put the probes on the module's VCC and GND pins and trigger the relay. A USB port or a small linear regulator can drop several hundred millivolts when a coil pulling 70 to 90 mA switches on alongside the board's own load. Below about 4.5 V many 5 V boards start to misbehave. Many modules have a JD-VCC jumper: remove it and feed the coil side from its own 5 V supply, sharing only ground.

3Measure the voltage drop on the ground side

A bad ground does not show up as an open circuit on the ohms range. It shows up as volts lost while current flows. With the relay energized, put the black probe on the battery negative post and the red probe on the relay's ground pin (pin 85 on most automotive relays, or the emitter side of the driver on a module). This reads only what the ground path is wasting. Under about 0.1 V is good; 0.2 to 0.3 V is worth cleaning; above about 0.5 V is a fault. Then repeat the measurement on the feed side, from battery positive to the coil's positive pin.

Shared grounds are the sneaky case. If the relay coil and a 20 A cooling fan return through the same ring terminal with 25 mΩ of corrosion, the fan alone drops 20 A × 0.025 Ω = 0.5 V across it, and the relay coil sees that loss too. That is why a relay can behave on the bench and chatter in the car. Our car voltage-drop guide covers the method on other circuits.

4Check the driver transistor is switching fully

Inside an ECU, body module or relay board, a transistor switches the coil to ground. If it is not fully on, it acts as a resistor in series with the coil, and the coil gets whatever is left over. With the relay commanded on, measure DC volts from collector to emitter (or drain to source on a MOSFET). A saturated small-signal BJT typically reads about 0.1 to 0.3 V. Several volts means it is underdriven or failing.

Here is the arithmetic for a common homemade mistake. A 12 V coil of 80 Ω needs 12 ÷ 80 = 150 mA. To saturate a small NPN transistor you want base current around one tenth of that, about 15 mA. From a 3.3 V microcontroller pin through a 10 kΩ base resistor, the base gets (3.3 − 0.7) ÷ 10,000 = 0.26 mA. That is far too little. The transistor sits partly on, the coil hums and the relay may click in and out as the voltage wanders. Drop the base resistor to around 220 to 470 Ω (check the pin's current limit) or use a logic-level MOSFET. To test the transistor itself, see how to test a transistor in circuit.

5Check for a flyback diode across the coil

When a coil switches off, its magnetic field collapses and the coil tries to keep current flowing. With nothing to absorb it, that produces a spike of tens or even hundreds of volts at the driver. The spike can kill the transistor over time, and in the meantime it couples into the logic supply and resets the microcontroller. A reset turns the relay off, the board reboots, the relay turns back on, and you get a slow, regular clicking that looks like a software bug.

Look before you measure. On a relay module the diode is usually a small black part right beside the relay, often marked D1, with its stripe toward the coil's positive pin. Automotive relays with suppression print a coil symbol with a diode or resistor on the case; diode types must be wired with the correct polarity on 85 and 86. Measuring across the coil in place will not prove the diode is there, because the coil's 50 to 90 Ω shunts it in both directions. To test the diode itself, power off, lift one leg and use diode test: a good one reads about 0.5 to 0.7 V one way and OL the other. If there is no suppression at all, fit a 1N4007-class diode across the coil with its stripe to the positive side.

Find the lost volts

A clicking relay is nearly always a voltage problem, and voltage problems only show up while current flows. Measure the coil, the supply, the ground drop and the driver drop with the circuit working. The step that shows the missing volts is the one to fix. Swapping relays first just hides the clue.

Which meter to use for relay chatter

Any meter with DC volts and diode test finds most relay faults. What helps on chatter is a display that follows fast changes, and enough resolution to see a 0.1 V ground drop clearly. For a coil that clicks too quickly for any meter, a scope shows each drop-out directly. For the basics of each range, see how to use a multimeter.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my car relay click when the engine is off?

Some modules wake up and run fuel pump priming, fan run-on or door lock routines after the key is off, so a single click can be normal. Repeated clicking with the engine off usually means the battery is low enough that the relay drops out whenever something else on the same circuit draws current. Check resting battery voltage first: about 12.6 V is full and 12.0 V is roughly half charged.

Can a bad relay cause chattering by itself?

Yes, but it is less common than a supply problem. A coil with a broken turn, a cracked solder joint on the relay pins, or a weak return spring can make a relay drop out early. Swap in a known-good relay of the same part number. If the new one chatters too, the fault is in the feed, ground or driver, not the relay.

Why does my Arduino relay module click on and off by itself?

Most often the board is browning out. The coil and the load draw a surge, the 5 V rail sags, the microcontroller resets, the output pin goes low and the relay releases. The board reboots and switches it on again. Power the relay coils from a separate 5 V supply, add a bulk capacitor near the board and make sure the coil has a flyback diode.

Is a clicking relay dangerous?

It can be. Each chatter cycle makes and breaks the load current, which arcs and pits the contacts, so they run hot and may weld shut. A motor or compressor on chattering contacts sees repeated inrush current. On mains-switching relays the heat can damage the socket or board. Switch the circuit off and fix the cause rather than letting it chatter.

What voltage does a 12 V relay need to stay on?

Typical datasheets say a 12 V relay must pull in at no more than about 70 to 75% of rated voltage, roughly 8.4 to 9 V, and will release somewhere above about 10% of rated voltage, around 1.2 V. Once pulled in, the relay holds at well under its pull-in voltage. Your relay's own datasheet gives the exact figures for its coil.

Will a capacitor stop my relay from chattering?

Sometimes, as a stopgap. A capacitor across the supply near a relay module can ride through short dips, and it helps a microcontroller survive switching surges. It will not fix a weak battery, a corroded ground or an underdriven transistor, because the coil still sees low voltage once the capacitor drains. Find and repair the real voltage loss first.
