HOW-TO GUIDE

Leaded vs. Lead-Free Solder

Leaded tin-lead solder melts at a single sharp point around 183°C and flows easily, wetting joints with little effort. Lead-free alloys, most commonly tin-silver-copper blends like SAC305, melt roughly 34–37°C higher, around 217–220°C, and tend to need more flux and a hotter iron to wet a joint as cleanly. Leaded solder remains legal and common for hobby and repair work; lead-free is required by regulation for most new commercially sold consumer electronics in several major markets, which is why it dominates factory-made boards even though hobbyists are generally still free to use either.

Lead safety, briefly

  • Solder in a ventilated area and avoid breathing flux fumes directly, regardless of alloy.
  • Wash your hands after handling leaded solder, and don't eat or drink while working with it.
  • Store leaded solder out of reach of children, and keep it separate from food-contact items.

The melting-point gap

63/37 tin-lead solder is the eutectic ratio for that alloy system, meaning it transitions directly from solid to liquid at one specific temperature with no soft in-between phase: about 183°C. Lead-free alloys swap out the lead for other metals — most commonly silver and copper in SAC305 (roughly 96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper) — which raises the melting point substantially, to roughly 217–220°C depending on the exact alloy and source.

That roughly 35°C gap is the source of nearly every practical difference between the two: how hot the iron needs to be set, how long a joint takes to flow, and how much margin there is before you're working right at the edge of what a lower-wattage or poorly calibrated iron can sustain.

Why the gap matters at the iron tip

An iron set to melt lead-free solder is working closer to its own maximum output on many consumer irons, which leaves less thermal reserve if the joint pulls heat away quickly — the same recovery problem covered in what wattage soldering iron do I need. Lead-free alloys are also generally reported to wet less readily and oxidize a tip faster at sustained high temperature, which is why lead-free work tends to use more flux and benefits from more frequent tip cleaning. None of this makes lead-free solder harder to use well, but it does mean a setpoint and technique tuned for 183°C leaded solder usually needs adjusting, not just reusing as-is.

Why lead-free became standard

Lead-free solder became the default in mass-produced consumer electronics largely because of environmental and health regulations restricting lead in new commercial products sold in several major markets, which pushed manufacturers toward lead-free assembly at scale. Those regulations generally target new products placed on the commercial market, not individual hobbyists, repair technicians, or existing equipment — which is why leaded solder remains widely available and commonly used for repair and hobby work even as factory-made boards have shifted to lead-free.

Neither alloy is wrong for hobby work

Where regulation doesn't dictate the choice, leaded solder's lower temperature and easier wetting make it a reasonable default for learning and repair, while lead-free is the right call for anything built to be sold as new.

Mixing leaded and lead-free

For repair work, adding leaded solder to an existing lead-free joint, or the reverse, is common practice and generally not a problem electrically or mechanically for typical hobby and repair applications. Where it does matter is certification: a board or assembly that must stay officially lead-free compliant for regulatory reasons shouldn't have leaded solder introduced into it, which is a consideration for manufacturers and certified repair shops rather than individual hobbyists reworking their own projects.

Irons that handle both

Any iron with a wide enough adjustable temperature range and enough wattage in reserve handles both alloy types without issue — the key spec to check is the top of its temperature range relative to lead-free's higher melting point, with margin left over for real joints.

Frequently asked questions

Is leaded solder illegal to use?

For hobby, repair, and most non-commercial use, no — restrictions on leaded solder generally target new commercially manufactured consumer products, not individual repair or hobby work. Still, handle any leaded solder with basic precautions: wash your hands after use, solder in a ventilated area, and avoid eating or drinking while working with it.

Why does lead-free solder look duller or grainier than leaded joints?

Common lead-free alloys solidify differently than eutectic tin-lead solder, often producing a slightly duller, more matte or grainy-looking joint even when it's perfectly sound. This is a known visual characteristic of the alloy, not necessarily a sign of a bad joint — judge a lead-free joint by its shape and coverage, not by comparing its shine to a leaded one.

Can I use leaded solder on a board that was originally soldered with lead-free?

Yes, for repair work this is common practice and generally fine electrically. The two alloys can mix at the joint; some assemblers avoid mixing on products that must stay certified lead-free for regulatory reasons, which matters for manufacturers more than for individual repairs.

Does lead-free solder need a different soldering iron?

Not a different iron, but a hotter one if yours can't reach the higher range lead-free solder needs — a temperature-controlled iron that only goes up to roughly 350°C may struggle with a 217-220°C-melting alloy once you account for real-world heat loss at the joint. Stations rated well above that, like the Hakko FX-888DX's full range, cover both alloy types comfortably.

Why does lead-free solder sometimes form a spiky or crystalline point?

This is sometimes called a disturbed joint or a cold-joint-like effect, and it happens when the joint is moved while the alloy is still solidifying — lead-free alloys can be more prone to this visually. Let the joint sit undisturbed until it has fully cooled and solidified, and reflow it if the surface looks irregular rather than smooth.

Is lead-free solder more prone to tin whiskers?

Tin whiskers — tiny metallic filaments that can grow from pure or near-pure tin finishes over time — are a recognized concern specifically with some lead-free finishes, which is why reliability-critical and aerospace applications often require leaded solder by exception even where lead-free is otherwise standard. For typical hobby and repair work, this is a minor consideration rather than a practical problem.
