Fluke 114 vs Fluke 115: Key Differences Explained

The Fluke 114 and Fluke 115 share a case, display, battery, 3-year warranty and CAT III 600V rating, and their voltage and resistance accuracy is identical. The key differences run in opposite directions. The 114 adds AutoVolt, a low-impedance (LoZ) position that picks AC or DC by itself and suppresses ghost voltage, but it measures no current, capacitance or frequency. The 115 adds 6A and 10A current, capacitance, frequency and a diode test, but has no LoZ. Choose by what you test, not by model number.

Fluke 114 vs 115 spec by spec

Fluke built the 110 Series on one platform, then gave each model a different set of dial positions. The 114 and 115 sit on opposite sides of that split: one leans toward verifying power wiring, the other toward measuring loads and components. The function count above treats AC volts, DC volts, AutoVolt, resistance and continuity as the 114's five, and AC volts, DC volts, current, resistance, continuity, capacitance, frequency and diode as the 115's eight. The point isn't the number but which ones: the 115's extra four are all about loads and components, the 114's one unique entry is about trusting a voltage reading.

Why the 114 can't measure amps

Measuring current with a multimeter means breaking the circuit and routing the load through the meter. That needs a dedicated, fused current input. The 115 has one, its A jack, plus 6.000 A and 10.00 A ranges for AC and DC. The 114's front panel has only COM and a volts/ohms jack, so there is physically nowhere for load current to go.

That limit is easy to work around for an electrician who carries a clamp meter, which is the usual tool for branch-circuit current anyway. It is a real gap for anyone who wants one meter for a blower motor, a 12 V accessory or a small appliance. Remember that the 115's current input also has a ceiling: 10.00 A continuous, with 20 A allowed for 30 seconds, and no mA or µA range.

What AutoVolt gives the 114

AutoVolt is one dial position that does two things at once. It drops the input to a low impedance, so a phantom voltage induced onto a dead conductor collapses instead of reading as tens of volts. And it decides AC or DC from whatever is on the leads, so you don't have to. Fluke's spec tab gives it a single 600.0 V range at ±(2.0% + 3) from DC to 500 Hz.

Worked from Fluke's figures, the convenience costs precision. On a 240 V reading, the 114's normal V AC position allows 1.0% of 240 (2.4 V) plus 3 counts of 0.1 V, about ±2.7 V. AutoVolt allows 2.0% of 240 (4.8 V) plus 0.3 V, about ±5.1 V. That is fine for live-or-dead, and the 114 still has its normal positions for a reading you will write down. The 115 owner gets the tighter figure every time but has to rely on other methods, explained in our guide to measuring voltage on a floating circuit, to separate a ghost from a real feed.

Capacitance, frequency and diodes on the 115

The three remaining 115 functions matter more to service technicians than to installers. Capacitance from 1 nF to 9,999 µF covers motor run and start capacitors on air handlers, condensers, well pumps and ceiling fans; our guide to testing a fan motor capacitor shows the method. Frequency up to 50 kHz confirms a generator's output or a drive's supply side. The diode test checks rectifiers and the diodes inside power supplies and alternators. None of the three appears in the 114's spec table.

Where Fluke's 114 page contradicts itself

The "current" wording on the 114

The overview on Fluke's 114 product page describes True RMS "voltage and current" readings, but the specification table on the same page lists no current ranges, and the meter in the Amazon listing photo has only two jacks. We treat the 114 as having no current function. The same page also carries a temperature footnote with no temperature function in the table, which looks like leftover text. Fluke lists the 114 at 550 g; the Amazon listing says 0.51 kg.

The Fluke 115 carries our Hands-on Tested badge; the 114 doesn't, so its details here come from Fluke and the official listing. Read the Fluke 115 review for more on the general-purpose model.

Safety: match the meter to the job

Neither meter is rated CAT IV, so keep both off service drops and meter bases. Never try to measure current on a 114 by putting its leads across a circuit: it has no current input. On a 115, set the dial and move the red lead to the A jack before connecting in series, and never put the leads across a voltage source while they are in the A jack. Prove dead with a live-dead-live test on a known source before touching a conductor. Panel work belongs with a licensed electrician.

Choose the Fluke 114 if…

  • ✓Your job is checking voltage on building wiring and proving circuits dead
  • ✓You want a LoZ input so phantom voltage on shared runs doesn't fool you
  • ✓You never measure amps or capacitors with a handheld meter

Choose the Fluke 115 if…

  • ✓You need to measure current up to 10 A on motors, heaters or vehicle circuits
  • ✓You check motor run capacitors or need a frequency reading
  • ✓You work on electronics and controls where a high-impedance input is the right choice

Neither if…

You need AutoVolt's ghost-voltage filtering and the 115's current, capacitance, frequency and diode functions together — the Fluke 117 covers both, neither of these two does.

The bottom line

Buy the Fluke 114 if your handheld meter only ever checks voltage, continuity and resistance on building wiring: AutoVolt's low-impedance input makes those checks quicker and harder to fool. Buy the Fluke 115 if you also measure current, capacitors, frequency or diodes. If you need AutoVolt and the 115's extras together, look at the Fluke 117.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Fluke 114 measure current?+

Not according to Fluke's own specification table, which lists no current ranges, and the meter has only COM and volts/ohms jacks. The overview text on Fluke's 114 page mentions voltage and current readings, which contradicts the table. We go by the table and the hardware. For current, the Fluke 115 offers 6 A and 10 A ranges for AC and DC.

Does the Fluke 115 have LoZ or AutoVolt?+

No. The 115's volts positions are all high impedance, over 10 MΩ for DC and over 5 MΩ for AC. AutoVolt with its low-impedance input is found on the 114, 116 and 117 in the 110 Series. To check a suspected ghost voltage with a 115, you need to load the conductor some other way.

Are the Fluke 114 and 115 equally accurate?+

Yes, wherever they share a function. Fluke specifies both at ±(0.5% + 2) for DC volts, ±(1.0% + 3) for True RMS AC volts from 45 to 500 Hz, and ±(0.9% + 1) for resistance up to 6 MΩ. The only extra accuracy line on the 114 is AutoVolt, which is looser at ±(2.0% + 3).

Can the Fluke 114 test capacitors?+

No. Capacitance isn't listed in the 114's specifications. If you test motor run or start capacitors on HVAC units, pumps or fans, the Fluke 115 measures from 1 nF to 9,999 µF at ±(1.9% + 2). The 114 can only check that a capacitor isn't shorted using resistance, which isn't a real capacitance test.

Which is better for a homeowner, the Fluke 114 or 115?+

It depends on the projects. For checking outlets, switches and whether a breaker killed a circuit, the 114's simple dial and LoZ input fit well. For appliance repair, where you test capacitors, heating elements and motor current, the 115 covers more. Both are CAT III 600V with the same case and 3-year warranty.

Is the Fluke 114 True RMS?+

Yes. Fluke specifies True RMS on the 114's AC volts and AC millivolts ranges, at ±(1.0% + 3) from 45 to 500 Hz. That keeps readings trustworthy on circuits with LED drivers, dimmers or other non-sinusoidal loads. The 115 is also True RMS, on AC volts and AC amps.
