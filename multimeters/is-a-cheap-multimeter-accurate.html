Is a Cheap Multimeter Accurate?

A cheap multimeter is usually accurate enough for the number it displays — most budget meters today are still digitally accurate within a percent or two on basic DC voltage. The real gap between cheap and premium meters is resolution (how many digits of change it can actually show you) and, more importantly, whether the safety rating printed on the case or the Amazon listing actually matches what the meter's own manual states. That second gap is the one worth checking before you trust a budget meter near mains voltage.

At the top end, a meter like the Brymen BM786 publishes a precise 0.025% basic DC accuracy figure. Budget meters often simply don't publish one at all. See what CAT rating you actually need for the safety side of this question.

The short answer

Yes, with two real caveats. First, the cheapest meters sometimes don't publish an accuracy percentage at all, just a count resolution, which makes it hard to know how much to trust a borderline reading. Second, and more importantly, a cheap meter's printed safety (CAT) rating on its case or Amazon listing doesn't always match what its own manual actually states — and the manual is the figure that counts.

What an accuracy spec actually means

A multimeter's accuracy is usually written as a percentage of the reading plus a number of "digits" (the smallest increment the display can show), for example ±(0.05% + 5 digits). On a 10.000 V reading, that's ±(0.005 V + 0.005 V) = ±0.010 V, so the true value is somewhere between 9.990 V and 10.010 V. The count (2,000, 6,000, 20,000, 60,000 and so on) is how many distinct values the display can show on its most sensitive range — a higher count means finer resolution, independent of the accuracy percentage itself.

Resolution and accuracy are two different numbers

A high-count meter can display more decimal places, but that doesn't automatically mean it's more accurate — a 60,000-count meter with a loose tolerance can show you a precise-looking number that's still wrong by more than a 2,000-count meter with a tight tolerance. Check both figures, not just the count.

When cheap is fine, and when it isn't

A basic meter is genuinely fine for checking batteries, continuity, and whether an outlet has power. Step up in both accuracy and safety-rating confidence once you're working with variable-speed loads, anything you'd call a precision reading, or any wiring where a wrong CAT assumption has real consequences.

Frequently asked questions

Can I trust a $15 multimeter's reading?

For basic DC voltage and continuity checks, generally yes — the underlying analog-to-digital conversion in even cheap meters is usually sound. What you often lose at the bottom of the price range is published accuracy specs at all (some budget meters state only a count resolution, not a tolerance percentage), plus features like True RMS that matter on non-sinusoidal AC signals.

Why do two different multimeters give slightly different readings on the same circuit?

Every meter has a tolerance band, commonly expressed as a percentage plus a number of counts (for example ±(0.05% + 5 digits)). Two meters within their own tolerance can legitimately disagree by a small amount. If the disagreement is large, suspect a wiring or loading issue rather than the meters themselves — see our guide on multimeter input impedance.

Is the CAT safety rating on a cheap meter's case always accurate?

Not always, and this is the more important accuracy question for a budget meter. We've found several verified picks where the case or Amazon listing states a higher CAT rating than the meter's own printed manual, which is the authoritative source. Always check the manual, not just the case silkscreen or the listing title.

Does a higher price always mean better accuracy?

Not strictly, but it correlates with published accuracy specs, resolution (count), and True RMS support. A meter that costs more because of extra safety certification or logging features isn't necessarily more accurate on basic DC volts than a mid-tier meter, but it's far more likely to state its accuracy precisely rather than leaving you to guess.

What's more important than accuracy for a cheap multimeter?

Safety rating honesty. A meter that's off by 0.1% on a voltage reading is a minor inconvenience. A meter whose CAT rating is inflated on its listing but not backed by its own manual is a real safety question if you're working anywhere near higher-energy circuits like a service panel.
