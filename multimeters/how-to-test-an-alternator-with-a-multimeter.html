How to Test an Alternator With a Multimeter

With the engine running, a healthy alternator raises the battery voltage above its resting level and holds it steady as electrical loads switch on and off. Measuring that charging voltage at idle, then again with headlights and blower running, is the quickest way to tell a weak or failed alternator from a battery that just can't hold a charge. If the dash charge light itself stays on, see why the battery light stays on while driving; if you suspect one bad diode rather than the whole unit, see how to check a car alternator diode.

Keep hands and leads clear of the engine while it runs

  • Keep meter leads, hands, and clothing clear of the fan, belt, and pulleys during a running test.
  • Set the parking brake and keep the transmission in park or neutral before testing with the engine running.
  • Work in a ventilated area; don't run the engine in an enclosed space.

What you're actually measuring

Inside the alternator, the belt spins a magnetized rotor inside three sets of stator windings, which produce three-phase AC. A bank of six diodes, the rectifier, turns that into DC, and the voltage regulator varies the rotor's field current to hold the output near its set point. The output leaves through the heavy B+ wire to the battery. Every test on this page is taken at the battery terminals, so you're seeing the end result of the whole chain: belt, windings, diodes, regulator and the cable between them.

1Check resting voltage first

With the engine off, measure voltage across the battery terminals. A healthy, charged battery rests around 12.6-12.8 V. This baseline matters because charging voltage is a rise above resting voltage, not an absolute number on its own — knowing where you started helps interpret the running reading.

2Measure charging voltage at idle

Start the engine and let it idle with no major accessories running. Measure voltage across the battery terminals again; it should read noticeably higher than the resting voltage, typically in the range your service manual specifies for your vehicle.

3Measure charging voltage under load

With the engine still running, switch on the headlights, blower fan on high, and rear defroster if fitted, then read the voltage again. A healthy alternator holds voltage reasonably steady; a struggling one sags noticeably and may not recover even if you raise the engine speed slightly.

ObservationLikely meaning
Rises well above resting, holds steady under loadCharging system working normally
Rises only slightly above resting voltageWeak alternator output or a slipping/worn belt
No rise above resting voltage at allAlternator not charging — check belt, connections, and the unit itself
Voltage far too high (overcharging)Faulty voltage regulator — can damage the battery and electronics if left unaddressed

4Check for AC ripple (diode fault)

With the engine running, switch the meter to AC volts and measure across the same battery terminals. A healthy system shows very little AC voltage present, since the alternator's diodes should fully rectify the output to DC; a meaningfully higher AC reading suggests one or more failed diodes.

Clean DC voltage, but still a no-charge condition?

If charging voltage looks right at the battery but accessories or the battery itself still misbehave, check the main charge wire and its connections for corrosion or looseness before condemning the alternator itself.

Interpreting the results, put together

Normal charging voltage that holds under load and shows little AC ripple means the alternator is healthy — look elsewhere for the symptom that sent you testing. Low or absent charging voltage points to the alternator, belt, or its main connections. Clean DC voltage with the charge light still on separately suggests a wiring or dash-circuit issue rather than the alternator itself.

The chart puts the typical reference points for a 12V car side by side. They're widely quoted general figures, not a spec for any particular vehicle; your service manual's numbers take priority, and many newer cars with smart charging deliberately let voltage drift lower at cruise.

Fully charged battery, engine off
~12.6–12.8V
Charging at idle, accessories off
~13.8–14.4V
Under load: lights, blower, defrost
a few tenths lower
Overcharging, suspect the regulator
above ~15V

A worked example with illustrative numbers: the battery rests at 12.6V, rises to 14.2V at idle, and drops to 13.9V with the headlights, blower and defroster on. That's a 1.6V rise from rest and only a 0.3V sag under load, which is a healthy charging system. If the same car read 12.9V at idle and 12.3V under load, the battery would be discharging while the engine runs, and the next stop is the belt tension and the B+ connections, then the alternator itself.

Rule out the charge cable before replacing the alternator

A corroded B+ cable or ground strap can make a good alternator look weak. With the engine running and loads on, put one probe on the alternator's output stud and the other on the battery positive post: you're reading the voltage lost in the cable. Then repeat between the alternator case and the battery negative post. Both readings should be small, and a common guideline is a few tenths of a volt at most. A larger reading on either side means the wiring is the fault, and our voltage drop guide shows the same technique on other circuits.

Frequently asked questions

What's a normal alternator charging voltage?

Most 12V automotive charging systems run around 13.8-14.4 V at idle with no major accessories on, though the exact figure varies by vehicle and can shift with temperature on vehicles with temperature-compensated charging. Check your service manual's specification rather than relying on a single universal number.

Why does charging voltage drop when I turn on the headlights?

A small drop under additional load is normal and expected, since the alternator's output has a finite limit it's sharing across more demand. A drop of a volt or more, or voltage that sags and doesn't recover once accessories are loaded, suggests the alternator is struggling to keep up even at normal engine speeds.

Can a good alternator still have a parasitic drain problem?

Yes — alternator output and parasitic drain are two different circuits (one active while running, one with everything off). A good charging-voltage test doesn't rule out a drain issue; if the battery goes flat only while parked, see how to measure parasitic battery drain instead.

What does AC voltage on the DC terminals mean?

A rectified alternator output should be essentially pure DC; a meaningful AC voltage riding on top of it (often checked on the meter's AC range at the same terminals) points to one or more failed rectifier diodes letting unfiltered AC ripple through, which can also cause electronic accessories to misbehave even while charging voltage looks acceptable.

Does engine rpm affect the charging voltage reading?

Yes. Alternator output generally increases with engine speed up to a point, so a reading taken right at idle can look lower than one taken at a fast idle or moderate rpm. Test at both idle and a slightly elevated rpm (per your service manual's test procedure) for a fuller picture.
