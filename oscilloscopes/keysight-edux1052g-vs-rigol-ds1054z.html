Keysight EDUX1052G vs Rigol DS1054Z for Beginners

Keysight EDUX1052G vs Rigol DS1054Z sets two 50MHz bench scopes with very different priorities against each other. Keysight's education model gives you two channels, built-in training signals, a 20MHz function generator and a 100,000 wfm/s update rate. The Rigol, which carries our Hands-on Tested badge, gives you four channels and far deeper memory, but no generator.

Key differences

  • →Channels: the EDUX1052G has two analog inputs, the DS1054Z has four. That is the single biggest practical gap between them.
  • →Only the Keysight has a built-in generator: a 20MHz WaveGen that also drives its Bode plot (frequency-response) mode. The DS1054Z has none; Rigol reserves the generator for its -S models.
  • →Keysight builds training signals into the EDUX1052G, so a beginner can practice triggering and measuring without a circuit. These are not a feature Rigol lists for the DS1054Z.
  • →Update rate is 100,000 wfm/s on the Keysight against up to 30,000 wfm/s on the Rigol, so the Keysight spends less time blind between captures.
  • →Memory runs the other way: 200 kpts on the Keysight against 12 or 24 Mpts on the Rigol, depending on which Rigol document you read.
  • →The Keysight keeps 1 GSa/s with both channels on; the Rigol drops to 500 MSa/s with two channels and 250 MSa/s with three or four.
  • →Rigol's datasheet lists SPI and parallel decoding as well as UART and I2C (its product page calls decoding an option); Keysight lists I2C and UART/RS-232 as standard.

Spec by spec

Both scopes are rated at 50MHz and both peak at 1 GSa/s, so the headline numbers suggest twins. The charts show where they really part company: how the sample rate holds up as channels are added, how many waveforms reach the screen each second, and how much memory each one has to fill.

Memory decides how long a capture can last at full speed: divide the points by the sample rate. At 1 GSa/s, 200 kpts fills in 200 µs, while 12 Mpts lasts 12 ms. Ask either scope for a longer window and it lowers the sample rate to fit, which the Keysight has to do sixty times sooner. Bandwidth, by contrast, is a tie: Keysight quotes a calculated 7 ns rise time, and the usual 0.35 ÷ bandwidth approximation gives about the same for the Rigol. Our explainers on oscilloscope bandwidth and what GSa/s means cover the theory behind these figures.

Keysight EDUX1052G vs Rigol DS1054Z for beginners

The awkward truth about a first oscilloscope is that it arrives before you have anything interesting to look at. The EDUX1052G is designed around that problem. Keysight builds training signals into the scope, so a newcomer can connect a probe and practice the core routine of scaling, triggering and measuring on known waveforms straight away. Add the 20MHz WaveGen and you also have a source: feed a sine wave into an RC filter or a transistor amplifier, probe the input on channel 1 and the output on channel 2, and the Bode plot mode will sweep the generator and chart gain against frequency for you. For someone studying alone rather than in a lab with a bench generator beside every scope, that is a lot of teaching material in one box.

The DS1054Z assumes you bring the signals. Without a generator, your first waveforms will come from an Arduino pin, a 555 timer or whatever circuit you are building, which is fine for hobbyists who already have projects on the go but slower for someone starting from zero. What it gives back is breadth. Four channels let you watch a microcontroller talk to a sensor over SPI with every line visible, or compare the four coil signals of a stepper driver, and its memory lets a long burst be recorded at a high sample rate and then zoomed into afterwards. It carries our Hands-on Tested badge, and our DS1054Z review covers it in more depth.

So the choice for a beginner comes down to what you lack today. If you have no signal source and want guided practice, the Keysight removes that obstacle. If you already have circuits to debug and expect them to involve more than two lines at once, the Rigol is the one you will not outgrow on channel count. Neither has digital channels, and neither offers more than 50MHz, so a jump to faster logic or RF work means a different scope whichever you pick.

The wiring is the whole lesson in one picture. Three leads leave the Keysight and one circuit sits in the middle, so the ratio of output to input at each frequency becomes a curve without any arithmetic on your part. Rebuild the same test on the DS1054Z and a second instrument joins the bench: an external generator, whose dial you turn and whose readings you note down step by step before plotting them yourself.

Why update rate matters when you are learning

A digital scope captures a waveform, processes it, draws it and only then captures again; anything that happens in that gap is never seen. At 100,000 wfm/s the EDUX1052G re-arms more than three times as often as the DS1054Z at its 30,000 wfm/s maximum, so an occasional runt pulse or glitch is more likely to flash onto the Keysight's screen. Both are maker maximums that fall at many timebase settings. Using persistence to find intermittent glitches makes the most of whichever rate you have.

By the 3–5x rule, a 50MHz scope shows signals up to roughly 10–17MHz with their shape intact. That covers Arduino PWM, servo pulses, audio, I2C, UART and most SPI at hobby clock rates on either scope. The WaveGen's own limits matter too: Keysight rates sine output to 20MHz but square and pulse only to 10MHz and ramps to 200kHz, which is plenty for filter and amplifier experiments. To put either scope to work, start with setting up a trigger and measuring an amplifier's bandwidth; Keysight also notes that the EDUX1052G has no segmented memory, so the segmented-memory technique is not available on it.

Mains, earth and the generator output

Both scopes run from the mains, so each probe's ground clip is tied to mains earth through the power cord. Clipping it to a live conductor or the primary side of a power supply creates a short through the scope. Keysight also states that the WaveGen output is not isolated, so its ground is the same earth: never connect it to a circuit whose reference is not earth-safe. Beginners should stay with battery, USB and low-voltage bench supplies, and use a differential probe if mains ever has to be measured.

The bottom line

Pick the Keysight EDUX1052G if you are learning from scratch and want the scope to supply its own practice signals, a 20MHz generator and Bode plots, with the faster 100,000 wfm/s update rate as a bonus. Pick the Rigol DS1054Z if your projects already make signals and you need more of them on screen: four channels and 12 or 24 Mpts of memory serve bus debugging and long captures that two channels and 200 kpts cannot.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Keysight EDUX1052G or the Rigol DS1054Z better for a complete beginner?+

It depends on whether you already have signals to look at. The EDUX1052G includes training signals and a 20MHz generator, so you can practice from the first day without building anything. The DS1054Z has no generator but offers four channels and much deeper memory, which matter more once you are debugging real circuits with several lines.

What are the training signals on the Keysight EDUX1052G?+

They are practice waveforms that Keysight builds into the EDUX1052G so a student can learn scaling, triggering and measurement without an external source. They suit self-study and classroom exercises. The DS1054Z doesn't list an equivalent, so its owners usually start with a microcontroller pin, a 555 timer or a separate function generator.

Does the Rigol DS1054Z have a built-in signal generator?+

No. Rigol's DS1000Z datasheet fits a generator only to the -S models, such as the DS1104Z-S Plus. The Keysight EDUX1052G includes a 20MHz WaveGen as standard, rated for sine waves up to 20MHz and square or pulse waves up to 10MHz, with up to 20 Vpp into a high-impedance load.

Is 200 kpts of memory enough on a beginner oscilloscope?+

For repetitive signals viewed a screen at a time, usually yes. It becomes a limit when you need a long capture at high resolution: at 1 GSa/s, 200 kpts covers only 200 µs before the EDUX1052G must lower its sample rate. The DS1054Z's 12 or 24 Mpts (Rigol's documents disagree) keeps full speed for far longer.

Has OhmSavvy tested either of these scopes?+

The Rigol DS1054Z carries our Hands-on Tested badge. The Keysight EDUX1052G assessment is desk research based on Keysight's InfiniiVision 1000 X-Series data sheet and product page, so every figure quoted for it here comes from Keysight's published specifications.
