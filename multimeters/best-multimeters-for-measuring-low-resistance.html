Why low resistance is hard to measure

A multimeter measures resistance by pushing a small current through the part and reading the voltage it develops. At a kilohm that works beautifully. At a tenth of an ohm the voltage is tiny, the test leads and probe contacts add as much resistance as the part itself, and the meter's fixed error of 10 or 20 digits can swamp the reading. Those limits matter when you're testing a shunt resistor, checking relay contact resistance or looking for a shorted motor winding.

We ranked these ten on the finest resolution of the lowest resistance range, the published accuracy on that range, whether the meter can zero out its leads, and whether it measures in 2-wire or 4-wire (Kelvin) mode. Two of the ten, the UNI-T UT620A and UT620C+, aren't multimeters at all: they are dedicated micro-ohm meters, included because no handheld multimeter reaches their resolution. For the basics of the ohms function, read measuring resistance with a digital multimeter; for tracing a board fault, see checking a PCB for a short circuit.

De-energize before you measure ohms

Resistance readings are only valid on a dead circuit, and a meter on its ohms range can be damaged by voltage. Switch off, isolate and lock out the supply, prove it dead with a voltage test, and discharge capacitors first. The UT620C+ lists input protection of only 36 V, so it must never touch a live conductor. Large windings store energy and can kick back when the test current stops, so keep the clips on until the reading has ended.

How the ten compare on paper

What to look for in a low-resistance meter

Lowest resistance range

A 50 Ω or 200 Ω range resolves far finer than the 600 Ω range most electrician's meters start at.

The digit term in accuracy

On a low range, '+10 digits' or '+20 digits' can be larger than the resistance you're measuring. Read it before the percentage.

Lead zeroing

Test leads and probe contacts add tenths of an ohm. REL or ZERO subtracts them, so a 0.05 Ω part doesn't read 0.3 Ω.

4-wire (Kelvin) measurement

Separate current and sense leads take lead and contact resistance out of the reading entirely. Ordinary multimeters can't do it.

Test current

More current gives a bigger voltage to measure across a milliohm part. Multimeters use milliamps; micro-ohm meters use up to amps.

Inductive-load handling

Motor and transformer windings take time to settle and kick back when the current stops. A dedicated mode for them helps.

2-wire vs 4-wire in one example

Measure a 0.1 Ω shunt with a 2-wire meter and the reading includes both leads and both probe contacts; REL removes the leads, but the contacts change every time you reposition the probes. A 4-wire meter sends current through one pair of clips and senses voltage through the other, so the contacts drop out. That's the difference between roughly ±0.02 Ω on the Fluke 289 and about ±0.5 mΩ on the UT620A at the same value. Long cables raise the same question; see measuring continuity on a long wire and measuring a speaker's resistance.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Decide how low you actually need to go

Speaker voice coils, heating elements, solenoids and relay coils sit in ohms or tens of ohms, where any 2-wire meter with a 200 Ω to 600 Ω range and REL does a good job. Current-sense shunts, relay contacts and long cable runs reach tenths of an ohm or less, which pushes you toward the Fluke 289. Busbar joints, crimps and welds need a 4-wire micro-ohm meter.

Do the uncertainty arithmetic before you buy

Take the lowest range's digit term and multiply it by the resolution. On many 0.01 Ω meters that gives 0.1 Ω or more, which makes a 0.05 Ω reading meaningless as an absolute value. Comparing two similar parts with the same leads is still useful, but for a pass or fail against a milliohm limit, buy the instrument whose fixed error is well below that limit.

Treat leads and contacts as part of the measurement

On a 2-wire meter, the probes, clips and the pressure you apply all add resistance. Zero the meter with the exact leads you'll use, press the tips firmly into clean metal, and take more than one reading. A 4-wire meter removes this problem, which is the main reason to buy one, more than its extra digits.

Don't overlook test current

A meter that sources 1 mA through a 1 mΩ joint produces just 1 µV to measure. The Fluke 289 uses 10 mA on its 50 Ω range, the UT620C+ up to 1 A and the UT620A up to 5 A, which is why their low-range readings are steadier. The flip side is that a high current can warm a small part, so allow for that on fine wires and fuses.

Methodology

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Can a regular multimeter measure milliohms?+

Only roughly. Most meters start at a 200 Ω to 600 Ω range with 0.01 Ω or 0.1 Ω steps, and their accuracy specs add 10 or more digits, so the fixed uncertainty is around 0.1 Ω or worse. The Fluke 289's 50 Ω range is the exception among handhelds here, at 1 mΩ resolution. For anything below a few tens of milliohms, a 4-wire micro-ohm meter is the right tool.

What is 4-wire (Kelvin) resistance measurement?+

One pair of leads drives a known current through the part and a second pair measures the voltage directly across it. Because almost no current flows in the sense leads, their resistance and the contact resistance at the clips don't add to the reading. That's why the UNI-T UT620A and UT620C+ can resolve micro-ohms while a 2-wire multimeter can't.

How do I zero out test lead resistance on a multimeter?+

Select the lowest resistance range, touch the probe tips firmly together, wait for the reading to settle and press REL (relative) or ZERO. The meter then subtracts that value from later readings. Redo it if you change leads, clips or probe tips, and keep the leads in the same position, because pressure and flexing change the contact resistance.

How accurate is a multimeter on a 0.1 Ω resistor?+

Work it out from the spec. On a range with 0.01 Ω resolution and a +10 digit term, the fixed part alone is 0.1 Ω, as large as the resistor. The Fluke 289's 50 Ω range, at ±(0.15% + 20 digits) with 1 mΩ resolution, gives roughly ±0.02 Ω. The UT620A's 120 mΩ range, at ±(0.25% + 25 digits) with 10 µΩ steps, gives about ±0.5 mΩ.

What resistance should a motor winding or busbar joint read?+

It depends on the conductor size and length, so compare against the manufacturer's figure or between phases of the same motor rather than against a universal number. Small motors and solenoid coils often read a few ohms, which a good multimeter handles, while large windings, busbar joints and welds sit in the milliohm or micro-ohm range where a 4-wire meter is needed.

Why does my multimeter show a few tenths of an ohm with the leads shorted?+

That's the resistance of the leads, the probe tips and the jack contacts. Typical values are a few tenths of an ohm, more if the tips are dirty or the leads are worn. Use the meter's relative mode to subtract it, clean the tips, and if the shorted reading jumps around, replace the leads before trusting any low-resistance measurement.
