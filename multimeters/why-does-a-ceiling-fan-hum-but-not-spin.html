Why Does a Ceiling Fan Hum but Not Spin? Electrical Faults to Check

A motor that hums with the power on but doesn't turn is almost always receiving power but failing to start, and a weak or failed run capacitor is by far the most common reason. This page covers the safe checks you can do; the full capacitor test itself is covered in how to test a fan motor capacitor, and a winding-specific check in how to test a 4-wire fan motor. Any work inside the fan's wiring or the switch housing should go to a qualified electrician if you're not comfortable with it.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 25, 2026

Turn off power before touching the motor or wiring

  • Turn off power at the wall switch and, for any work beyond a brief observation, at the breaker as well.
  • Let the motor and capacitor cool before handling if the fan has been humming for a while.
  • A capacitor can hold a charge after power is removed — discharge it safely before testing, per the capacitor-testing guide.

1Confirm power is reaching the motor

With the wall switch on, confirm voltage reaches the fan with a non-contact tester or a CAT-rated meter at an accessible point, then switch off at the breaker before opening any housing. This confirms power genuinely reaches the fan's own wiring. Humming itself is usually a strong sign power is present, but confirming it rules out a marginal connection as a contributing factor.

2The capacitor is the usual suspect

With power off, locate the fan's capacitor (often in a small housing near the motor or in the switch housing) and test its capacitance against its rated value, following how to test a fan motor capacitor.

Most ceiling fans use a permanent split capacitor (PSC) motor with two windings. The main winding connects straight across the supply, while the auxiliary winding is fed through the capacitor, which shifts its current ahead of the main winding's. That offset turns two pulsing fields into one rotating field, which is what gives the rotor its starting torque. If the capacitor loses its value, the auxiliary current shrinks and the two fields line up in time: the main winding still buzzes at line frequency, but nothing pushes the rotor in either direction.

FindingLikely cause
Capacitance well below rated valueWeak capacitor — replace it
Capacitance reads zero or openFailed capacitor — replace it
Capacitance reads correctlyCapacitor is fine — check the motor windings and for obstructions instead

3Check for a physical obstruction

With power off, spin the blades by hand and feel for binding, grinding, or resistance. A bearing that's seized or heavily worn, or debris caught in the motor housing, can also prevent starting even with a perfectly good capacitor and motor winding.

A capacitor failure is far more common than a winding failure

Start with the capacitor test before assuming a motor winding fault — it's both the more likely cause and the simpler, cheaper part to replace.

When it's the motor winding instead

If the capacitor tests correctly and there's no physical obstruction, the motor's own windings may have an internal fault. See how to test a 4-wire fan motor for resistance checks between the winding terminals.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a bad capacitor cause humming instead of just no response at all?

The capacitor provides the phase shift needed to start the motor spinning in the right direction; without it (or with a weak one), the motor's windings are still energized and trying to create a magnetic field, which produces the hum, but there isn't enough starting torque to actually get the blades moving from a standstill.

If I give the blades a push by hand and they start spinning, does that confirm a capacitor fault?

It's a strong supporting clue, though not an absolute proof — a motor that runs fine once manually started but can't start on its own is a classic capacitor symptom, since the motor's running winding works but the starting assist doesn't. Still confirm with an actual capacitance measurement rather than relying on this alone.

Is it dangerous to spin the blades by hand to test this?

Turn off power at the wall switch and ideally at the breaker before touching the blades for any reason beyond a very brief, careful nudge with power on to observe the behavior — and even then, keep clear of the motor housing and any exposed wiring.

Could the pull-chain switch be the problem instead of the capacitor?

Yes, particularly on fans where the pull-chain switches between speeds by selecting different capacitor taps — a worn or dirty switch contact can fail to make a good connection, with symptoms very similar to a failed capacitor itself. If available, try other speed settings to see if any of them start the fan.

When should I stop troubleshooting and call an electrician?

Call an electrician if the fault seems to be in the house wiring or switch box itself rather than the fan unit, if you're not comfortable safely accessing the fan's internal wiring, or if you see any signs of burning, melted insulation, or scorching.
