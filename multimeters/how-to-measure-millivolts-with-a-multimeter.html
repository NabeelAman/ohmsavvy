HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Measure Millivolts With a Multimeter

To measure millivolts with a multimeter, plug the leads into COM and V, select DC (or AC) volts, and either turn the dial to the mV position or let an autoranging meter drop to its millivolt range by itself; then read the value marked mV, where 1 V equals 1,000 mV. The harder part is trusting a reading that small. This guide shows which range to use, how to remove lead and contact offsets, and how to turn millivolt readings from thermocouples, current shunts, load cells and connection voltage drops into temperatures, amps and verdicts, with the arithmetic shown.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated November 18, 2026

Fluke specifies 10 µV maximum DC resolution for the 87V, ten times finer than a 0.1 mV entry meter. See our Fluke 87V review.

Small readings, big circuits

  • A millivolt reading across a shunt or a connection does not mean the circuit is low voltage. The shunt may sit on a battery bank that can deliver hundreds of amps, or on a line-voltage circuit. Rate the meter and your precautions for the whole circuit.
  • Keep the red lead in the V jack. Measuring millivolts across a shunt with the lead in the A jack shorts the shunt through the meter and can blow its fuse.
  • Around gas appliances, test thermocouples and thermopiles only as the appliance maker describes. If you smell gas, leave and call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

Millivolts in one minute

A millivolt is one thousandth of a volt. Many sensors and checks produce signals in that range, which is why the mV range matters even if you mostly measure batteries and outlets. The table shows where millivolts turn up and what a reading means.

SourceTypical signalWhat it tells you
Type K thermocouple4.096 mV at 100 °C (0 °C reference)Temperature difference across the junctions
Current shunt rated 100 A / 75 mV0.75 mV per ampCurrent through the shunt
Load cell rated 2 mV/V, 10 V excitation0 to 20 mV over full loadForce or weight
Gas appliance thermopileHundreds of mV (maker's spec)Whether it can hold the gas valve open
Voltage drop across a connectionA few mV to hundreds of mVWhether the joint is clean and tight

The thermocouple figure comes from the NIST ITS-90 reference tables, and the shunt and load cell figures are their ratings, explained in the steps below. Millivolt work is mostly DC; the AC mV range is for audio and small-signal checks, and its accuracy usually applies only over a limited frequency band.

1Set up the meter for millivolts

Plug the black lead into COM and the red lead into V. Select DC volts. On a manual-range meter, choose the lowest position, often marked 200m: the display then reads directly in millivolts with 0.1 mV resolution on a 2,000-count meter. On an autoranging meter, leave it on volts; when the signal is small it moves to its bottom range, which is 400.0 mV on the Fluke 15B+ and 600.0 mV on the Fluke 115, per Fluke's specifications. If the reading hovers near a range boundary and keeps switching, press RANGE until the mV range is locked. If the dial has a dedicated mV position, use it, because it often has better accuracy than letting the volts position autorange.

2Check the zero before you trust a small reading

Touch the probe tips together and look at the display. A good meter reads 0.0 mV or within a count or two. That residue comes from the meter's input offset plus tiny thermocouple voltages wherever two different metals touch, such as a brass jack, a nickel-plated probe and a copper wire. Warming a joint with your fingers can shift it. If your meter has REL (relative) mode, as the Fluke 17B+ does, press it with the probes shorted to zero the offset; otherwise note the figure and subtract it.

3Measure and read the value

Hold the probes firmly on clean metal, because a light, wobbly touch adds resistance and noise. Keep the leads short and close together so they enclose little area for magnetic pickup near motors or inverters. Let the reading settle for a few seconds, then read it with its unit: 37.5 mV is 0.0375 V. If the last digit wanders, record the middle value, or use MIN MAX to capture the range. Then apply the accuracy spec, because the counts term is large relative to a small reading. Fluke specifies the 115's DC volts as ±(0.5% + 2): on its 600.0 mV range a 37.5 mV reading is good to 0.19 + 0.2 = ±0.39 mV, about ±1%, but a 5.0 mV reading is good to only ±0.23 mV, about ±4.5%.

4Convert shunt millivolts to amps

A shunt is a precise low-value resistor rated by the current that produces its full-scale voltage, commonly 50 mV or 75 mV. Battery monitors and DC panel meters read it the same way you can. Put the probes on the two small sense screws, not the big current bolts, so the bolt contact resistance isn't in your reading.

The arithmetic is a ratio: amps = measured mV ÷ rated mV × rated amps. For a 100 A / 75 mV shunt reading 37.5 mV, that is 37.5 ÷ 75 × 100 = 50 A. Each millivolt is 100 ÷ 75 ≈ 1.33 A, so a ±0.39 mV meter uncertainty is about ±0.5 A. The shunt's resistance is 75 mV ÷ 100 A = 0.75 mΩ, which is why you measure the voltage across it rather than trying to read its resistance with the ohms range.

5Read a thermocouple's millivolts

A thermocouple produces a voltage proportional to the temperature difference between its hot junction and the point where its wires meet copper. For Type K, the NIST reference table gives 4.096 mV at 100 °C with the reference at 0 °C, roughly 41 µV per degree in that range. So a 1 °C change is only 0.04 mV, below what a 0.1 mV meter can see; for temperature, use a meter with a Type K input, which applies the cold-junction correction for you.

On gas appliances, the mV reading is used as a health check. A standing-pilot thermocouple or thermopile must produce enough voltage to hold the gas valve open, and the appliance maker states the minimum open-circuit and loaded figures. Measure with the pilot heating the tip fully and compare. Our thermocouple guide walks through the furnace and water-heater test.

6Measure voltage drop in millivolts

A connection that should be a near-perfect conductor shows up as millivolts when current flows through it. Put one probe on each side of a battery clamp, a crimp or a switch contact while the circuit carries its normal current. Divide the millivolts by the amps to get the joint resistance, and multiply by the amps again to get the heat. A joint dropping 50 mV at 10 A is 5 mΩ and dissipates 0.05 V × 10 A = 0.5 W; the same joint at 100 A of starter current drops 500 mV and dissipates 50 W, enough to make it hot.

Compare each joint with an identical good one, or with the limit in the vehicle or equipment service manual. For cars, the car voltage drop test shows the cranking and charging circuit checks in detail.

At millivolts, the leads are part of the circuit

Offsets of a fraction of a millivolt from probes, clips and dissimilar metals don't matter at 12 V but can be several percent of a 5 mV signal. Zero the meter, keep contacts clean and still, and read the accuracy spec against the reading you actually got.

Which multimeter to use for millivolts

For shunts, voltage drops and thermopiles, 0.1 mV resolution is enough. For thermocouple voltages, strain gauges and load cells, where the whole signal is a few millivolts, the 87V's 10 µV resolution and ±(0.05% + 1) DC accuracy make a real difference. Frequently asked questions

How do I set my multimeter to millivolts?

On a manual meter, turn the dial to the DC volts position marked 200m or similar; readings there are in millivolts. On an autoranging meter, select volts and connect the probes; the meter switches itself to its mV range when the signal is small enough, and the display shows mV. Some meters have a separate mV position on the dial.

How many millivolts are in a volt?

There are 1,000 millivolts in one volt. To convert mV to volts, divide by 1,000, so 750 mV is 0.75 V and 37.5 mV is 0.0375 V. To convert volts to mV, multiply by 1,000, so 0.2 V is 200 mV. One millivolt is in turn 1,000 microvolts (µV).

Can a cheap multimeter measure millivolts accurately?

For readings of tens to hundreds of millivolts, usually yes, within a few counts. A typical 4,000- or 6,000-count meter resolves 0.1 mV on its bottom range. Below about 5 mV, the fixed counts in the accuracy spec and offsets from leads and connections dominate, so a meter with 10 µV resolution, such as the Fluke 87V, is the better tool.

How do I test a thermopile's millivolts?

Set the meter to DC mV and measure across the thermopile's two leads at the gas valve with the pilot lit and fully heating the thermopile. Compare the reading with the appliance maker's minimum in the manual, both open-circuit and with the valve connected. A reading well below the maker's figure points to a weak thermopile, a dirty pilot flame or poor connections.

Why does my multimeter show a few millivolts with the probes shorted?

That residual reading is offset from the meter's input, small voltages created where dissimilar metals meet, and noise picked up by the leads. A count or two is normal. Note it and subtract it, or use the meter's REL (relative) button to zero it before measuring. A large or drifting offset can mean dirty probe tips or a weak meter battery.
