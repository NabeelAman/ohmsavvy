Rigol DS1102Z-E vs Siglent SDS1102CML+ spec by spec

Memory decides how long the scope can keep sampling at full speed. At 1 GSa/s, 24 Mpts holds 24 ms of signal; 40 Kpts holds 40 µs. Beyond that, a scope has to lower its sample rate to fit the timebase. The front-end numbers on the two spec sheets match, so memory, decoding and update rate are where the money goes.

The last column is memory divided by the time on screen, capped at 1 GSa/s, and scopes round to their own steps, so treat it as a guide. It shows why shallow memory hurts: at a slow timebase the Siglent in normal mode samples a thousand times slower than its headline figure.

How the Siglent reaches 50 GSa/s

The CML+'s 50 GSa/s figure is equivalent-time sampling, not real-time. On a signal that repeats exactly, the scope takes a sample on each trigger, shifted slightly later each time, and stitches the results into one finely resolved cycle. It is a genuine way to see fast edges on a clock or a test signal, but it can't capture anything that happens once.

Why this matters for serial buses

A UART byte at 9,600 baud lasts about a millisecond, and it never repeats exactly, so equivalent-time sampling can't help. A short record then forces a lower sample rate and loses edge detail. Our guide to what GSa/s means explains the trade-off.

Use cases in practice

Students and hobbyists tend to outgrow a scope through serial buses and one-off events, and both point to the Rigol. Decoding turns an I2C transaction into readable addresses and bytes, and deep memory lets you capture the whole transaction at a sample rate high enough to see the edges. Our guide to using persistence to find intermittent glitches shows why update rate matters too.

The Siglent's strengths suit a classroom: 32 automatic measurements with five on screen, a frequency counter, and a pass/fail mask that flags when a waveform leaves a template. If a lab only needs students to read amplitude, period and phase on sine and square waves, it does that well. For a broader comparison, see the DS1102Z-E against the four-channel DS1054Z or our oscilloscope buying guide.

Safety: bench scope inputs are not for mains

Benchtop scope inputs like these are typically rated only for low-energy circuits, and their probe ground clips connect to earth through the power cord. Clipping ground to a live mains conductor creates a short circuit. Check each scope's input rating in its manual, use a rated differential probe for anything mains-connected, and leave panel work to a licensed electrician.

  • ✓You want UART, I2C, SPI and parallel decoding without extra cost

The bottom line

The Rigol DS1102Z-E is the better buy for almost everyone: far deeper memory, standard serial decoding and a stated 30,000 wfms/s update rate on the same 100 MHz, two-channel base. The Siglent SDS1102CML+ only makes sense for basic classroom viewing at a clearly lower price.

Frequently asked questions

How much memory does the SDS1102CML+ have?+

Siglent lists 40 Kpts in normal mode and 2 Mpts in long-memory mode; its Amazon listing says 1 Mpts. We use Siglent's own figures and flag the conflict. Either way it is far short of the Rigol DS1102Z-E's 24 Mpts, which matters as soon as you slow the timebase or capture a serial packet.

Does the Rigol DS1102Z-E decode serial buses?+

Yes. Rigol's datasheet lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C and SPI decoding as standard, two decodes at a time. That lets you read bytes and addresses on screen instead of counting edges. Siglent lists no serial decoding for the SDS1102CML+, so on that scope you would decode by hand from the waveform.

Are both scopes really 100 MHz?+

Yes. Both are 100 MHz, two-channel scopes with 1 GSa/s maximum real-time sampling and a 7-inch 800 × 480 display. With both channels on, Rigol's figure halves to 500 MSa/s per channel; Siglent's page doesn't break down the two-channel rate. Either is comfortable on signals up to roughly 20 to 30 MHz.

Is the SDS1102CML+ discontinued?+

Siglent North America still sells it new, and we found no discontinued notice on its site. It is an older and simpler design than the Rigol, with no serial decoding and shallow memory, so check the live Amazon listing before buying, since stock on older models can come and go.

Which is better for students, the DS1102Z-E or the SDS1102CML+?+

The DS1102Z-E, because deep memory and decoding stay useful as projects grow from blinking LEDs to sensors and serial buses. The CML+ covers basic waveform viewing, automatic measurements and frequency counting, which is enough for an introductory lab but limiting once you start debugging microcontroller communications.
