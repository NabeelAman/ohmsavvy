HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Use a Multimeter

A multimeter has one job underneath all its modes: it measures electrical properties — voltage, resistance, continuity, and usually current — so you can confirm what's actually happening in a circuit instead of guessing. This guide walks through every mode a beginner needs, in the order you'll actually use them.

Most multimeters, including every pick in our full multimeter roundup, share the same core layout. The steps below apply regardless of which one you own.

Before you touch anything: three safety habits

  • Check your meter's CAT rating against what you're measuring. CAT III covers household panels and fixed wiring; CAT II covers outlets and plugged-in appliances only. Measuring a higher-category circuit with a lower-rated meter is a genuine safety risk, not a minor spec mismatch — see our buying guide for the full breakdown.
  • Always start on the highest range for an unfamiliar voltage (or trust auto-ranging, which most modern meters have) rather than guessing a specific range and risking an over-range reading.
  • Inspect your probe leads before every session. Cracked insulation or an exposed wire defeats the CAT rating printed on the meter itself.
CATCovers
CAT IProtected, low-energy electronic circuits
CAT IIOutlets and plug-in equipment
CAT IIIPanels and fixed building wiring
CAT IVThe utility connection / service entrance

Reading the dial: what the symbols mean

Every multimeter uses roughly the same set of symbols, which is why switching between brands is easier than it looks once you know them.

DCV

DC voltage — batteries, electronics

ACV

AC voltage — outlets, mains wiring

Ω

Resistance — resistors, wiring

))

Continuity — a beep means connected

Typical voltages, so you can sanity-check a reading

If a reading looks wildly off, it helps to know roughly what you should be seeing. These are typical values, not guarantees — always compare against the specific device's own rating.

The spread is the reason range and safety rating matter. Everything below about 13 V in this chart is low-energy DC you can probe on a bench without much risk; the two highlighted bars are mains circuits where a wrong dial setting, a probe in the amps jack or a meter without a CAT III rating can cause an arc flash. If you're manually ranging, pick the first range above the value you expect, such as 20 V for a car battery or 600 V for an outlet, then step down only if the reading is tiny.

1How to measure DC voltage

DC voltage is what you'll check most as a beginner: batteries, USB power, microcontroller boards, and most hobby electronics. Set the dial to DCV (or let auto-ranging handle it), plug the black probe into the COM jack and the red probe into the voltage jack, then touch black to the negative terminal and red to the positive. The display shows the voltage directly — a negative sign just means the probes are reversed, not that something's wrong.

2How to measure AC voltage

AC voltage covers wall outlets and household wiring, and it's where CAT rating matters most. Set the dial to ACV, confirm your meter's CAT III rating covers the circuit you're testing, then insert the probes into an outlet's two slots (polarity doesn't matter for AC), keeping your fingers behind the finger guards molded into the probes rather than near the metal tips. A healthy US outlet reads roughly 120V; a reading well outside that range, or no reading at all, points to a wiring issue worth investigating before you assume the outlet is simply "off." Never test a socket, switch, or panel that shows visible damage, scorching, or exposed wiring — that's a job for an electrician, not a multimeter check.

3How to check 240V with a multimeter

240V shows up two different ways depending on where you are and what you're testing, and the probe placement is different for each:

US split-phase: probes on the two hot legs.

  • US 240V appliance circuits (dryers, ranges, AC units) are split-phase: two separate hot legs, each around 120V to neutral, that add up to roughly 240V between them. Set the dial to ACV, then touch one probe to each hot terminal — the two outer prongs on a 240V outlet, or the two hot bus bars in a panel. Polarity doesn't matter for AC.
  • UK/EU/Australia-style 240V mains is a standard single-phase line voltage, not split-phase. Touch one probe to Line (L) and the other to Neutral (N) for the voltage reading, or Line to Earth/Ground as a separate safety check.

Expect a reading somewhere around 220–250V depending on your region's normal tolerance; a reading well outside that, or nothing at all, points to a real fault. Because 240V carries more energy than a standard 120V outlet check, confirm your meter and leads are CAT III-rated before testing a panel or hardwired appliance circuit, and stop and call an electrician if you see any damage, scorching, or exposed wiring rather than troubleshooting further yourself.

4How to measure resistance

Switch to the Ω setting and touch a probe to each end of the component you're measuring — never on a powered circuit, since resistance mode sends its own small current through the probes and a live circuit will give a meaningless or dangerous reading. For best accuracy, remove the component from the circuit entirely, since other parts wired in parallel can skew the number you see.

Ranges, lead resistance and resistor color codes are covered in how to measure resistance in ohms with a digital multimeter.

5How to test continuity

Continuity mode is a specialized, faster version of resistance mode: it beeps when the resistance between the probes is low enough to count as a solid electrical connection. It's the fastest way to check a fuse, a cable, or a switch — touch both ends, and a beep confirms current can flow. No beep on something you expect to be connected usually means a break somewhere in that path.

6How to test a battery

Set the dial to DCV, touch red to positive and black to negative, and compare the reading to the battery's rated voltage. A fresh AA battery reads close to 1.5V; a reading noticeably below that suggests it's worth replacing. For a more realistic picture, test under load if your meter supports it — a battery that reads fine with nothing connected can still sag badly once it's actually powering something.

7How to measure current — and why it's different

Current is the one measurement that changes how you connect the meter entirely. Voltage, resistance, and continuity all measure in parallel — probes touching two points on a circuit that stays otherwise intact. Current measurement requires breaking the circuit open and placing the meter in series, so every electron has to pass through the meter itself to be counted.

Voltage: meter connects in parallel, alongside the circuit.

Current: meter connects in series, breaking the circuit open.

Move the red probe to the dedicated current (A) jack before switching modes — most meters fuse this jack separately, and leaving the probe in the voltage jack while measuring current in series can blow that fuse or, on a cheap unmetered meter, damage the meter entirely. If you measure current often, a clamp meter avoids opening the circuit at all.

Mains AC current isn't a beginner DIY job

Opening a circuit to measure current is routine on low-voltage DC work — batteries, microcontroller boards, hobby electronics. It's a different proposition on mains AC wiring: breaking open a live household circuit to insert a meter in series is professional electrical work, not a beginner task, and it's exactly the situation a clamp meter exists to avoid. If the circuit you need current on is mains-powered, use a clamp meter or call an electrician rather than opening it by hand.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Leaving the probe in the current jack, then measuring voltage — this shorts the circuit through the meter's internal fuse.
  • Measuring resistance on a powered circuit, which gives a false reading and can damage the meter.
  • Forgetting that continuity mode and resistance mode read the whole parallel network, not just the one component you're trying to isolate.
  • Assuming a digital reading of "0" always means no connection — on some ranges it can mean a value too small for that range to resolve.
