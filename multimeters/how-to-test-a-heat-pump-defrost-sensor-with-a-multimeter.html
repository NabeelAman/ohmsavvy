How to Test a Heat Pump Defrost Sensor With a Multimeter

To test a heat pump defrost sensor, unplug it from the defrost board, measure its resistance, and compare the reading with the manufacturer's resistance-versus-temperature chart at the coil temperature you measured. A thermistor sensor that reads OL, reads near 0 Ω, or sits far off the chart has failed; an older snap-disc defrost thermostat should read closed (near 0 Ω) when cold and open when warm. Below is how to test a heat pump defrost sensor of either type, what a typical 10k NTC reads around freezing, and how to tell a bad sensor from a bad board.

Outdoor unit safety

  • The outdoor unit is fed at 208–240 VAC. Turn off the disconnect and the breaker and verify 0 V on the line terminals with your meter before reaching into the cabinet.
  • The run capacitor can stay charged after power is off. Discharge it through a resistor and confirm 0 V.
  • Coil fins are sharp, and the compressor and its discharge line can be hot enough to burn even in winter. Wear gloves. If the defrost board or refrigerant circuit is the problem, call an HVAC technician; anything involving refrigerant needs an EPA-certified tech in the US.

How heat pump defrost works

In heating mode the outdoor coil runs colder than the outdoor air, often below freezing, so moisture in the air freezes onto the fins. Frost blocks airflow and the unit loses capacity. The defrost board watches two things: how long the compressor has run and how cold the coil is. When both say frost is likely, the board flips the reversing valve to cooling mode, stops the outdoor fan so the coil heats up quickly, and brings on the backup heat strips so the house doesn't get a blast of cold air. When the sensor reports the coil has warmed up, or a maximum time runs out, the board ends defrost.

The sensor is the board's only window onto the coil. If it lies, the board makes the wrong call in one of two directions: no defrost at all, or defrost every cycle. Both cost money, which is why defrost faults show up so often in high winter heat pump bills.

Thermistor or snap-disc defrost thermostat

Before you measure anything, find out which part you have, because they read in completely different ways. Check the wiring diagram inside the service panel; it usually names the part.

NTC thermistor

A small bead or bullet in a clip. Resistance rises as it gets colder. Read it in ohms and compare with a chart.

Snap-disc thermostat

A round metal disc in a clip. A switch: closed when the coil is cold, open once it warms up.

Ambient sensor

A second thermistor in free air on some units. Same test, but it reads air temperature, not coil.

1Isolate the outdoor unit

Set the thermostat to off, pull the outdoor disconnect, switch off the breaker and remove the service panel. Confirm 0 V across the contactor line terminals with your meter. Photograph the defrost board and the sensor plug before unplugging anything.

2Measure the coil temperature at the sensor

A resistance reading means nothing until you know the sensor's temperature. If your meter has a Type K thermocouple input, tape the bead to the same tube right beside the sensor and give it a minute. Write the number down. Without a probe, a kitchen thermometer against the tube is a rough substitute. Our guide on checking a thermocouple covers the probe itself.

3Test the heat pump defrost sensor resistance

Unplug the sensor from the board so the board's circuit isn't in parallel with it. Set the meter to ohms (auto-range, or the 200 kΩ range on a manual meter) and read across the two sensor leads at the plug. Then compare with the maker's chart, which is often printed in the service manual or on the panel diagram.

For a thermistor, the reading should sit close to the chart value for the temperature you measured, allowing a few percent for tolerance. Many brands use a 10k NTC, which reads about 10 kΩ at 77 °F (25 °C) and climbs to roughly 33 to 34 kΩ at 32 °F (0 °C). Not every maker uses 10k, so check before you condemn a sensor that reads 3 kΩ or 50 kΩ. For a snap-disc thermostat on a coil warmer than about 60 °F, expect OL (open); on a frosted coil, expect near 0 Ω (closed).

4Do the ice-water test

When the weather won't give you a known temperature, make one. Unclip the sensor, drop it into a cup of crushed ice and water, stir, and wait two to three minutes. Ice water sits at 32 °F (0 °C), so a 10k NTC should settle near 33 to 34 kΩ. Lift it out, hold it in your hand and the reading should fall smoothly as it warms. A reading that jumps, drops to OL when you flex the lead, or never moves is a bad sensor.

A snap-disc thermostat needs a little more cold. Many close around freezing or slightly below, so add a spoonful of salt to the ice water to push it a few degrees lower, then listen for the click and watch the meter drop to near 0 Ω. Warm it in your hand and it should open again, though some only open once they pass roughly 55 to 70 °F, so a cup of warm tap water is a quicker check. For more on NTC behavior and the B value, see how to test a thermistor with a multimeter.

5Check the defrost board

If the sensor passes and the unit still won't defrost, the board is next. Many boards have a pair of TEST pins that, when shorted briefly as the board label describes, speed up the timer and force a defrost, provided the sensor reports a cold coil. With power restored and the system in heat, short the pins and watch: the outdoor fan should stop, the reversing valve should shift with a whoosh, and W2 should send 24 VAC to the backup heat. If the board gets a cold-coil signal and those outputs never change, the board is the likely fault. Its relays can be checked the same way as any relay in how to test a relay, and if the fan stops but the valve doesn't shift, test the reversing valve solenoid.

This step is live: 240 V is back on the contactor and the fan can restart without warning. Keep hands out of the fan guard and probes on the low-voltage terminals only. If you are not comfortable, hand this step to an HVAC technician.

Typical 10k NTC defrost sensor values near freezing

These figures come from the standard beta equation, R = 10 kΩ × eB(1/T − 1/298.15) with B = 3950 and T in kelvin. At 32 °F, T = 273.15 K, so the exponent is 3950 × (0.003661 − 0.003354) ≈ 1.21 and R ≈ 10 × e1.21 ≈ 33.6 kΩ. They show the shape of a common curve. Your maker's chart may use a different B value or base resistance, and its numbers win.

Open reads cold, shorted reads hot

To the board, an open thermistor looks like an extremely cold coil and a shorted one looks like a hot coil. That is why an open sensor often causes constant defrosting and a shorted one causes a coil that never defrosts. Match the symptom to the reading and you know you have found the fault.

Which multimeter to use for defrost sensors

Any meter with a resistance range to 200 kΩ or more will read the sensor. The useful extra is a temperature input with a Type K probe, so you can read the tube temperature next to the sensor and compare the two numbers on the spot. Use a CAT III rated meter, because the same cabinet holds the 240 V supply. For a wider list, see our best multimeter for HVAC technicians guide.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of a bad defrost sensor on a heat pump?

The outdoor coil turns into a solid block of ice and stays that way, or the unit drops into defrost far too often, including on mild dry days. Some boards also flash a sensor fault code. Heating capacity falls, the backup heat strips run more, and the electric bill climbs. Ice that clears after a defrost and returns later is normal.

How often should a heat pump go into defrost?

It depends on the weather and the board setting. Many time-temperature boards check every 30, 60 or 90 minutes of compressor runtime and only defrost if the coil sensor reads cold. A typical defrost lasts a few minutes and is capped at around 10 to 14 minutes on many boards. In humid weather near freezing, frequent cycles are normal.

Can I bypass or jump a heat pump defrost sensor?

Briefly, for testing only. On many boards with a snap-disc thermostat, jumping the sensor terminals tells the board the coil is cold, and with the test pins shorted the unit enters defrost so you can watch the outputs. Never leave a jumper in place. The unit would defrost every cycle, wasting energy and heating with backup strips instead of the compressor.

Is the defrost sensor the same as the outdoor temperature sensor?

No, though some units have both. The defrost or coil sensor clips onto a refrigerant tube and reports coil temperature. An outdoor ambient sensor sits in free air, often on the cabinet or board, and helps the board decide when defrost is needed and when to lock out the compressor. Check the wiring diagram on the panel to tell which leads go where.

Where is the defrost sensor located on a heat pump?

Usually low on the outdoor coil, clipped to one of the small tubes that feed the coil or to a return bend near the bottom, because that area frosts first. Follow the two wires from the DFT, COIL or SENSOR terminals on the defrost board. On some units the sensor sits under the coil's sheet-metal guard, so remove the top or side panel to reach it.
