HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test a Brake Light Switch with a Multimeter

To test a brake light switch with a multimeter, unplug it and check continuity across its brake lamp pins: it should read OL with the pedal released and beep at close to 0 Ω with the pedal pressed. Then plug it back in and confirm battery voltage on the feed pin all the time and on the output pin only when the pedal is down. The switch is a small plunger switch above the brake pedal arm, and on many cars the same part also cancels cruise control and releases the shift interlock, so one failed switch can cause several odd symptoms at once. This guide covers both tests, the numbers behind a worn contact, and how to tell a bad switch from a bad adjustment.

Disconnect before you probe for continuity

Continuity and resistance checks are only valid with the switch unplugged from the harness. With the connector attached, other circuits can give false readings, and putting a live circuit on the meter in continuity mode is never a good idea. Live voltage tests use DC volts, with the red lead in the V jack. Some cars have a knee airbag or wiring for it under the dash near the pedals: don't probe yellow airbag connectors, and follow the service manual if a trim panel hides an airbag module.

Symptoms of a bad brake light switch

A brake light switch fault usually shows up as one of four things: brake lamps that never light, brake lamps that stay on with the pedal at rest, a shifter that won't come out of Park even with the pedal pressed, or cruise control that won't engage or cancels at random. Some vehicles also log a brake switch code or show a warning, because the engine or stability control computer compares the switch signal with other inputs. On some newer cars the switch no longer carries lamp current at all: it sends a signal to a body module, which then switches the lamps. The tests below work either way, but the wiring diagram tells you what to expect on the output pin.

1Rule out bulbs, the fuse and the ground

If only one lamp is out, the switch isn't the cause: a single switch feeds all the brake lamps, so check that bulb or its socket. If all of them are dead, check the stop-lamp fuse first, then confirm the lamp sockets have a good ground. A corroded ground at the rear can give dim or dead lamps, or lights that glow faintly when the tail lights are on. Our guide to the OL reading explains what a blown fuse looks like on a continuity check, and reading a fuse with power on one side only shows how to check it in place.

Two-pin and multi-pin switches

Look at the connector before testing. A two-pin switch has a single set of contacts that close when the pedal is pressed. Switches with three or more pins carry additional contact sets for other systems:

Two-pin

One normally open contact. Open with the pedal up, closed with it down.

Multi-pin

Adds contacts for cruise cancel or a computer input. One set is often closed at rest and opens when you brake.

Interlock signal

On many cars the shift-interlock release uses the brake switch signal, directly or via a module.

Use the wiring diagram in your service manual to identify which pins form each contact set and whether each is normally open or normally closed.

2Continuity test with the pedal up and down

Unplug the switch and set the meter to continuity (or the lowest ohms range). Touch the probe tips together first and note the reading; typical test leads add a few tenths of an ohm, which you subtract from what follows. Place the probes on the brake lamp pins. With the pedal released, a normally open set should read OL. Press the pedal, or remove the switch and press the plunger by hand, and the meter should beep and read close to 0 Ω, ideally no more than a few tenths of an ohm above your lead reading. Release it and the reading should return to OL every time. Repeat several times; an intermittent beep or a reading that wanders points to worn contacts. Repeat for any other contact set, expecting the opposite behavior on a normally closed pair.

3Live voltage in and out

With the switch plugged in, back-probe the connector on DC volts with the black lead on a good ground. The feed pin should show battery voltage whether or not the pedal is pressed (on some cars only with the key on). The output pin should read near 0 V with the pedal up and switch to battery voltage when you press it.

4Measure voltage drop across a worn switch

A switch can pass both tests above and still be failing. Each time the contacts close under load, a tiny arc pits their surfaces, and the resistance creeps up. Continuity mode beeps at anything below its threshold, often tens of ohms, so it can't see the difference between a healthy contact and a worn one. The live way to see it is a voltage drop test: with the switch plugged in and the pedal held down, put one probe on the feed pin and the other on the output pin. A healthy switch carrying lamp current typically drops no more than a couple of tenths of a volt.

Here is the arithmetic on a switch that carries lamp current directly. Two brake bulbs of 21 W each draw I = P ÷ V = 42 ÷ 12.6 ≈ 3.3 A. A contact resistance of 0.1 Ω drops 3.3 × 0.1 ≈ 0.33 V and turns 3.3² × 0.1 ≈ 1.1 W into heat inside a small plastic body. At 0.5 Ω the drop is about 1.7 V and the heat is about 5.6 W, enough to discolor the housing and dim the lamps. LED lamps and module-input designs carry far less current, so the drop is smaller and harder to read.

For more on the method, see our guide to checking voltage drop on a car and measuring resistance across a relay contact, which explains why a contact can beep and still be bad.

5Check the adjustment before you condemn the switch

Many brake light switches are set by position: some self-adjust when the pedal is pulled up after fitting, others thread in and lock with a nut. If the plunger doesn't reach the pedal arm, or the stop pad on the arm is missing, a good switch behaves like a faulty one. Follow the service manual procedure, then repeat the live test.

Two readings settle most brake switch faults

Continuity unplugged tells you whether the contacts open and close; voltage plugged in tells you whether power reaches and leaves the switch. If both are right and the lamps are still dead, the fault is downstream, in the wiring, sockets or ground. The contact logic is the same as any small snap-action switch, covered in how to test a microswitch.

Frequently asked questions

Can a bad brake light switch keep the shifter stuck in Park?

Yes, on vehicles with a brake-shift interlock. The interlock solenoid only releases the shifter when it receives a brake-applied signal, and on many designs that signal comes from the brake light switch or its circuit. If the brake lights also fail to come on, the switch, its fuse or its adjustment is a strong suspect.

Why do my brake lights stay on with the pedal released?

The usual causes are a switch that has stuck closed, a switch that has moved out of adjustment, or a missing rubber stop pad on the pedal arm, which lets the plunger extend fully. Check the plunger is being pushed in when the pedal rests at the top before replacing anything, and refit the pad if it has fallen out.

Will a faulty brake light switch drain the battery?

It can. If the switch sticks closed, the brake lamps stay lit with the car parked, and two incandescent brake bulbs draw several amps, enough to flatten a typical battery overnight. Walk around the back of the parked car, or ask someone to look, to confirm the lamps are off. Our parasitic drain guide covers measuring smaller hidden loads.

Does it matter which of the two pins I connect the probes to?

Not for a continuity check, since you are only asking whether the contacts are closed, and the switch has no polarity. It matters for the live test: one terminal is the fused feed and the other runs to the lamps or a module input, and the wiring diagram tells you which is which, so you know where to expect constant voltage.

My meter beeps across the switch in both pedal positions. Is it bad?

If it beeps across the same pair of pins with the plunger both pressed and released, that contact set is stuck or shorted and the switch should be replaced. On a multi-pin switch, make sure you are on the right pair first, because one set is often designed to be closed at rest and open when you brake.

Can a brake light switch cause a check engine or ABS light?

On many modern cars, yes. The engine, ABS or stability control computer compares the brake switch signal with other inputs, such as brake pressure or a second switch contact, and logs a code when they disagree. A misadjusted or failing switch can set that code and may also disable cruise control. Read the code, then test the switch with the steps here.
