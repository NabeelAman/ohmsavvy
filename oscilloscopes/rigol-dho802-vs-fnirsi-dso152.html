Rigol DHO802 vs FNIRSI DSO152

Rigol DHO802 vs FNIRSI DSO152 puts a full bench instrument against a scope that fits in a pocket. The DHO802 is a two-channel, 70 MHz, 12-bit scope sampling at up to 1.25 GSa/s, with a 7-inch touchscreen and standard serial decoding. The DSO152 is a single-channel, 200kHz, 2.5 MSa/s pocket scope at an extremely low entry price. Which one you want depends on where you measure and how fast your signals move.

Spec by spec

The headline gaps here are in the hundreds, so the bandwidth and sample-rate bars sit on a log scale. The fourth chart is the one worth a second look: it divides each sample rate by the scope's own bandwidth, which shows how many points each one draws per cycle of the fastest signal it is rated for.

That last chart is a reminder that the DSO152 isn't badly balanced, just small. Measured against its own 200kHz ceiling it samples 12.5 times per cycle, more finely than the DHO802 does at 70 MHz with both channels on. The difference is the ceiling itself. Our bandwidth explainer covers why the −3 dB figure sets that ceiling, and the GSa/s explainer covers why sample rate has to stay well above it.

Bench vs pocket: where each one lives

The DHO802 is built to sit in front of you while you work. Its 7-inch, 1024 × 600 capacitive touchscreen gives room for two traces plus decoded bus data, HDMI lets you mirror it onto a bigger monitor for a class or a stream, and LAN puts it on the network. It runs from a USB-C 15 V supply rather than a mains lead, which makes it lighter on cabling than a traditional benchtop, but Rigol's data in our records lists no internal battery, so plan on a power source wherever you set it up.

The DSO152 is built to go where the problem is. Being pocket-sized is its whole case: you can check a speaker line inside a cabinet, a sensor output on a machine across the workshop, or a PWM pin on a project you've already mounted, without carrying a bench scope over. Our verified data for it doesn't list a battery type, screen size or maximum input voltage, so confirm those on FNIRSI's listing before you rely on it away from an outlet or near higher voltages.

That makes the pairing less of a rivalry than it looks. A DHO802 owner may still want a DSO152 in the tool bag; a DSO152 owner who starts debugging digital circuits will want a bench scope. The question is which one you need first.

What each one can and can't measure

Bandwidth is the frequency at which a scope shows a sine wave about 3 dB low, roughly 70% of its real amplitude. The working rule is to buy 3–5 times the frequency you want to see accurately, and more for square waves, whose edges carry harmonics far above the fundamental. That rule draws the boundary for each of these scopes.

FNIRSI DSO152: slow signals, one at a time

A 200kHz front end is comfortable up to roughly 40–65kHz. Inside that band it can show an audio signal clipping, mains-frequency ripple on a linear supply after the rectifier, a thermistor or light-sensor output drifting, or a servo's 50Hz control pulse. Push higher and it starts to mislead rather than simply fail: a 100kHz I2C clock is already beyond its comfortable range, so the edges look slower than they are. Bus traffic at megahertz rates and the switching node of most modern regulators are out of reach, and with a single input it can't line up a trigger cause with its effect.

Rigol DHO802: two signals, in fine detail

At 70 MHz the DHO802 is comfortable on signals up to roughly 14–23 MHz, which takes in hobby microcontroller clocks, PWM motor drives, typical UART, I2C and SPI speeds, and everything the DSO152 can show. Its memory changes what you can capture in one shot: 25 Mpts at 1.25 GSa/s fills in about 20 ms, and 10 Mpts per channel at 625 MSa/s with both on fills in about 16 ms, long enough to hold a whole burst of serial packets at full speed and decode it afterwards. Its limits are channel count and features: two inputs mean SPI's clock, data and chip-select lines can't all be on screen together, and there is no built-in generator to drive a circuit under test.

What 12 bits buy you

An 8-bit converter splits its input range into 256 levels; a 12-bit one into 4,096. If the range spans 8 divisions at 100 mV per division, that is roughly 3 mV per step at 8 bits against about 0.2 mV at 12 bits. Real scopes spend some of that on noise and the exact range varies by design, but it is why a 12-bit scope like the DHO802 shows small ripple on a large signal more cleanly. FNIRSI doesn't state the DSO152's resolution in our data, so we don't assign it a figure.

Grounds and high voltage

On a bench scope the probe ground clip is usually connected to earth, through the power supply or through anything plugged into it such as an HDMI monitor or LAN cable. Clipping it to a live or non-earthed point can short a circuit through the scope. Use a differential probe or isolation for mains work, and don't take the DSO152 near mains unless FNIRSI's own input rating covers it; that rating isn't in our verified data.

The bottom line

If you build or debug circuits at a desk, the Rigol DHO802 is the better buy: 350 times the bandwidth, two channels, 12-bit detail, deep memory and serial decoding as standard. The FNIRSI DSO152 makes sense as an extremely affordable first look at waveforms or as a pocket tool for audio-range checks away from the bench, alongside a bench scope rather than instead of one.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Rigol DHO802 worth it over a pocket scope like the FNIRSI DSO152?+

Yes, if your work goes beyond audio-range signals. The DHO802 has 70 MHz against 200kHz, two channels against one, 12-bit resolution and standard UART, I2C, SPI and CAN decoding. If you only need to glance at slow signals in the field, or want a very cheap first scope, the DSO152 covers that.

Can the FNIRSI DSO152 decode UART or I2C like the Rigol DHO802?+

Decoding isn't listed for the DSO152 in our verified data, and its bandwidth rules out most bus work anyway. A 100kHz I2C clock is past its comfortable range of roughly 40–65kHz. The DHO802 decodes parallel, UART, I2C, SPI and CAN as standard.

Is the Rigol DHO802 portable?+

It is a bench scope with a 7-inch touchscreen that runs from a USB-C 15 V supply, so it moves between benches more easily than a mains-corded scope. Our data lists no internal battery for it, so it still needs a suitable power source. The DSO152 is the pocket-sized option of the two.

Does 12-bit resolution matter for a beginner?+

It matters most when you look at a small detail riding on a larger signal, such as ripple on a 5 V rail or noise on a sensor output. A 12-bit scope has 4,096 vertical levels against 256 for an 8-bit one, so those details show more cleanly. For basic waveform shapes, bandwidth and channels matter more.

Which is better for checking audio circuits?+

Both can show audio, which tops out around 20kHz, inside the DSO152's comfortable range. The DSO152 is enough to spot clipping on one output. The DHO802 is better for real amplifier work: it shows input and output together and its 12-bit resolution resolves low-level distortion and hum more clearly.
