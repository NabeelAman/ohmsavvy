50MHz vs 100MHz Oscilloscope

A 100MHz scope resolves roughly half the rise time a 50MHz scope can, using the common rise-time estimate of 0.35 ÷ bandwidth: about 3.5 ns versus 7 ns. For general hobby electronics, audio, and most digital logic well under a few tens of megahertz, a 50MHz scope has comfortable margin. For faster digital signals, sharper edges, or simply more headroom as projects get more demanding, 100MHz buys real room before the scope itself becomes the limiting factor.

What bandwidth does to rise time

Fluke's own guidance on this relationship gives the common estimate: a scope's minimum measurable rise time, in seconds, is approximately 0.35 divided by its bandwidth in hertz, for scopes under roughly 1 GHz. A faster real-world edge than a scope's rise-time figure gets rounded off and shown slower and smoother than it actually is.

The math: 0.35 ÷ 50,000,000 = 7 × 10⁻⁹ s (7 ns) for the 50MHz scope, and 0.35 ÷ 100,000,000 = 3.5 ns for the 100MHz scope. A genuinely faster edge than either figure gets displayed slower than it really is — the scope's own bandwidth becomes the limit on what edge speed you can trust the display to show accurately.

Why bandwidth isn't a hard cutoff

A scope's rated bandwidth is the frequency at which measured amplitude has already fallen to about 70.7% of its true value — a 3 decibel attenuation, not a point where the scope simply stops working. Amplitude accuracy degrades gradually as frequency rises, starting well before the rated bandwidth number and continuing to worsen above it.

Choosing bandwidth for your signal

Tektronix's oscilloscope primer describes a commonly used guideline sometimes called the "5 times rule": choosing a scope with bandwidth at least 5 times the highest frequency component you need to measure accurately, which it states keeps amplitude error under roughly 2%. A 10MHz signal, by that guideline, calls for at least 50MHz of scope bandwidth — which puts a 50MHz scope right at the edge of comfortable margin for that signal, and a 100MHz scope well inside it.

Fundamental frequency isn't the whole signal

A square wave's sharp edges carry significant energy in harmonics well above its fundamental frequency. The 5x guideline is meant to apply to the highest frequency content you actually care about resolving, which for a fast digital edge can be considerably higher than the signal's basic repetition rate.

When 50MHz is enough, and when it isn't

A 50MHz scope comfortably covers most hobby electronics, audio work, basic microcontroller debugging, and general repair diagnostics, where signal frequencies and edge speeds stay well under its range. It becomes the limiting factor on faster digital buses, higher-speed switching power supplies, RF-adjacent work, or simply debugging a circuit with edges fast enough that the 5x guideline above starts to apply. 100MHz doesn't change what's possible at the low end — it extends how far up you can go before the scope itself, rather than your probing technique or the circuit, becomes the constraint.

Bandwidth can't be added later

Unlike memory depth or firmware features on some scopes, bandwidth is a hardware limit set by the analog front end. If there's a real chance your projects will need more in the next year or two, buying the extra bandwidth now avoids replacing the whole scope later.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 100MHz oscilloscope twice as good as a 50MHz one?

Not twice as good in every sense, but it does resolve roughly twice the signal detail at the edge of its range: about half the rise time, and headroom for frequency content up to twice as high before attenuation becomes significant. For signals well within a 50MHz scope's comfortable range, the extra bandwidth adds margin you may never use.

Do I need 100MHz bandwidth for Arduino or basic microcontroller projects?

Usually not. Most microcontroller clock and signal frequencies, and the much slower logic transitions typical of hobby projects, sit well inside what a 50MHz scope handles comfortably. Faster buses, especially ones running tens of megahertz or above with sharp edges, are where the extra bandwidth starts to matter.

Does higher bandwidth also mean a better scope overall?

No, bandwidth is one spec among several. Sample rate, memory depth, channel count, trigger capability, and build quality all matter too, and some well-regarded lower-bandwidth scopes outperform higher-bandwidth ones on everything else. Compare the full spec sheet, not bandwidth in isolation.

What does the -3dB point on a bandwidth spec actually mean?

A scope's stated bandwidth is the frequency at which its measured amplitude has already dropped to about 70.7% of the true value — a 3 decibel loss. The scope doesn't suddenly stop working at that frequency; it's already under-reading amplitude there, and the error gets worse as frequency climbs further past that point.

Can I use a 50MHz scope on a 50MHz signal?

Technically it will show something, but at exactly its rated bandwidth the scope is already under-reading amplitude by around 29% per the standard -3dB definition. For a signal at or near a scope's bandwidth rating, expect visibly reduced amplitude accuracy, not a clean, fully faithful measurement.

Is it worth paying more for 100MHz if I'm not sure I need it?

If budget allows, extra bandwidth is a reasonable hedge against projects getting more demanding over time, since you can't add bandwidth to a scope after buying it. If budget is tight, matching bandwidth to your actual signals — using the 5x guideline below — avoids overspending on headroom you may never use.
