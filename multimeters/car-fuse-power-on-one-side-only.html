Why Does My Car Fuse Have Power on One Side Only? How to Test It

A car fuse with power on one side only is usually blown: the supply tab still sees battery voltage, but the melted element stops it reaching the load tab. The exception is a fuse whose load is switched off, which can leave a few stray volts or nothing at all on the dead side, so you confirm with the circuit switched on and a continuity check. This guide shows how to read each tab to ground, how to tell a blown fuse from an ignition-switched or relay-fed one, and what to do when the fuse turns out to be fine.

Before you probe the fuse box

Use the meter on DC volts with the red lead in the V jack, never the current jack. Keep metal probes from bridging two adjacent fuse tabs, and don't back-probe airbag circuits (usually marked with yellow connectors or labels on the fuse lid): your service manual explains how those must be tested. Large fuses and fusible links near the battery carry hundreds of amps; if a main fuse or link has blown, or anything in the box smells hot or looks melted, disconnect the battery negative and have a mechanic inspect the wiring before you power it up again.

What each side of a fuse is

Every fuse sits in series between a supply and a load. One tab connects to the feed, usually a busbar inside the fuse box; the other tab connects to the wire running out to the circuit it protects. Current enters on the supply side, passes through the thin metal element and leaves on the load side. Most modern blade fuses have small test points exposed on top, one per tab, so you can probe both without pulling the fuse.

When the element is intact, both tabs sit at the same potential, so you read essentially the same voltage to ground on each. When it has melted open, the supply tab still reads battery voltage, but the load tab is cut off. It reads close to 0 V, because the load connects it to ground.

Why does the dead tab read zero rather than something in between? Because the meter and the load form a voltage divider. Suppose the load is a lamp of about 2 Ω and the meter's input is 10 MΩ. With the fuse open, whatever tiny voltage leaks onto the load tab is shared between them, and the lamp's 2 Ω is five million times smaller than the meter, so the tab is held within a few microvolts of ground. Switch the lamp off and that 2 Ω path disappears; the tab now floats, and the meter can show a few volts of leakage that means nothing.

1Verify your ground first

A one-sided reading is only meaningful if your reference is solid. Clip the black lead to the battery negative post or a known-good chassis ground stud, not a painted bracket. Then touch the red probe to the battery positive post: you should see full battery voltage, typically about 12.6 V on a charged battery with the engine off. With a test light, clip it to ground and touch the battery positive; it should light brightly. If it doesn't, your ground point is the problem, and every reading after this would mislead you. Our guide to testing a car ground wire covers finding a good reference.

2Probe each side of the fuse to ground

Turn the key to the position the circuit needs (many circuits only work in run) and switch on the component the fuse protects. Touch the red probe to one test point on the fuse, note the reading, then the other. You are looking for one of three patterns: both sides at battery voltage, one side at battery voltage and the other near zero, or both sides at zero.

That pair of readings, full voltage in and nothing out with the load on, is the signature of an open fuse. A pair that reads within a few hundredths of a volt of each other, such as 12.48 V and 12.46 V, means current is passing through the element normally.

3Confirm the fuse is open

If one side is live and the other is dead, pull the fuse with the circuit off and look at the element, then confirm with a continuity check across its two blades. An intact fuse beeps and reads close to 0 Ω (most of what you see is your test leads); a reading of OL confirms the element is open. See what OL means on a multimeter and our general guide to testing a fuse with a multimeter. If you prefer to keep the fuse in place, the across-the-fuse voltage method gives the same answer by reading the difference between the two tabs directly.

4Check whether the fuse is ignition-switched or relay-fed

Not every fuse is hot all the time. If both sides read zero, the fuse may simply be on a supply that is not active yet. Typical feeds fall into a few groups:

Always hot (battery)

Live with the key out. Hazard lights, horn and memory circuits often sit here.

Ignition-switched

Live only in accessory or run. Dead on both sides with the key off is normal.

Relay-fed

Live only when a relay closes, often commanded by a module. Dead until the relay pulls in.

With a relay-fed fuse, power on neither side may point upstream to the relay or its control circuit rather than to the fuse. Our guide to testing an automotive relay walks through the coil and contact checks. The fuse box lid label or the wiring diagram in your service manual tells you which group each fuse belongs to. If a whole row of fuses is dead with the key on, suspect the main fuse or fusible link that feeds that busbar.

The last row catches people out. With the component switched off, nothing pulls the load side to ground firmly, so a blown fuse can show a stray, unstable reading on its dead side. Fuse ratings and the numbers behind them

Blade fuses use a standard color code, so you can check that the fuse in the slot matches the rating on the lid. The rating is the current the fuse carries continuously; circuits are designed to run well below it, so a fuse that blows is usually reacting to a fault rather than a normal load. Multiplying by system voltage gives a rough feel for what each rating protects (P = V × I):

ColorRatingPower at 12 V (V × I)Typical circuits (varies by car)

Red10 A12 × 10 = 120 WLamps, radio memory, ignition feeds
Blue15 A12 × 15 = 180 WHorn, fuel pump, power outlets
Yellow20 A12 × 20 = 240 WPower seats, sunroof, outlets
Clear / natural25 A12 × 25 = 300 WBlower motors, window circuits
Green30 A12 × 30 = 360 WFans, larger motors, defrosters

An intact fuse is a very small resistance, not a perfect conductor. Fuse makers publish a maximum voltage drop at rated current, and for blade fuses it is typically in the tens of millivolts to around 0.1 V or so, depending on rating and size. As a worked example, a fuse with about 10 mΩ of element resistance carrying 5 A drops 5 × 0.010 = 0.05 V, which is why the two tabs of a healthy fuse read within a few hundredths of a volt of each other. Some meters, such as the Power Probe DM300AUTO below, use exactly this drop to estimate the current through a fuse without pulling it.

Both sides live, but the circuit still doesn't work

If both tabs read battery voltage with the component switched on, the fuse is doing its job and the fault is further along: an open wire, a failed switch, a bad connector or a missing ground at the component itself. Back-probe the component's feed and ground terminals while it is switched on, and use a voltage drop test on the feed and ground paths to find a connection that reads fine unloaded but fails under current.

Frequently asked questions

If only one side of the fuse has power, is the fuse definitely blown?

Only if the side with power is the supply side and the circuit is switched on. If power appears on the input tab and nothing on the output tab, the element is open and the fuse needs replacing. Confirm by pulling it and checking continuity before you buy parts, and look for the short that blew it.

Why do some fuses have no power on either side with the key off?

Many fuses are fed through the ignition switch or a relay, so their supply bus is dead until the key is in run or accessory, or until the module energizes the relay. Check the fuse box lid or the wiring diagram to see which fuses are always hot and which are switched before assuming anything has failed.

Can a test light give a different answer than a multimeter on a fuse?

Yes. A meter has very high input impedance, so it can show voltage through a corroded or nearly open connection that cannot carry real current. A test light draws a small current, so a weak feed may make it glow dimly or not at all. If the two disagree, suspect a high-resistance connection upstream of the fuse.

Why did the new fuse blow as soon as I put it in?

A replacement fuse that opens immediately, or as soon as a switch is turned on, points to a short to ground somewhere downstream, such as a chafed wire, a failed motor or a water-damaged connector. Never fit a higher-rated fuse to stop it blowing; that moves the weak point to the wiring. Find the short instead.

Why does the dead side of a blown fuse show a few volts on my meter?

With the load switched off, nothing holds the load side firmly at ground, so the meter's 10 MΩ input picks up leakage through modules, indicator bulbs or moisture and shows a few volts that wander. It is not usable power. Switch the load on, or load the tab with a test light, and the reading should drop to zero.

Can a fuse look fine but still be blown?

Yes. Blade fuses sometimes crack at the leg where it joins the element, or open inside a cloudy housing you cannot see through, so a visual check is not proof. Mini and low-profile fuses are especially hard to read by eye. Use the test points or a continuity check: an intact fuse beeps and reads close to 0 Ω, a blown one reads OL.
