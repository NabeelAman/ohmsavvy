How to Check for Voltage Fluctuations in Your Home

To check for voltage fluctuations in your home, start by writing down when the symptoms happen, then take spot readings at a few receptacles, and finally record the voltage over time with a Min/Max function, a logging meter or a plug-in monitor. The pattern you capture is what separates a supply problem the utility must fix from a wiring fault inside the house that needs an electrician. Every measurement here is taken at ordinary 120 V receptacles; nothing involves opening the panel or the meter.

Measure safely at the receptacle only

  • Use a meter rated CAT III 600V or higher, with undamaged leads and probe guards. Wear safety glasses and keep your fingers behind the guards.
  • Do not remove the panel cover, open the meter base or touch the service wires. Those stay live and are for a licensed electrician or the utility.
  • If you smell burning, see scorching at a receptacle or notice lights going very bright, switch off sensitive equipment and call an electrician; for a fire, call 911.

What counts as a voltage fluctuation

"Fluctuation" covers several different events, and each one points somewhere different. A sag is a short drop, from a cycle to a few seconds, usually when something large starts. A swell is the opposite, a short rise, often when a heavy load switches off. A sustained low or high voltage lasts minutes or hours and tends to track the time of day. Flicker is repeated small changes you can see in the lights. Finally there are imbalances, where the two 120 V halves of the service move in opposite directions, which is a classic sign of a neutral problem.

For reference, a US 120 V circuit supplied within ANSI C84.1 Range A sits at roughly 114 to 126 V, and the 240 V supply at roughly 228 to 252 V. Readings briefly outside that band during a motor start are not unusual. Readings that sit outside it for long periods, or swing widely without an obvious cause, are worth chasing.

1Record the pattern before measuring

A week of notes is often more useful than an hour with a meter. Keep a simple list with the date, time, which lights or devices reacted, what was running, and the weather. Within a few days most problems fall into one of three shapes:

  • Tied to one appliance. The dip happens when the same thing starts, every time. Look at that appliance's circuit and its startup current.
  • Tied to the clock. Low in the late afternoon and evening, normal at night. That usually reflects demand on the utility side.
  • Random or getting worse. No clear trigger, possibly with warm outlets or odd smells. Treat as a connection fault and move quickly.

Ask a neighbor on the same street whether their lights do the same. Shared symptoms point outward, to the utility.

2Spot-check several receptacles

Set the meter to AC volts and, at a receptacle, read hot to neutral (the narrow slot to the wide one), hot to ground, and neutral to ground. Hot to neutral is the supply voltage. Neutral to ground is normally close to zero with no load and rises a little when the circuit carries current; a few volts under heavy load can be ordinary, but a large or unsteady value suggests a poor neutral connection somewhere on that run.

Repeat on circuits in different parts of the house, ideally including circuits on both legs of the service. You can tell which leg is which only from the panel directory or an electrician's labeling, but in practice comparing a kitchen, a bedroom and a garage receptacle usually covers both. If one group reads a few volts high while another reads low, and the gap widens when a large 120 V load turns on, stop there and call for help. Our multimeter basics guide covers lead placement, and unstable multimeter readings helps rule out the meter itself when numbers jump.

3Capture Min/Max and logged readings

A spot reading only shows the moment you look. A meter with Min/Max holds the lowest and highest values it saw while recording, so you can start it, trigger the suspect appliance, and read back the extremes. A logging meter goes further and stores a timestamped series you can graph, which is the best evidence when the problem follows the clock. Stay with the meter while the probes are in a receptacle, keep leads where nobody can trip on them, and use probe tips that seat firmly in the slots. For recordings of many hours, a plug-in monitor, below, is the safer choice.

Keep in mind the limits. Min/Max on a general-purpose handheld responds at the meter's update speed, so a dip lasting a cycle or two may not appear at its full depth. Check the manual for a fast peak or response-time figure. If you can see the lights flicker but the meter shows nothing, the event is probably faster than it can capture.

4Leave a plug-in voltage monitor running

A plug-in voltage monitor goes into an ordinary receptacle, shows the live reading and, depending on the model, records the high and low or logs over time. Because nothing is exposed, it can safely run for days, which suits problems that come back at random. Move it between a few rooms over a couple of weeks: if every location shows the same evening sag, the cause is upstream; if only one circuit misbehaves, the cause is in that circuit. Treat its absolute accuracy as a rough guide and confirm anything surprising with a meter.

Utility or in-home cause?

The evidence usually sorts itself. Symptoms that hit every room at once, follow the time of day, appear in bad weather, or affect neighbors point to the supply: the transformer, the service drop or the meter connection. Symptoms confined to one circuit, triggered by a particular appliance, or accompanied by warm receptacles point to the house wiring. A neutral problem can sit on either side of the meter, which is why it is the one pattern that needs both parties to look quickly.

If the whole-house dip coincides with the air conditioner or a fridge compressor starting, the startup current is the trigger. Our page on lights flickering when the refrigerator or AC starts explains how much of that is normal. If part of the house loses power altogether, see why half the house can lose power.

When to call an electrician or the utility

Call the electric utility when fluctuations affect the whole house, follow the time of day, show up at neighbors' homes too, or arrive with storm damage to the overhead line. Call a licensed electrician when a single circuit sags badly, a receptacle is warm or discolored, or breakers trip alongside the dips. Call either one the same day if lights go noticeably brighter or one half of the house rises while the other falls, and unplug sensitive electronics until it is fixed.

Frequently asked questions

What voltage should a US outlet read?

Homes are supplied at a nominal 120 V per leg and 240 V across both legs. ANSI C84.1 Range A, the band utilities generally aim to hold at the service, is plus or minus 5 percent, about 114 to 126 V on a 120 V circuit. Readings at a receptacle can sit a little lower than at the service because of wiring drop under load.

Can a multimeter catch a quick flicker?

Sometimes. Min/Max on many handheld meters records at its own response speed, so a dip lasting a fraction of a second may be smoothed out or missed. Check your meter's manual for its Min/Max response time, and treat a clean Min/Max as 'nothing slow happened' rather than proof of a perfect supply.

Some outlets read high and others low at the same time. Is that serious?

Yes. When readings on one set of circuits rise while others fall, especially as loads switch on, a loose or broken neutral is a common cause. It can damage electronics and overheat connections. Turn off sensitive equipment and call the utility or an electrician the same day.

Will the utility come out to check my voltage?

Most utilities investigate voltage complaints, and many will install a recorder at the meter. Dates, times and readings from your own log make the request more specific and help them decide where to look.

Will a whole-house surge protector stop voltage fluctuations?

No. A surge protective device clamps short, high-energy spikes such as those from lightning or utility switching. It does nothing about sags when a motor starts, a supply that sits high or low for minutes, or swings caused by a loose neutral. Those need the cause found and fixed. A surge protector is still worth having, but alongside the diagnosis, not instead of it.
