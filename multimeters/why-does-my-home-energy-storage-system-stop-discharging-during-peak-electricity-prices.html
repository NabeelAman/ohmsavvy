Why Does My Home Energy Storage System Stop Discharging During Peak Electricity Prices?

When a home energy storage system stops discharging during peak electricity prices, the cause is far more often a setting or a built-in limit than a broken battery. The usual reasons are a backup reserve that the battery has already reached, an operating mode that isn't built around time-of-use rates, a schedule entered with the wrong hours or season, an export rule that keeps the battery from sending power to the grid, or a state-of-charge or temperature limit set by the battery management system. Nearly all of it can be checked from the system's app and your utility account, without going near the hardware. This guide walks through those checks in the order that finds the answer fastest.

Your installer can confirm what the battery and grid are really doing with power-measuring tools; the owner's best instrument is the app plus the utility's interval data. Related: reading smart meter data.

Settings are yours to check; the hardware is not

  • Home batteries store a large amount of energy at high DC voltage, and the inverter holds dangerous voltage even when it looks idle. Never open the battery enclosure, the inverter cover, the gateway or any disconnect, and never try to reset a battery by unplugging cables.
  • Swelling, hissing, a chemical or burning smell, smoke, or a fault light paired with heat: leave the area, call 911, and tell the dispatcher there is a home battery. Contact the installer afterwards.
  • Change only the options the app presents to the owner. Anything locked behind an installer login, such as export limits or grid-code settings, should be changed by the installer.

Is it stopping, or just not covering everything?

Open the app's power-flow or history view for a recent peak period and look at three lines: battery power, home consumption and grid import. If the battery shows zero output while the house is pulling from the grid, it has genuinely stopped. If the battery is discharging at a steady figure but the grid is still supplying part of the load, it is working at its power limit, and the home is simply asking for more than the inverter can deliver. That second case is common when an oven, electric dryer, EV charger and air conditioner overlap with the peak hours.

Also check the state of charge at the moment discharge ended. A battery that stops at a round number such as 20% or 30% is meeting a reserve setting. One that stops near empty ran out of energy earlier than you expected. One that stops partway with plenty of charge left, at a precise clock time, is following a schedule.

Reserve, modes and schedules

Backup reserve. Most systems let you set a backup reserve, the share of capacity kept for an outage. The higher it is, the less energy is available for peak shaving. A reserve set to 100% turns the battery into a pure backup device that will not discharge at all while the grid is up. Raising it before a forecast storm and forgetting to lower it afterwards is a frequent cause of a battery that suddenly stops helping with the bill.

Operating mode. Systems generally offer some version of three modes: backup only, self-consumption (store surplus solar and use it whenever the house needs it), and a time-based or cost-saving mode that tries to discharge when import prices are highest. Only the third is designed around time-of-use pricing. Exact names differ by manufacturer, so read the description in your own app rather than going by the label.

Time-of-use schedule. A time-based mode is only as good as the rate plan it was given. Check the peak start and end times, the weekday and weekend split, summer and winter seasons, holidays and the time zone. Utilities revise rate plans, and a schedule entered at installation may not match the plan you are on now. Some apps let you pick the tariff from a list; if yours does, confirm it matches the plan name on your latest bill.

Export, power and temperature limits

Grid-export rules. Many interconnection agreements allow solar export but not battery export, and some installations are set to zero export. In those cases the battery only discharges to match the home's own consumption. On a mild evening when the house uses little, the battery may barely move during an expensive peak, which looks like a fault but is the system obeying its permission. Rules vary between US utilities and across European grid operators, which may impose their own feed-in limits, so the installer and utility are the authority here.

Inverter and backup limits. Every battery inverter has a continuous discharge rating. When the home load exceeds it, the grid makes up the difference. Some backup configurations also feed only a protected-loads subpanel, so large appliances on the main panel draw from the grid no matter how full the battery is.

State-of-charge and temperature limits. The battery management system protects the cells with lower state-of-charge cutoffs that sit beneath your reserve, and it reduces charge or discharge power when cell temperature is outside its preferred range. Lithium iron phosphate packs in particular restrict charging near freezing. A battery that didn't fully charge the day before because of cold or a charge limit around 80% will naturally run short during the peak.

1Checks in the app and utility data

  1. Note the current mode, backup reserve and any active storm, outage or program event. Look for firmware update notices, since an update can reset settings.
  2. Compare the schedule's peak window with the rate plan on your bill, including season and weekend rules.
  3. Pull up the history for a day with the problem. Record state of charge at the start of the peak, the time discharge stopped and the battery's maximum output during the window.
  4. Download the utility's interval data for the same day and confirm grid import during the peak matches what the app claims. A big disagreement suggests a monitoring or sensor problem.
  5. Late at night, with solar producing nothing, check that the app's home consumption is a sensible positive number. Negative or wildly varying values point to a current-sensor installation issue.

2Measurements and their limits

Unlike a tripping breaker or a dead outlet, this problem rarely needs a meter in the owner's hands. The current sensors and DC wiring that would answer it sit inside the panel, the gateway or the battery cabinet. What you can measure safely is individual plug-in appliances, with a plug-in energy monitor or a clamp meter on a line splitter, to see which loads push the house past the inverter's output during the peak. Shifting one of those, such as a dryer cycle or EV charging session, often fixes "the battery isn't covering the peak" outright.

When the app and the utility data disagree, the installer can verify actual power flows with a clamp that measures watts on the AC side, a DC clamp on the battery conductors, or a logging meter left in place across a peak window. Those measurements happen inside equipment that only qualified people should open. For safe clamp technique on accessible cords, see how to measure AC current with a clamp meter; for cell-level health questions, see testing a 48V LiFePO4 battery for cell imbalance.

When to call the installer, the utility or the manufacturer

Call the installer if the app shows faults or temperature warnings that persist, if home consumption reads negative, if export settings need to change, if settings you need are locked, or if the battery stops well above its reserve with no schedule to explain it. Call the utility to confirm your rate plan, export permission and any demand-response program that may control the battery. Contact the manufacturer, usually through the installer, for firmware problems or a battery that loses usable capacity quickly. Anything involving heat, smell or swelling is an emergency, not a settings question.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my battery sit at 20% or 30% and refuse to go lower?

That is almost certainly the backup reserve, the share of capacity the system holds back for outages. Once the battery reaches it, normal discharge stops and the grid supplies the house. Most systems let the owner lower the reserve in the app, though utility programs, installers or warranty terms sometimes set a minimum.

My battery was full at noon but empty before the peak started. Why?

In a self-consumption mode, the battery covers the house whenever solar falls short, so cloudy afternoons, cooking and air conditioning can drain it before the expensive window arrives. A time-based mode that saves energy for the peak hours, or a later start time on the schedule, keeps the charge for when it is worth the most.

Can I make the battery export to the grid during peak prices?

Only if your interconnection agreement and tariff allow battery export and the system is configured for it. Many installations are permitted to export solar but not stored energy, or are limited to zero export. Ask your installer and utility what your permission covers before changing anything; exporting without approval can breach the agreement.

Does cold weather stop a home battery from discharging?

Cold mainly limits charging, but the battery management system can also reduce discharge power at low or high cell temperatures, and many systems run heaters or fans that use some of the stored energy. Batteries in unheated garages or outdoors show this most. The app often reports a temperature-related derate as a warning or reduced power figure.

How long should a home battery cover the evening peak?

Divide the energy available above your backup reserve by the average household draw during the peak. As an illustrative example, 10 kWh above reserve feeding a steady 2.5 kW load lasts about four hours, while the same battery against a 5 kW load with cooking and air conditioning running lasts about two. If the peak window is longer than that, running out early is expected, not a fault.
