HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Test a Coolant Temperature Sensor With a Multimeter

To test a coolant temperature sensor with a multimeter, unplug it and measure the resistance across its two pins cold, then again with the engine warm: the resistance should fall smoothly as the coolant heats, from thousands of ohms at room temperature to a couple of hundred ohms near operating temperature. Then plug it back in and back-probe the signal wire in DC volts, which should drop from near the top of the 0 to 5 V range toward under about 1 V as the engine warms. This guide covers the common two-pin engine coolant temperature (ECT) sensor that feeds the engine computer, the wiring faults behind P0117 and P0118, and how it differs from the single-wire gauge sender.

Hot coolant and a running engine

  • Never open the radiator or expansion tank cap on a hot engine. Pressurized coolant above its boiling point can spray out and scald you.
  • When back-probing with the engine running, route the leads away from the belts, fan and exhaust manifold, and remember an electric cooling fan can start without warning even with the engine off.
  • Back-probe from the rear of the connector with thin pins. Don't pierce the insulation, which lets water in and corrodes the wire over time.
  • If the temperature gauge is in the red or the engine is boiling over, stop driving. That is an overheating problem first and a sensor question second.

How a coolant temperature sensor works

The engine coolant temperature (ECT) sensor is a brass or plastic plug screwed into a coolant passage, usually near the thermostat housing or in the cylinder head. Inside is a negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor: its resistance falls as it gets hotter. The engine computer feeds it a regulated 5 V reference through a fixed resistor inside the computer, and reads the voltage at the point between that resistor and the sensor. A cold sensor has high resistance, so the signal sits high; a hot one has low resistance and pulls the signal low.

The computer uses that number to decide fuel enrichment on a cold start, idle speed, ignition timing, when to run the cooling fans and, on automatic transmissions, when to allow torque-converter lockup. That is why a sensor that reads wrong can cause so many different symptoms, and why a multimeter test of both the sensor and its wiring is worth doing before buying parts. The thermistor theory is covered in more depth in how to test a thermistor; this page sticks to the car.

1Find the right sensor

Look for a two-wire connector on a sensor threaded into the coolant path near the thermostat housing. On many engines a second, single-wire sender sits nearby for the dashboard gauge, and some engines carry two two-wire sensors, one for the computer and one for the gauge or a separate module. The service manual or a parts catalog diagram settles which is which. Note the wire colors at the connector before you start, because you will need the signal and ground positions for step 4.

2Measure resistance with the engine cold

With the engine off and fully cold, ideally overnight, unplug the connector. Set the meter to ohms and place the probes on the two sensor pins; polarity doesn't matter on a thermistor. Write down the reading and the air temperature. On the common GM-style chart, 68 °F corresponds to about 3,520 Ω. A reading of OL means the thermistor is open, and a reading near 0 Ω means it is shorted. Either one is a failed sensor.

3Watch the resistance as the engine warms

A sensor can read correctly cold and still be wrong hot. Plug it back in, run the engine to operating temperature, switch off and unplug again, then measure quickly. Near 194 °F the GM chart gives about 241 Ω, and at 176 °F about 332 Ω. If you have an infrared thermometer, aim it at the thermostat housing so you compare like with like. What you're looking for is a smooth, steady fall in resistance with no sudden jumps to OL. On the bench, the same check works with the sensor tip in a pan of heated water and a cooking thermometer beside it.

4Check the 5 V reference and the sensor ground

With the connector still unplugged, turn the key on, engine off. Set the meter to DC volts and measure from the harness signal pin to a clean engine ground. Most systems show about 5 V here, the computer's reference through its pull-up resistor. Then measure from the signal pin to the harness ground pin; you should see the same 5 V. If the signal-to-engine reading is good but signal-to-harness-ground is low or zero, the sensor ground wire is open or high in resistance.

No 5 V at all points to a broken signal wire, a chafed wire shorted to ground, or a computer problem. With the battery disconnected, a continuity check from the harness pin to the matching computer pin finds an open wire; see how to find a broken wire with a multimeter. Corroded connector pins are common on older cars, so inspect them for green deposits before condemning anything else.

5Back-probe the signal with the engine running

Reconnect the sensor and back-probe the signal wire with the black lead on the sensor ground. Start the engine from cold and watch the DC volts. On most vehicles the voltage starts high, often somewhere around 3 to 4 V on a cool morning, and falls steadily as the engine warms, typically ending well under 1 V once the thermostat opens. A meter with a bar graph or min/max recording makes it easy to catch a dropout, which shows as a sudden spike toward 5 V.

6Match the reading to the trouble code

The generic OBD-II codes (SAE J2012) tell you which direction the computer saw. P0117 is a low input, so the circuit looks too hot: a shorted sensor or a signal wire touching ground. P0118 is a high input, which looks too cold: an open sensor, a broken signal wire or a lost ground. P0116 is range or performance, where the reading is plausible but doesn't agree with other temperatures, and P0128 points to the thermostat.

High volts means cold, low volts means hot

The computer only sees a voltage. An open circuit reads like an engine at the North Pole and a short reads like one boiling over, so test the wiring before replacing a sensor that may be telling the truth.

Coolant sensor resistance vs temperature

The chart below uses values from the widely published GM ECT and intake air temperature sensor table. Many other makers use similar curves, but not identical ones, so treat it as typical and check your manual for the exact figures. The scale is logarithmic, because resistance changes by roughly a factor of 20 between a cool morning and full operating temperature.

Because the curve is so steep, a few degrees of difference between your thermometer and the sensor tip moves the reading a lot at the cold end. Judge the shape, not a single number: a good sensor falls smoothly and lands in the right neighborhood at each temperature. If a high-resistance ground connection is suspected, the methods in how to test a car ground wire apply here too.

Which multimeter to use on car sensors

Any digital meter with ohms and DC volts can do the static tests. For the warm-up sweep, a responsive bar graph and min/max recording help you catch a brief dropout that a slow digital display would miss. Our automotive multimeter guide compares more options.

Frequently asked questions

What should a coolant temperature sensor read in ohms?

It depends on the maker's chart, but most engine coolant sensors are NTC thermistors that read a few thousand ohms at room temperature and a couple of hundred ohms near 200 °F. A widely published GM chart lists about 3,520 Ω at 68 °F and 177 Ω at 212 °F. Use the service manual chart for your exact engine when you have it.

Can I test a coolant temperature sensor without removing it?

Yes. Unplug the connector and measure across the two sensor pins while the sensor stays in the engine, cold and then warm. For a live check, leave it plugged in and back-probe the signal wire in DC volts with the key on. Removing the sensor is only needed for a bench test in heated water against a thermometer.

What voltage should a coolant temperature sensor have?

Unplugged with the key on, the harness signal pin on most vehicles shows about 5 V, which is the computer's reference through its pull-up resistor. Plugged in, the signal sits high when the engine is cold and falls as it warms, typically to well under 1 V at full operating temperature. Check your service manual for exact figures.

What are the symptoms of a bad coolant temperature sensor?

Hard starting when warm, black smoke or a rich smell, poor fuel economy, a high idle that never settles, cooling fans running all the time or never, and a check engine light with codes P0115 to P0118 or P0128. A sensor stuck reading cold makes the computer add fuel; one stuck reading hot leans the mixture on a cold start.

Is the coolant temperature sensor the same as the temperature gauge sender?

Not always. Many older vehicles have a separate single-wire sender for the dashboard gauge and a two-wire sensor for the engine computer. Newer cars often use one ECT sensor and send the reading to the cluster over the data network. If the gauge works but the engine runs rich, test the two-wire computer sensor, not the gauge sender.

Can a bad thermostat cause a coolant temperature sensor code?

Yes. P0128 means the coolant took too long to reach the regulating temperature, which is usually a thermostat stuck open rather than a failed sensor. Before replacing the sensor, check whether the upper radiator hose gets hot early in the warm-up; if it does, the thermostat is opening too soon and the sensor is telling the truth.
