Fluke 117 vs Fluke 115

The Fluke 117 and Fluke 115 have identical core specs: True RMS, CAT III 600V, a 6,000-count display with bar graph, 10A current, capacitance to 9,999 µF and frequency, all with the same accuracy. The 117 adds three features for electricians: VoltAlert non-contact voltage detection, a low-impedance (LoZ) input that suppresses ghost voltage, and AutoVolt automatic AC/DC selection. Same body, same battery, same 3-year warranty; the choice is whether you'll use those three extras.

Where Fluke's own documents disagree

The comparison table on Fluke's 115 and 117 product pages lists frequency only under the 117 and gives a temperature range for both. The datasheet and manual list frequency on both meters and temperature on the 116 only, so we follow those. Fluke's 2020 manual also adds a 99.99 kHz frequency range that the datasheet and spec tabs (50.00 kHz top range) don't show. Finally, the manual lists a low-impedance capacitance power-up option on the 115 and 117, while the datasheet lists it for the 114, 116 and 117, and the 115 page says Low Z is exclusive to the 117. None of this changes the main point: the 115 has no LoZ voltage check.

How LoZ drains ghost voltage

A disconnected conductor running beside a live one picks up voltage through the stray capacitance between them. That coupling can push only a tiny current, but a meter with a 5 MΩ input barely loads it, so the display shows a large, believable number on a wire that can't power anything. This is the classic open neutral or abandoned-run trap. The 117's AutoVolt position drops the input to about 3 kΩ, which drains the coupled charge so a phantom reading collapses toward zero while a real supply holds up.

These readouts are worked arithmetic, not measurements. Assume 300 pF of coupling on a 120 V, 60 Hz circuit: that capacitance has a reactance of about 8.8 MΩ, so a 5 MΩ input sees about 59 V, while a 3 kΩ input sees about 0.04 V. Real coupling varies with cable length and spacing. On a 115 you can still unmask a phantom by adding a load, as our guide to measuring voltage on a floating circuit and spotting phantom voltage explains, but the 117 does it at the turn of the dial.

How VoltAlert spots a live conductor

An energized AC conductor is surrounded by an alternating electric field even through its insulation. The 117's VoltAlert sensor sits in the top of the meter; hold that edge near a cord, outlet or wire and it lights a red LED and beeps when it senses AC voltage. Fluke gives two sensitivity settings: Lo for flush wall sockets, power strips and cords, and Hi for recessed sockets, where it works on bare wire down to 24 V. The 115 has no sensor, so you'd carry a separate non-contact pen tester instead.

How AutoVolt decides AC or DC

On the 115 you turn the dial to AC volts or DC volts. The 117 adds a third volts position that, per Fluke's manual, selects AC or DC from whatever is applied across the leads, and sets the input to about 3 kΩ at the same time. That makes it the quick position for a mixed day of receptacles, control transformers and DC supplies.

Fluke 115 vs Fluke 117 for electricians

On a panel or a branch circuit, the two meters carry the same protection. Both are rated CAT III 600V, which covers distribution panels, feeders, fixed wiring and receptacle circuits, and neither carries a CAT IV rating, so neither belongs on the line side of the meter base or the service drop. What changes on an electrician's day is how fast each meter gets you to a trustworthy answer.

Three jobs show the gap. First, finding which cord, receptacle or fixture whip is still energized: the 117 answers from its top edge with VoltAlert before the leads come out of the pouch, while the 115 owner probes each point or carries a separate pen. Second, a reading on a conductor that should be dead, such as a switch leg or an abandoned run pulled through a pipe with live circuits: on the 117, AutoVolt's roughly 3 kΩ input tells a coupled phantom from a real backfeed; on the 115 you have to add a load across the leads to find out. Third, a control cabinet that mixes 24 V AC transformer secondaries with DC sensor supplies: the 117's AutoVolt position reads either without a dial change, while the 115 needs V AC or V DC chosen for each point.

True RMS differences explained

Both meters are True RMS: our Pick data lists "True RMS" for the Fluke 115 and the Fluke 117, and Fluke's datasheet applies it to AC volts and AC amps on both. True RMS means the meter computes the heating-equivalent value of whatever waveform is on the leads. An average-responding meter instead assumes a clean sine and scales the average, so it drifts when the waveform isn't one.

That matters on the loads electricians now meet daily. A variable-frequency drive chops its output into pulses. LED drivers and switch-mode supplies draw current in short, tall peaks near the top of each cycle rather than a smooth sine. On those circuits, the AC volts and especially the AC amps readings from either Fluke stay trustworthy within spec. The difference between the two meters is not True RMS; it is which dial position you read from.

Worked from those figures for a 277 V LED lighting circuit on the 600.0 V range, where one count is 0.1 V: the dedicated V AC position on either meter allows 1.0% of 277 (2.77 V) plus 3 counts (0.3 V), so about ±3.1 V. Two limits apply to both meters equally. The quoted bands start at 45 Hz, so a drive slowed below that runs outside the figures we cite. And neither meter lists a low-pass filter, which keeps a drive's switching edges out of the reading on the motor side; the Fluke 87V has one, which is why it leads our Fluke 117 vs Fluke 87V comparison for drive work. On the supply side of a drive, or on LED and electronic-ballast circuits, either True RMS Fluke is the right tool. Our budget True RMS multimeters guide covers lower-cost alternatives.

Fluke 115 vs 117 for home electrical testing

Most homeowners buy a meter for five jobs: checking that a receptacle has power, confirming a breaker really killed a circuit, testing appliance parts such as heating elements and fuses, checking batteries, and chasing a nuisance trip. Both meters do the measuring part of every one of those identically. The difference shows up in the two moments where home wiring most often fools people.

The first is "is it off?". After flipping a breaker, the 117 lets you sweep the receptacle or switch box with VoltAlert to confirm the circuit you killed is the one you're about to touch, then prove it with the leads. The second is the half-dead outlet that reads 30 or 40 volts: on house wiring that is often coupling from a neighboring cable, and the 117's LoZ position shows whether it is real. On a 115 you can still get there, but you need a separate non-contact tester and a load-test habit.

What neither meter does

  • No temperature. In this family only the Fluke 116 reads a thermocouple. The Fluke 179 also measures temperature with its supplied probe.
  • No mA or µA ranges. The lowest current range is 6.000 A with 1 mA resolution, too coarse for standby current or flame sensors. See our guides to measuring microamps and the best meters for microamps.
  • No logging or app. Min/Max/Avg captures extremes on the display, but neither meter stores readings or connects to a phone.
  • No CAT IV rating. Both stop at CAT III 600V. For service entrances and outdoor feeds, the Fluke 87V and Fluke 179 are rated CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V; compare them in Fluke 117 vs Fluke 87V and Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179.

Safety: NCV is a first check, not proof of dead

Fluke's own manual warns that no VoltAlert indication does not mean no voltage: shielded cable, socket design and insulation thickness can all hide a live conductor, and it cannot sense DC at all. Use the beep to find live circuits fast, then confirm with the test leads on a known live source before and after (live-dead-live) before touching anything. Both meters are CAT III 600V, which covers distribution panels, feeders and branch circuits; don't use either at the meter base or service drop, where CAT IV applies. When checking receptacles, see our guide to testing a GFCI outlet that won't reset.

Buy the Fluke 115 if your work is equipment, controls, electronics or automotive, or if you already carry a pen tester; you get the same measuring core, case and warranty. If you need temperature or microamps, skip both and look at the Fluke 116.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between the Fluke 115 and Fluke 117?+

Three things, all on the 117: VoltAlert non-contact voltage detection, a LoZ input that drains ghost voltage on shared conduit runs, and AutoVolt, which picks AC or DC automatically. Everything else — True RMS, CAT III 600V, 10A current, capacitance, frequency, accuracy, case and warranty — is identical on Fluke's own datasheet.

Is the Fluke 117 worth the extra money over the 115?+

For electricians, usually yes. The extra buys VoltAlert non-contact detection, AutoVolt and a LoZ input that stops ghost voltage fooling you on shared runs. If your work is equipment repair, electronics or automotive, those features sit unused and the 115 measures everything else identically. Prices move on Amazon, so compare the live gap before deciding.

Does the Fluke 115 have LoZ?+

No. Fluke lists AutoVolt with its roughly 3 kΩ low-impedance input on the 114, 116 and 117, not the 115. Fluke's 2020 manual does mention a low-impedance capacitance power-up option on the 115, but that is for capacitance, not voltage. To unmask a ghost voltage on a 115, add a load across the leads.

Can the Fluke 117 measure temperature?+

No. Fluke's datasheet lists the K-type thermocouple function, -40 °C to 400 °C, for the Fluke 116 only. The comparison table on Fluke's product pages shows a temperature range for the 115 and 117 too, but the accuracy table and manual don't, so don't buy either for temperature work.

Is the Fluke 115 good for electricians?+

It is capable: True RMS, CAT III 600V, 10A current and the same accuracy as the 117. What it lacks is the LoZ check, so a disconnected conductor in a shared conduit can show a convincing phantom voltage. Pair it with a separate non-contact tester and know how to load-test a reading, or buy the 117.

What is the difference between the Fluke 116 and 117?+

The 116 is Fluke's HVAC model: it adds a K-type thermocouple input and a 600.0 µA range for flame sensors, but has no 6A or 10A current ranges and no VoltAlert. The 117 keeps the amp ranges and adds non-contact voltage detection. Both have AutoVolt with a LoZ input and the same CAT III 600V rating.

Can the Fluke 117 measure milliamps?+

Only coarsely. Fluke says the 117 has a 10 A current range for AC or DC with a maximum resolution of 1 milliamp, which comes from its 6.000 A range. There is no dedicated mA or µA input, so it can't resolve small currents like standby draw or flame-sensor signals. The 115 is the same.

Are the Fluke 115 and 117 both True RMS?+

Yes. Both read AC volts and AC amps as True RMS, with the same accuracy: ±(1.0% + 3) on AC volts and ±(1.5% + 3) on AC amps from 45 Hz to 500 Hz. That keeps readings trustworthy on variable-speed drives, LED drivers and other distorted waveforms where an average-responding meter drifts.

Which is better for HVAC, the Fluke 115 or 117?+

The 117, narrowly. Its LoZ input helps on long thermostat and control runs where ghost voltage is common, and both share the same capacitance range for run capacitors. Neither has µA or temperature, though, so many HVAC technicians are better served by the Fluke 116 or an HVAC clamp meter.

Do the Fluke 115 and 117 have the same accuracy?+

Yes, for every shared function. Fluke's datasheet gives one accuracy line for both: DC volts ±(0.5% + 2), resistance ±(0.9% + 1), capacitance ±(1.9% + 2) and frequency ±(0.1% + 2). The only extra line on the 117 is AutoVolt, which is looser at ±(2.0% + 3) because it trades precision for speed.

Is the Fluke 117 more accurate than the 115 on VFD and LED circuits?+

No. Both are True RMS with the same AC accuracy, ±(1.0% + 3) on volts and ±(1.5% + 3) on amps from 45 to 500 Hz. The only AC line that differs is the 117's AutoVolt position at ±(2.0% + 3), which is looser. Neither lists a low-pass filter for a drive's motor side; for that, look at the Fluke 87V.

Can an electrician use the Fluke 115 or 117 in a panel?+

Yes. Both are rated CAT III 600V, which covers distribution panels, feeders and branch circuits. Neither is rated CAT IV, so keep both off the meter base and service drop. The 117 is quicker in a panel because VoltAlert finds live conductors without the leads and its LoZ AutoVolt position separates ghost voltage from a real backfeed.

Is the Fluke 115 enough for a homeowner?+

For appliances, cords, batteries and reading outlets with the leads, yes: it measures everything the 117 does, with the same accuracy. If you will open switch and receptacle boxes yourself, the 117's VoltAlert and LoZ make the 'is it really off?' check faster and harder to get wrong. Either way, prove dead with the leads before touching.

Can the Fluke 117 measure a space heater's current?+

Not a typical 1,500 W heater on 120 V. That draws about 12.5 A, above the 117's 10.00 A range, and Fluke allows 20 A for only 30 seconds. The 115 has the same limit. Measuring in series also means opening the circuit, so a clamp meter is the safer, easier tool for appliance current at home.
