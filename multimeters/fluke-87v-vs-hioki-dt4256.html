Fluke 87V vs Hioki DT4256: Which Is Better for Industrial Work?

Both are CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V True RMS meters with low-pass filters for drive outputs, so either belongs in an industrial bag. Buy the Hioki DT4256 for panel and instrumentation work: its AUTO V input picks AC or DC at low impedance, it shows a 4–20 mA loop as a percentage, and it cost about a third of the 87V on Amazon when we checked. Pay for the Fluke 87V if you need microamps, temperature, peak capture or DC accuracy six times tighter.

A control panel with 24 V DC and 120 V AC: AUTO V against a standard input

A typical machine panel has a 24 V DC control supply, 120 V AC coils and long runs of multi-conductor cable between them. Two things go wrong for a technician there: probing a DC terminal with the meter set to AC (or the reverse), and chasing a coupled phantom voltage on a conductor that has been switched off. The DT4256's AUTO V position handles both. Hioki specifies it at 900 kΩ input impedance, it detects AC or DC by itself, and its accuracy is ±(2.0% + 3 digits) on a 600.0 V range.

Fluke lists no low-impedance mode for the 87V, so on the same conductor it reads through its normal high-impedance input. One caution on the Hioki: 900 kΩ is a much lighter load than the roughly 3 kΩ LoZ inputs on electricians' meters such as the Fluke 117. How far a given phantom voltage collapses depends on how strongly the conductor is coupled, so we can't promise AUTO V will pull every ghost reading to zero. It also has a built-in AC voltage detector (Hi 40–600 V, Lo 80–600 V), which the 87V doesn't list.

A 4–20 mA level transmitter: percent display against tighter milliamps

Hioki gives the DT4256 a percentage display for 4–20 mA signals: 4 mA shows as 0% and 20 mA as 100%, alongside the milliamp value on the dual display. On a tank level transmitter reading 12.00 mA, the second line simply says 50%, which saves the conversion on every loop you check. Fluke's 87V shows 12.00 mA and leaves the arithmetic to you.

Accuracy runs the other way. The 87V's 60 mA range at ±(0.2% + 4 digits) allows ±(0.024 + 0.04) = ±0.064 mA on that reading. Hioki's brochure table gives the 60.00 mA range ±(0.8% + 5), which works out to ±(0.096 + 0.05) = ±0.15 mA. The same table carries a DT4256-only footnote of ±(1.8% + 15 digits), and the layout doesn't make clear which range it modifies. If it applies to 60 mA, the band widens to ±(0.216 + 0.15) = ±0.37 mA. We show both rather than choose.

Even the wider Hioki band is about 2.3% of a 16 mA span, enough to diagnose a stuck or drifting transmitter but not to sign off a calibration. For loop calibrators that also source current, see our 4–20 mA loop testing picks.

Drive outputs, microamps and temperature: what the 87V's premium buys

Both meters filter. Fluke's analog filter on the 87V is 3 dB down at 1 kHz. Hioki's digital filter has a 100 Hz or 500 Hz pass-band, and Hioki states that with the filter on, accuracy isn't specified at or above that frequency. For a motor running between roughly 30 and 60 Hz, the 100 Hz setting covers the fundamental on paper; for higher-speed spindles, check the drive's output frequency against the pass-band first. We go deeper on the 87V's drive measurements in Fluke 87V vs Fluke 179.

The bigger gaps are functions the Hioki doesn't have. The 87V resolves 0.01 µA on DC current, reads temperature from -200 to 1090 °C with a suitable probe, captures peaks as short as 250 µs and offers a 19,999-count mode. The DT4256 has no microamp range, no temperature input and no peak capture listed. Fluke also backs the 87V with a lifetime warranty against Hioki's three years, and quotes about 400 hours from one 9 V cell against about 130 hours from the Hioki's four AAAs.

For panel builders, maintenance electricians and instrument techs who don't need microamps or temperature, the DT4256 does the daily work for a third of the price. Rated for the service entrance, not for shortcuts

Both meters are rated CAT IV 600V and CAT III 1000V, which covers motor control centers, switchgear and the service entrance when used with leads of the same rating. Neither is rated above 1000 V, so check PV and drive DC bus nameplates first. Follow lockout/tagout and arc-flash PPE requirements, and leave utility-side work to qualified personnel.

Choose the Fluke 87V if…

  • ✓You measure microamps, such as flame sensors or leakage, down to 0.01 µA
  • ✓You need temperature, 250 µs peak capture or a 19,999-count mode
  • ✓You verify supplies and transmitters against tight DC tolerances

Choose the Hioki DT4256 if…

  • ✓You work in control panels where 24 V DC and 120 V AC sit side by side
  • ✓You check 4–20 mA loops and want the reading shown as a percentage of span
  • ✓You want a low-impedance mode, which Fluke doesn't list on the 87V

Neither if…

The bottom line

For industrial panel and instrumentation work the Hioki DT4256 is the better value: the same CAT IV 600V rating and a drive filter, plus AUTO V, voltage detection and a 4–20 mA percent display, at about a third of the price. Choose the Fluke 87V if you need microamps, temperature, peak capture, tighter DC accuracy or a lifetime warranty.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Hioki DT4256 have a low-pass filter for VFDs?+

Yes. Hioki gives the DT4256 a digital low-pass filter with a 100 Hz or 500 Hz pass-band, and states that accuracy isn't specified at or above that frequency with the filter on. The Fluke 87V's analog filter is rated 3 dB at 1 kHz. Both can read a drive's fundamental output voltage; check your drive's output frequency against the Hioki setting.

Does the Fluke 87V have a LoZ mode?+

Fluke doesn't list a low-impedance mode for the 87V. The Hioki DT4256 has AUTO V, a 900 kΩ input that also selects AC or DC automatically. That load is lighter than the roughly 3 kΩ LoZ found on meters such as the Fluke 117, so it may not collapse every phantom voltage, but the 87V offers no equivalent at all.

Can the Hioki DT4256 measure microamps?+

No. Hioki's DC current ranges on the DT4256 start at 60.00 mA, and AC current starts at 600.0 mA, so flame-sensor or leakage currents in microamps are out of reach. The Fluke 87V resolves 0.01 µA on DC current. If microamps are routine work, the 87V or a meter with a dedicated µA input is the better choice.

What does the DT4256's 4–20 mA percent display do?+

It converts a loop current into a percentage of span, with 4 mA shown as 0% and 20 mA as 100%, on the meter's second display line next to the milliamp value. A 12 mA reading appears as 50%. Hioki lists the function only for the DT4253 and DT4256 in the DT4200 series. The 87V shows milliamps without a percentage conversion.

Is the Hioki DT4256 waterproof?+

No. Hioki rates it IP40 when operating and IP42 in storage, which covers dust larger than 1 mm but not water while in use. It is also listed as drop proof, without a stated height. Fluke's comparison table lists the 87V at IP30. For a sealed meter, the Fluke 87V MAX and Extech EX505A are both rated IP67.

Can the DT4256 measure motor current above 10 A?+

Through its 10 A input, no. Hioki gives the DT4256 an AC clamp function that reads 10 A to 1000 A with an optional 9010-50, 9018-50 or 9132-50 clamp-on probe, and the probe's own error isn't included in the meter's accuracy figure. Without a probe, the direct current input is rated to 10 A AC or DC.
