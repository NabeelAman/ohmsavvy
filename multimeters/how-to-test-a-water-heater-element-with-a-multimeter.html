How to Test a Water Heater Element With a Multimeter

To test a water heater element with a multimeter, turn the breaker off, confirm 0 V at the thermostat, lift one wire off the element and measure ohms across its two terminals. A good element reads close to V² ÷ W, so a 4500 W, 240 V element should show about 12.8 Ω; OL means the coil is broken, and any reading from a terminal to the tank means it is shorted to ground. Test both elements, because a typical residential electric tank has two and they fail with different symptoms.

240 V and a tank full of water

  • A residential electric water heater in North America is usually fed at 240 V on a double-pole breaker, often 30 A. Switch that breaker off and lock or tape it so nobody switches it back on while your hands are in the access panel.
  • Don't trust the breaker label. Measure between the two incoming supply terminals and from each to the tank or ground screw with a CAT III rated meter, and confirm 0 V before touching anything. Check the meter on a known live outlet first so you know it works.
  • Never restore power to a tank that has been drained. An element run dry can burn out in seconds. If the wiring is scorched, the tank is leaking, or you aren't comfortable working on 240 V, call a licensed plumber or electrician.

How the two elements share the work

Most 40 and 50 gallon electric tanks have an upper and a lower element, wired so only one heats at a time. The upper thermostat has priority: when the top of the tank is cold, it powers the upper element. Once the top is up to temperature, it switches power down to the lower thermostat, which runs the lower element until the bottom is hot too. That arrangement explains why the symptom tells you which element to suspect before you pick up the meter.

If the upper element dies, the upper thermostat never gets satisfied and never hands power down, so the whole tank goes cold. If the lower element dies, the upper one still heats the top section, and you get a short burst of hot water that turns lukewarm quickly. The thermostats can cause the same symptoms, so test them as well; our guide on how to test an electric water heater thermostat covers the ECO reset and both thermostats.

How to test a water heater element with a multimeter, step by step

You need a digital multimeter with a resistance range, an insulated screwdriver, and about twenty minutes. Leave the water in the tank. The whole test happens at the terminal block behind each access panel.

1Switch off the breaker and prove the circuit dead

Turn off the double-pole breaker, then remove the upper access panel, fold back the insulation and unclip the plastic safety guard. Set the meter to AC volts and measure across the two supply screws at the top of the upper thermostat, then from each screw to the green ground screw or bare tank metal. All three readings should be 0 V. A reading of about 120 V from one screw to ground with the breaker off means you have the wrong breaker or a single-pole one has been mislabeled, so stop and find the right one.

2Work out the resistance you should see

Read the voltage and wattage stamped on the element flange or printed on the tank label. Resistance equals voltage squared divided by power. For the most common element, 4500 W at 240 V: 240 × 240 = 57,600, and 57,600 ÷ 4500 = 12.8 Ω. Manufacturing tolerance and the meter's own test-lead resistance mean a good element typically reads within about 10% of the calculated figure, so 11.5 to 14 Ω is a pass for that one.

Notice that a higher-wattage element has lower resistance, not higher. Lower resistance lets more current flow at the same voltage. The chart below makes the same point for the three common 240 V ratings.

3Lift one wire and measure across the element terminals

Photograph the wiring, then loosen one terminal screw and pull that wire clear. With both wires still on, the meter reads the element in parallel with the thermostat and wiring, which can hide an open coil. Set the meter to ohms (the 200 Ω or 400 Ω range on a manual meter), touch the probes together to note the lead resistance, often 0.1 to 0.3 Ω, then press them firmly onto the two element screws.

A reading far below the expected value, say 2 or 3 Ω, is rare but points to a partly shorted coil; replace it. If the reading wanders, scrape any corrosion off the screw heads and try again. For more on low-ohm technique and lead offset, see how to measure resistance of a heating element.

4Check from each terminal to the tank

This is the step people skip, and it catches the fault behind most tripped breakers. Leave the meter on its highest resistance range. Put one probe on bare tank metal (the ground screw, or a scraped spot on the element flange) and touch the other to each element terminal in turn. A sound element reads OL both times. Any number at all, even several hundred kilohms, means water has reached the coil through a cracked sheath.

Ohms prove the coil, OL to the tank proves the sheath

An element can read a perfect 12.8 Ω across its terminals and still be leaking current to the tank. The coil is fine; the sheath around it is not. A GFCI or breaker sees that leak even when your across-terminals reading looks textbook, so only the pair of tests together clears an element.

5Repeat on the second element and act on the results

Reconnect the wire, refit the guard and move to the lower panel. Test that element the same way. Elements for most tanks are standard screw-in parts sold by voltage and wattage, so match both numbers; the length and the screw-in or bolt-on mounting must match too.

Across terminalsTerminal to tankVerdict
Within ~10% of V² ÷ WOLElement good; test the thermostats next

After a replacement, refill the tank completely and open a hot tap until water runs without spitting air before you switch the breaker back on. If both elements pass, the fault is upstream. A clamp meter on one supply conductor, with the panel guard back on and the tank heating, should show roughly 4500 ÷ 240, about 18.8 A, for a 4500 W element; 0 A with a call for heat points to the thermostat or ECO. Only make live measurements if you are trained for 240 V work.

Which meter to use for water heater work

Any meter with a resistance range will read 12.8 Ω, but the safety step at the start is a 240 V measurement inside an appliance, so buy for that: CAT III 600V and good leads. A clamp meter is useful for the live current check. For washer heaters and other appliance elements, our washing machine heating element test follows the same logic at 120 V, and what OL means on a multimeter explains the open reading in more depth.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to drain the water heater to test the element?

No. The resistance and ground checks are made on the terminals outside the tank, so the water can stay in. You only drain the tank, or at least lower the level below the element, when you replace an element. Shut the cold supply and open a hot tap first so the tank isn't under pressure when the old element comes out.

Which water heater element usually fails first?

The lower element tends to fail first because it does most of the heating. Cold water enters at the bottom through the dip tube, so the lower element runs every time you draw hot water, while the upper one mainly recovers the top of the tank. Sediment also collects at the bottom and can bury the lower element, which makes it run hotter.

Can a water heater element test good and still be bad?

Yes, in one common way. An element can read the correct ohms across its terminals but leak to the tank through a cracked sheath, which trips the breaker or a GFCI. That's why the terminal-to-tank check matters. Less often, an element can open only when hot, so if symptoms return, test again right after it has been running.

What should a 3500 watt water heater element read?

At 240 V, a 3500 W element works out to 240² ÷ 3500, about 16.5 Ω. Real elements typically land within roughly 10% of the calculated figure, so somewhere around 15 to 18 Ω is normal. Use the voltage and wattage stamped on the element flange, not the tank label, if a previous owner has fitted a different element.

Why does my breaker trip as soon as the water heater turns on?

A tripping breaker usually means a short to ground, most often an element whose sheath has split and let water reach the coil. Check each element from both terminals to bare tank metal; any reading other than OL points to the faulty one. Damaged wiring at the junction box can do the same, so if both elements pass, call an electrician.

Will a 240 V element work on 208 V?

It works but delivers less heat. Resistance stays about the same, so a 4500 W, 240 V element on a 208 V supply makes roughly 208² ÷ 12.8, about 3,380 W, around 75% of its rating. Recovery is slower. Apartment buildings and commercial sites with three-phase service often have 208 V, so check before assuming the element is weak.
