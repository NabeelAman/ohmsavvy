Klein Tools MM400 vs MM450

Klein Tools MM400 vs MM450 compares a discontinued Klein meter with the slim True RMS model still in the catalog. Both are 4,000-count, auto-ranging, CAT III 600V meters with microamp-to-10 A current ranges, temperature, capacitance, frequency and duty cycle. The MM450 adds True RMS, an AC spec that runs from 50 to 400 Hz instead of 50 to 60 Hz, a 2 m drop rating, Lead-Alert lights and a body 17 mm thinner. Klein lists the MM400 as no longer in production, and Amazon now sells it only through other sellers' offers.

Klein Tools MM400 vs MM450 on AC: 60 Hz or wider

The MM400's manual gives a frequency range of 50 to 60 Hz for both AC volts and AC amps. Outside that band, Klein makes no accuracy promise. The MM450's manual widens the same spec to 50 to 400 Hz, and the MM450 is True RMS where the MM400 isn't. Those two facts together are the main technical reason to prefer the newer meter.

For a homeowner or residential electrician measuring outlets, breakers and appliance circuits, both meters read 120 V and 240 V mains inside their spec. The gap shows on the waveform rather than the frequency: the current drawn by LED drivers, electronic ballasts and switch-mode supplies isn't a sine, so the MM400's reading can be off where the MM450's True RMS reading holds. The 400 Hz end of the MM450's band covers equipment that runs on 400 Hz power, such as some aircraft ground and military gear. Neither meter is specified for a motor drive running below 50 Hz, so for that work see the drive-rated meters in Brymen BM235 vs Fluke 117.

Why True RMS still matters on 60 Hz

Frequency and waveform are separate problems. A 60 Hz current can still be badly distorted, and an average-responding meter like the MM400 assumes a sine shape when it scales its reading. Our explainer on when you need True RMS walks through the arithmetic.

Ranges side by side

Both meters have a microamp range of 400.0 µA and both share the same 500 mA and 10 A fuse ratings. The MM450 extends the ranges at the edges: capacitance to 400 µF, resistance to 50 MΩ and temperature from −40 °F up to 1832 °F. The MM400's temperature input starts at 0 °F, which rules out freezer and outdoor winter readings.

Two smaller differences are worth knowing on the bench. The MM450's frequency function triggers on signals above 2 V RMS, where the MM400 needs more than 8 V, so the MM450 can read the frequency of a 5 V logic or sensor signal and the MM400 can't. And the MM450's diode test puts out 3.9 V open circuit against the MM400's 3.0 V. White and blue LEDs typically need around 3 V to conduct, so the MM450 has more headroom to light or test one. For ordinary silicon diodes, both work.

Carrying it: thickness, drops and cold

Klein gives the MM450 as 165 × 80 × 27.8 mm and 262 g with batteries; the MM400 as 153.4 × 78 × 45.2 mm and 230 g in its manual, 232 g on its product page. So the MM450 is longer and slightly heavier, but much flatter, which is what lets it slide into a shirt or back pocket. It is drop-rated to 2 m against the MM400's 1 m.

The MM450 is also rated to work from −10 to 60 °C, where the MM400's manual gives 0 to 40 °C. A condenser on a summer roof or an unheated garage in January can fall outside the MM400's range. Its reverse-contrast display adjusts brightness from an optical sensor, and Lead-Alert LEDs light at the jacks the selected function needs, which helps prevent the classic mistake of probing voltage with the red lead in a current jack.

Leads in the wrong jack blow fuses

On both meters, the µA/mA function shares the VΩ jack and is fused at 500 mA, and the 10 A jack has its own fuse. Measuring voltage with a lead left in the 10 A jack puts a short across the circuit. Check lead placement before every voltage test, keep to CAT III 600V circuits, and call an electrician for anything upstream of the main panel.

Buying a discontinued MM400

Klein's page says the MM400 is no longer in production and names the MM420 as the newer model. On Amazon, the MM400 listing has no buy box; it is available only through "See All Buying Options" from other sellers, so condition, packaging and return terms depend on the seller. The MM450 listing has a normal buy box and shows in stock. Check the seller and the warranty terms before you buy an MM400.

The MM400 still makes sense at a clear discount for a homeowner who checks outlets, batteries and appliance heaters. For anyone else, the MM450 fixes its real weaknesses: no True RMS, a narrow AC spec, a 1 m drop rating and a narrow operating temperature range.

You need low-impedance (LoZ) voltage or non-contact voltage detection for ghost-voltage problems, or a CAT IV rating: Klein lists neither LoZ nor NCV for these two, and both are CAT III 600V. The Fluke 117 adds LoZ and NCV.

The bottom line

The Klein MM450 is the better buy: True RMS, AC specified from 50 to 400 Hz, a 27.8 mm body with a 2 m drop rating, a wider temperature range and Lead-Alert jacks, and it is Klein's current model. The MM400 is discontinued, not True RMS and specified for AC only at 50 to 60 Hz, so it suits only a discounted purchase for basic home checks.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Klein MM400 discontinued?+

Yes. Klein's product page says the MM400 is no longer in production and links to the MM420 as the newer model. On Amazon the MM400 has no buy box and is sold only through other sellers' offers. The MM450 is a current Klein model and is in stock on Amazon.

Is the Klein MM400 True RMS?+

No. Klein's manual describes the MM400 as an auto-ranging multimeter without a True RMS claim, and Klein specifies its AC volts and amps only from 50 to 60 Hz. The MM450's manual describes it as True RMS, with AC specified from 50 to 400 Hz.

Does the Klein MM450 measure microamps?+

Yes. Its manual lists 400.0 µA and 4000 µA ranges with 0.1 µA resolution, plus 40 mA, 400 mA, 4 A and 10 A ranges. The MM400 has the same 400.0 µA and 4000 µA ranges. Both share the VΩ jack for µA and mA, fused at 500 mA.

Which is smaller, the Klein MM400 or MM450?+

The MM450 is much thinner, 27.8 mm against 45.2 mm, so it fits a pocket. It is slightly longer at 165 mm against 153.4 mm and heavier at 262 g against 230 g. Klein rates it for a 2 m drop, twice the MM400's 1 m.

Can the Klein MM450 test LEDs?+

Its diode test supplies up to 3.9 V open circuit at 1.8 mA max, per Klein's manual. White and blue LEDs typically need around 3 V to conduct, so the MM450 has headroom to test many of them. The MM400 supplies 3.0 V open circuit, which is marginal for those colors.

What replaced the Klein MM400?+

Klein's MM400 page names the MM420 as the newer model. The MM450 is a different, slimmer meter in Klein's range that adds True RMS, Lead-Alert lights and a 2 m drop rating. We compare the MM450 here because it is the in-stock Klein pick on our multimeter list.
