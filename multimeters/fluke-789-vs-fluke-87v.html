Fluke 789 vs Fluke 87V: ProcessMeter or Industrial Multimeter?

Buy the Fluke 789 if you work on 4–20 mA instrument loops: it measures milliamps to 0.001 mA at ±(0.05% + 2), and it can also source, simulate and power a loop, with a 24 V supply and a switchable 250 Ω HART resistor built in. Buy the Fluke 87V if your work is electrical rather than instrumentation: it handles up to 10 A (the 789 stops at 1 A), reads AC volts out to 20 kHz with a low-pass filter for drives, and adds temperature, capacitance and 250 µs peak capture. Both are CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, 1000 V meters.

Key differences

  • →Loop output: the 789 sources 0–20 or 4–20 mA (overrange to 24 mA) at 0.05% of span, simulates a transmitter in a live loop, and powers a transmitter from a built-in 24 V supply. The 87V only measures.
  • →HART: the 789 can switch a 250 Ω series resistor into its loop-power circuit so a HART communicator can talk to the transmitter. The 87V has no equivalent.
  • →Milliamp precision: on a 12 mA reading the 789's spec allows about ±0.008 mA; the 87V's 60 mA range allows about ±0.064 mA, eight times wider.
  • →Current ceiling: the 789 tops out at 1 A (440 mA continuous). The 87V goes to 10 A, 20 A for 30 seconds.
  • →AC bandwidth: the 789's AC volts spec loosens to 7.0% + 4 between 200 and 500 Hz; the 87V is specified to 20 kHz and has a low-pass filter for drive outputs.
  • →Extras on the 87V only: temperature, capacitance, 250 µs peak capture, a 19,999-count mode and a lifetime warranty (the 789's is 3 years).

A calibrator with a meter attached, or a meter with nothing attached

Fluke files the 789 under its mA loop calibrators and the 87V under digital multimeters, and that is the best one-line summary of the choice. The 789 is a 1000 V CAT IV multimeter wrapped around a loop calibrator. The 87V is a deeper multimeter that cannot put a single milliamp into a circuit.

789: output

Source, simulate, step and ramp 0–20 or 4–20 mA.

789: loop power

24 V supply plus a 250 Ω HART resistor.

789: mA precision

0.001 mA at ±(0.05% + 2) to 30 mA.

87V: 10 A

Ten times the 789's 1 A current ceiling.

87V: drives

20 kHz AC bandwidth and a low-pass filter.

87V: extras

Temperature, capacitance, 250 µs peak.

We have tested the 87V ourselves, and it holds our 4.8 rating. The 789 is rated 4.7 from Fluke's product page and users manual; we haven't used one. Every figure below comes from those Fluke documents, and the loop arithmetic is ours.

The working for the first chart: 0.05% of 12.000 mA is 0.006 mA, and two counts of 0.001 mA add 0.002 mA, so the 789 allows ±0.008 mA. On the 87V, 0.2% of 12.00 mA is 0.024 mA and four counts of 0.01 mA add 0.04 mA, for ±0.064 mA. Across a 16 mA span that is 0.05% of span against 0.4%. When a transmitter's own tolerance is a few tenths of a percent, only the 789 is precise enough to judge it.

One conflict to flag: Fluke's 789 web page lists the source drive as 24 V compliance, or 1200 Ω at 20 mA, while the users manual specifies 28 V compliance with healthy batteries. Plan on the lower figure. Its battery table also quotes about 10 hours sourcing 12 mA into 500 Ω, where the web page says 10 hours sourcing 20 mA.

Source, simulate, loop power: what each 789 mode is for

The 789 picks its mode partly from which jacks you use, so the wiring tells you what the meter is doing. Here's how its three output modes compare with the 87V's only option, measuring in series.

Fluke's manual points out that simulate draws less from the meter's batteries than source, because the loop supply does the work, and its battery table backs that up: about 140 hours simulating against about 10 hours sourcing. On a long commissioning day, simulate wherever a loop supply is available.

The SpanCheck buttons jump straight to 0% and 100% (4 and 20 mA by default), %STEP moves in 25% jumps, and coarse and fine adjust in between. Auto step and auto ramp let you watch a valve or a trend display while the 789 drives the signal for you. The display shows % of span next to the mA value, which saves converting 12 mA to 50% in your head.

Why the HART resistor matters

A HART communicator talks to a smart transmitter by riding a small signal on top of the 4–20 mA current, and it needs some series resistance in the loop to develop that signal. A panel loop usually has it in the input card. A transmitter on the bench, powered by a plain supply, does not, which is why technicians carry a loose 250 Ω resistor. Fluke's manual says the 789 switches an internal 250 Ω series resistor into its loop power circuit with one button, and the display shows when it's in.

The 87V has nothing like this, so with an 87V you need a separate supply and resistor before a HART communicator can talk to a bench transmitter. For more on milliamp loop work, including clamp-on alternatives, see our guide to the best multimeters for 4–20 mA loop testing and the walkthrough on how to measure milliamps with a multimeter.

Where the 87V pulls ahead

Turn away from the instrument loops and the 789's limits show. Its current input is fused at 440 mA and allowed 1 A for only 30 seconds, so motor, heater and contactor coil currents above that need a clamp or a different meter. Its AC volts spec is written around 50 to 60 Hz and grows to 7.0% + 4 between 200 and 500 Hz, and Fluke lists no low-pass filter, so a drive's output is not a job for it.

The 87V covers all of that: 10 A, AC volts specified to 20 kHz, a low-pass filter for drive outputs, a thermocouple input, capacitance and 250 µs peak capture. It is also the lighter meter (355 g bare) and runs about 400 hours on a 9 V cell. Our Fluke 87V review covers those functions in depth, and Fluke 117 vs 87V explains the drive filter.

Instrument tech or electrician?

If the job is proving that a 4–20 mA signal is right, the 789 does it alone: it powers, drives and reads the loop eight times more precisely than the 87V can. If the job is power, motors and drives, the 87V does more and the 789's loop tools sit unused. Many plants give instrument techs a 789 and electricians an 87V for exactly that reason.

Safety: set the 789's dial before you touch a live loop

Fluke's 789 manual warns to select an mA output position before connecting to a current loop. Other dial positions can present a low impedance that lets up to 35 mA flow, which can trip interlocks or move a valve. Tell the control room before you break or drive a loop, since alarms and final elements may respond. Both meters are CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V, but loop wiring often shares a cabinet with mains; use rated leads, keep the red lead out of the current jack when measuring volts, and follow your site's lockout/tagout rules.

The bottom line

The Fluke 789 is the right meter for 4–20 mA work: it measures milliamps to ±(0.05% + 2) at 0.001 mA, sources and simulates loop current at 0.05% of span, and powers transmitters from a 24 V supply with a switchable 250 Ω HART resistor. The Fluke 87V, our hands-on tested pick, is the right meter for electrical work, with 10 A current, 20 kHz AC bandwidth, a low-pass filter, temperature, capacitance and a lifetime warranty. Pick by the job, not by the badge.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Fluke 87V test a 4-20 mA loop?+

It can measure one. Put it in series on the 60 mA range and it reads to 0.01 mA with ±(0.2% + 4) accuracy, enough to tell whether a transmitter is alive and roughly right. It can't source, simulate or power a loop, and it has no HART resistor, so you can't use it to drive an input or bench-test a transmitter on its own.

What is the difference between source and simulate on the Fluke 789?+

In source mode the 789 supplies both the power and the current, for passive devices such as a PLC input with no loop supply. In simulate mode it stands in for a transmitter in a loop that already has a 15 to 48 V supply, regulating the current while the loop provides the power. Simulate drains the 789's batteries far less.

Does the Fluke 789 have a HART communicator built in?+

No. It has a built-in, switchable 250 Ω series resistor that lets a separate HART communicator talk to a transmitter the 789 is powering. It doesn't read or configure HART devices itself. Fluke's page describes the resistor as removing the need to carry a separate one.

Can the Fluke 789 measure 10 amps?+

No. Its current input is rated 440 mA continuous and 1 A for up to 30 seconds, protected by a 440 mA, 1000 V fuse. For motor or heater currents, use a meter with a 10 A input such as the 87V, or a clamp meter, and keep the 789 for milliamp loops.

Is the Fluke 789 True RMS?+

Its AC volts and AC current are true-rms, per Fluke's manual, at ±(0.7% + 2) on 50 to 60 Hz voltage ranges. The specification loosens quickly with frequency: 1.2% + 4 up to 200 Hz and 7.0% + 4 from 200 to 500 Hz. The 87V is specified to 20 kHz, so it's the better choice for distorted or high-frequency waveforms.

Which has the longer warranty, the Fluke 789 or 87V?+

The 87V. Fluke lists a lifetime warranty for it, while the 789 manual gives three years from purchase. The 789 warranty excludes fuses, disposable batteries and damage from misuse. Fluke also recommends calibrating the 789 once a year, which matters if your loop checks go into a quality record.
