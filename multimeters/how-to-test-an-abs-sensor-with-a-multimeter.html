How to Test an ABS Sensor With a Multimeter

To test an ABS sensor with a multimeter, first find out whether it is passive or active. A passive two-wire sensor is a coil: unplug it, measure its resistance (commonly somewhere around 800 to 2,500 Ω, per the service manual), then spin the wheel by hand on the AC volts range and look for a voltage that rises with speed. An active sensor is electronic, so its resistance means nothing; instead you check for the supply voltage from the ABS module and, in series on the mA range, watch the current step between two levels as the wheel turns. In both cases the quickest verdict comes from comparing the suspect wheel with the same sensor on the other side of the car.

ABS testing needs ohms, AC millivolts and a DC mA range. A backlit 6,000-count meter such as the Fluke 179 covers all three. See our Fluke 179 review.

Lifting the car safely

  • Never work under a car held only by a jack. Use rated jack stands on solid ground, set the parking brake and chock the wheels that stay on the ground.
  • Spin wheels by hand only, with the engine off. Running the engine in gear to turn a raised wheel is how people get hurt.
  • The ABS hydraulic unit and module aren't DIY repairs. If the sensors and wiring test good and the warning light stays on, or the brake pedal feels wrong, have the brakes checked by a professional before driving.

Passive vs active ABS wheel speed sensors

Every wheel speed sensor reads a toothed tone ring, or a magnetic encoder ring built into the wheel bearing seal, that turns with the wheel. How the sensor turns that motion into a signal decides how you test it.

A passive sensor, also called variable reluctance or inductive, is a permanent magnet wrapped in a coil of fine wire. Each tooth passing the tip changes the magnetic field and induces an AC voltage in the coil. It needs no power, and its output grows with speed: a fraction of a volt when you turn the wheel by hand, several volts at road speed. At walking pace the signal can be too small to read, which is one reason carmakers moved away from them.

An active sensor contains a Hall effect or magnetoresistive chip powered by the ABS module. Instead of generating a voltage, it switches its current draw between two fixed levels as each tooth or magnet pole passes. On many common designs those levels are roughly 7 mA and 14 mA, though your manual may list different figures. The square signal has the same height at any speed, so the module can see the wheel move at almost zero speed. Most modern cars use active sensors.

1Find the sensor and work out its type

Read the fault code first if you can, since it tells you which wheel to test. Raise that corner and support it on a stand. The sensor is bolted to the knuckle or hub behind the brake rotor, with a cable running up to a connector in the wheel well or engine bay. Look up whether your vehicle uses passive or active sensors. If the information isn't handy, unplug the sensor, turn the key on (engine off), and measure DC volts between the two harness pins: a steady voltage from the module means active, nothing means passive. Turn the key off before going further.

2Inspect the sensor, wiring and tone ring

A quick look finds many ABS faults before the meter comes out. Follow the cable along its whole length; it flexes with every suspension movement and often chafes on brackets or gets cut during brake work. Check the connector for green corrosion or bent pins. Pull the sensor if it slides out easily and look at the tip: magnetized rust and brake dust stick to it and blur the signal, and a light clean often helps. With the wheel turning slowly, look for cracked, rusted or swollen teeth on the tone ring. A cracked ring causes a signal dropout once per revolution that no new sensor will fix.

3Passive sensor: measure the coil resistance

With the key off and the sensor unplugged, set the meter to ohms and measure across the two sensor pins. Compare the reading with the manual. Many passive sensors land somewhere around 800 to 2,500 Ω, and the matching sensor on the other side of the axle should read within a small margin of the suspect. Then measure from each pin to the sensor body or a clean chassis ground; that should read OL. Flex the cable near the sensor while watching the display, since broken strands often only open when the wire moves.

4Passive sensor: spin the wheel and read AC volts

Good resistance proves the coil is intact, not that it produces a signal. Leave the sensor unplugged, set the meter to AC volts (mV if it has a separate range), and connect across the sensor pins. Spin the wheel by hand at a steady pace. A working sensor typically gives a few hundred millivolts AC at hand speed, and the reading should roughly double when you spin about twice as fast. Compare the same test on the opposite wheel; a sensor producing a fraction of its partner's output usually has debris on its tip, a too-large air gap or a weak magnet.

The underlying physics is covered in our magnetic pickup sensor guide; crank sensors on many engines use the same coil design and are tested the same way, as shown in how to test a crankshaft position sensor.

5Active sensor: check the supply, then the current

Don't judge an active sensor with the ohms range; the reading depends on the chip inside and the meter's test voltage, and it proves nothing. Start with the supply: sensor unplugged, key on, measure DC volts across the two harness pins. It should be close to the value in the service manual, often near battery voltage or a regulated level from the module. No voltage means a wiring or module problem, not a sensor fault.

To see the signal, wire the meter in series. Key off, move the red lead to the mA jack, and connect the meter between the harness supply pin and the matching sensor pin (a pair of back-probe jumper leads makes this easier), with the other pin connected straight through. Turn the key on and rotate the wheel very slowly by hand. The current should step between a low and a high level, for example about 7 mA and 14 mA, as each tooth or pole passes. A current that sits fixed at one level while the wheel turns, or reads zero, points to a failed sensor, a damaged encoder ring or an air gap problem.

Turn the wheel slowly

A digital display updates only a few times a second, so a fast spin averages the two current levels into one middle value. Turning the wheel a small amount at a time lets you watch it switch cleanly between low and high.

6Compare side to side and confirm

Wheel speed sensors come in pairs, so the best reference is the matching sensor on the same axle. Similar resistance and similar AC output (passive), or the same supply voltage and the same two current levels (active), on both sides mean the suspect sensor is fine and the fault is upstream. In that case test the harness for continuity from the sensor connector back to the ABS module connector, and check for shorts to ground, using the methods in how to find a broken wire with a multimeter. After any repair, clear the codes and test-drive; many ABS systems only clear the warning light after they see all four wheels moving above a few miles per hour.

Which multimeter to use for ABS sensor testing

You need resistance into the kilohms, an AC voltage range that resolves tenths of a volt or better, and a DC mA range for active sensors. Back-probe pins and alligator-clip leads matter as much as the meter, since you'll be turning the wheel with one hand.

Frequently asked questions

What should an ABS sensor read in ohms?

Only passive two-wire sensors have a meaningful resistance. Many read somewhere between about 800 and 2,500 Ω, but values vary a lot between makes and between front and rear sensors on the same car, so the service manual figure is what counts. OL means an open coil or broken lead, and a reading near zero means a short. Active sensors don't give a useful ohms reading at all.

How do I tell if my ABS sensor is active or passive?

Check the service information for your vehicle first. As a rule of thumb, most cars from the mid-2000s onward use active sensors, and many active sensors have two wires just like passive ones, so wire count alone won't tell you. A key-on voltage on one harness pin, coming from the ABS module, points to an active sensor. A passive sensor's harness has no supply voltage.

Can a bad ABS sensor cause the traction control light to come on?

Yes. Traction and stability control use the same wheel speed signals as the ABS, so a faulty sensor, damaged wiring or a cracked tone ring usually turns on the ABS, traction and stability warning lights together, and may disable those systems. Some vehicles also use wheel speed for the speedometer, cruise control or transmission shifting, so those can misbehave too.

Can I test an ABS sensor without removing it?

Yes. All the meter tests here are done with the sensor installed, by unplugging its connector and measuring on the sensor side, or by back-probing the harness. Removing it is only needed to clean metal debris off the tip, inspect the tone ring, or replace it. Raise and support the car so the wheel can turn freely for the output tests.

Why does my ABS sensor test good but the light stays on?

The signal can fail between the sensor and the module. Check the harness for chafing where it flexes with the suspension, and test continuity from the sensor connector to the module connector. Also inspect the tone ring for cracks, rust swelling or missing teeth, and check wheel bearing play, since a loose bearing changes the air gap. A scan tool showing live wheel speeds pinpoints which wheel drops out.
