Top 10 Best Oscilloscopes for Arduino and ESP32 Projects

Microcontroller work rewards channels and serial decoding far more than raw bandwidth. We ranked ten scopes on how well they read I2C, SPI, UART and PWM from Arduino and ESP32 boards.

An Arduino or ESP32 project usually fails at the boundary between chips: a sensor that never answers on I2C, an SPI display showing garbage, a serial port printing nonsense because the baud rate drifted. A multimeter only reports an average voltage on those lines. An oscilloscope shows the actual edges, the logic levels and the timing between clock and data, and a scope with protocol decoding labels each byte so you can compare it with what your sketch meant to send. Our guides to diagnosing a UART problem and measuring PWM duty cycle show the workflow step by step.

That changes how you should rank scopes for this job. Microcontroller signals are slow by instrument standards, so bandwidth beyond 50 to 100 MHz rarely helps. What decides the ranking is how many signals you can watch at once, whether the maker lists I2C, SPI and UART decoding, how much memory holds a long transaction, and whether the scope fits on a cluttered maker desk. Ten candidates, all sold on Amazon.com, are ranked below on those terms. If you are new to the instrument, start with probing a logic signal.

What to look for in an Arduino oscilloscope

Channels

Two for I2C or one UART link, four for a full SPI bus with chip select.

Serial decoding

Bytes and addresses shown on screen instead of edges you decode by hand.

Bandwidth

50 to 100 MHz covers AVR boards and most ESP32 buses with margin.

Memory depth

Millions of points keep a whole transaction sharp when you zoom in.

Signal source

A generator can fake a sensor or clock while your sketch runs.

Desk footprint

USB-C power or a USB scope keeps the bench uncluttered.

Rule of thumb

Match channels to your busiest protocol first, confirm decoding second, and only then compare bandwidth. For background on why the bandwidth figure matters less here, read what oscilloscope bandwidth means, or see our head-to-heads of the Rigol DHO804 and DS1054Z and the Hantek DSO2C10 and DSO2D10.

Before you buy

Buying advice

Count the wires on the bus you debug most

The channel count you need follows directly from your protocols. UART needs one channel per direction, I2C needs two (SDA and SCL), and a full SPI capture needs four (clock, MOSI, MISO and chip select). If SPI displays, SD cards or radio modules are in your projects, a four-channel scope such as the DHO804, SDS1104X-E or DS1054Z saves you from capturing the bus in pieces. If you mostly read sensors over I2C, two channels are enough and the DHO802 or a Hantek DSO2000 model will do the job.

Treat serial decoding as a requirement, not an extra

Decoding turns a row of square waves into readable bytes, addresses and ACK bits on screen. Without it you count edges and convert binary by hand, which works once and becomes tedious fast. Every scope in our top six lists UART, I2C and SPI decoding from its maker, though Rigol's sources conflict on whether the DS1054Z includes it as standard. The FNIRSI 1014D, MINIWARE DS213 and FNIRSI DSO152 list no decoding at all.

Do not overspend on bandwidth

A 16 MHz AVR board, a 400 kHz I2C bus and a 115,200 baud serial port are slow by oscilloscope standards. Bandwidth matters mainly when you push SPI into the tens of MHz on an ESP32 or want to see ringing on fast edges. A 50 to 100 MHz scope covers nearly all maker work; the money is better spent on channels, decoding and memory depth. Our bandwidth explainer covers the rule of thumb for square-wave edges.

Look at memory depth for long captures

Debugging often means catching one bad byte in a long burst of traffic. A scope with millions of points can capture a whole transaction at a high sample rate and let you zoom in afterwards. The Rigol DHO models list 25 Mpts on one channel and the Hantek DSO2000 models 8 Mpts, while the PicoScope 2204A has 8 kS of capture memory (it can stream more over USB) and the handhelds hold a few thousand points.

Know when you need a generator or logic inputs

A built-in signal generator, as on the Hantek DSO2D10, FNIRSI 1014D and MINIWARE DS213, lets you feed a known waveform into an ADC pin or simulate a sensor while your code runs. Logic inputs, which only the DS213 has here, add extra digital lines without using analog channels. Neither is essential, but both shorten bench time on sensor-heavy projects.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What bandwidth do I actually need for Arduino work?+

Less than most buyers expect. Classic Arduino boards run at 16 MHz, and the buses you probe most often, such as I2C at 100 or 400 kHz, UART and PWM, are far slower. A 50 MHz scope like the Rigol DS1054Z shows these with clean edges. Consider 70 to 100 MHz if you run fast SPI on an ESP32 or want to see edge ringing in detail.

Is a pocket scope like the FNIRSI DSO152 enough for Arduino debugging?+

For quick checks, yes: confirming a pin toggles, measuring a PWM frequency or seeing a servo pulse. Its single 200 kHz channel is the limit. You cannot compare a clock with its data line, and a 400 kHz I2C bus is beyond its bandwidth. If you debug communication between chips, a two- or four-channel bench scope with decoding is the better tool.

Should I buy a logic analyzer or an oscilloscope for Arduino projects?+

A logic analyzer shows only ones and zeros, while an oscilloscope also shows voltage levels, slow edges, noise and ringing, which is where many hardware faults hide. A scope with serial decoding covers most of what a cheap logic analyzer does for I2C, SPI and UART. If you need many digital lines at once, the DS213's two logic inputs or a dedicated analyzer can complement a scope.

How many oscilloscope channels do I need to debug SPI?+

A full SPI capture uses four signals: clock, MOSI, MISO and chip select. A four-channel scope such as the Rigol DHO804, Siglent SDS1104X-E or Rigol DS1054Z can show all of them together and decode the bytes. With two channels you can still watch clock and one data line, then move a probe to check the other, but you lose the complete picture of each transaction.

Can a USB oscilloscope like the PicoScope 2204A work with an Arduino?+

Yes. It is powered from your computer, sits beside the board and runs PicoScope 7 on Windows, macOS or Linux, including serial decoding. Its 10 MHz bandwidth suits I2C, UART, PWM and analog sensors. The trade-offs are that it needs a computer every time and that fast SPI edges will appear rounded at that bandwidth.

Where should I clip the oscilloscope ground on an Arduino or ESP32 board?+

Clip it to the board's GND pin, never to a signal or supply pin. On a mains-powered bench scope the ground clip connects to earth, so touching it to anything other than the circuit's ground can short that point. Keep the ground lead short for clean edges, and use the 10X probe setting for most digital signals to reduce loading.
