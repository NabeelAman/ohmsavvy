Hantek 6022BE vs Hantek DSO2D10

Hantek 6022BE vs Hantek DSO2D10 is two ends of one brand's range. The 6022BE is a screenless USB box: two channels, 20MHz and 48 MS/s, powered and displayed by a Windows PC. The DSO2D10 is a mains-powered bench scope with a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen, two channels at 100MHz and 1 GSa/s, serial decoding and a built-in 25MHz signal generator.

  • →The 6022BE has no screen or controls of its own; every capture happens in Hantek's Windows software. The DSO2D10 is self-contained with a 7-inch display.
  • →The DSO2D10 adds a single-channel generator (sine to 25MHz, square to 10MHz, arbitrary waveforms) that the 6022BE lacks entirely.
  • →Hantek lists standard UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding for the DSO2D10; the 6022BE's trigger list is rising or falling edge only.
  • →The 6022BE is DC-coupled only with a 20 mV/div floor; the DSO2D10 offers AC coupling and goes down to 2 mV/div, which matters for ripple and small sensor signals.
  • →The 6022BE draws its power from USB and measures 205 × 120 × 35 mm; the DSO2D10 runs from mains and weighs 1.9 kg.

Spec by spec

Both are two-channel, 8-bit Hantek scopes, so on paper the gap looks like a simple step up. In practice the sample rate gap is the bigger one. The 6022BE's 48 MS/s is a low ceiling for a 20MHz front end: it leaves fewer than three samples per cycle at the rated bandwidth. The DSO2D10 keeps ten samples per cycle at 100MHz with one channel on, and five with both.

A shorter bar wins in the last two charts. Memory is the one figure we can't chart fairly: Hantek gives the DSO2D10 8M points on one channel and 4M on two, but the 6022BE's product table shows a 1M buffer without making clear which model it belongs to. For the theory behind these numbers, see what oscilloscope bandwidth means and what GSa/s means.

PC or bench: how each one fits your desk

The 6022BE turns a laptop into the scope. Plug it into a USB 2.0 port and it takes its power from there, with the waveform, the timebase and the trigger all set with a mouse in Hantek's software. That makes it tiny to store and cheap to own, and a laptop screen can be bigger than a 7-inch panel. The cost is that the scope only exists while the computer is running the software, and Hantek's list of supported systems names Windows versions only (up to Windows 10 on its product page). Mac and Chromebook owners should treat it as unsupported.

The DSO2D10 is the opposite: a 1.9 kg, 318 mm deep instrument that needs a mains socket and a permanent spot on the bench, but is ready to probe as soon as it boots. Dedicated knobs for volts per division and timebase make it quicker to hunt for a signal than clicking through menus, and its USB host and device ports cover saving captures. The built-in generator is the other half of the bench argument: one box both drives a circuit and watches what comes out.

Why the 6022BE runs out of samples before it runs out of bandwidth

A scope needs more than two samples per cycle to draw a recognizable sine, and around ten to show its shape cleanly. At 48 MS/s the 6022BE reaches ten samples per cycle at about 4.8MHz, well below its 20MHz rating. Above that, the trace is a handful of dots joined by straight lines. The DSO2D10 still has ten samples per cycle at its full 100MHz on one channel.

What each one can and can't measure

Where the 6022BE is enough

By the 3–5x rule of thumb, a 20MHz scope is comfortable on signals up to roughly 4–7MHz, and the sample rate argument above points to the low end of that range. That still covers a lot of hobby work: a 555 timer, the PWM driving a fan or LED strip, a 9,600 or 115,200 baud UART line, 100kHz I2C, audio through an amplifier, or the output of a slow sensor. Two channels let you put a clock and its data line, or an input and an output, on screen together, and Hantek lists FFT and X-Y mode for frequency and phase work.

Its weak spots are specific. The inputs are DC-coupled only, and the most sensitive setting is 20 mV/div, so 50 mV of ripple on a 12V rail is hard to see: Hantek lists no AC coupling to strip the 12V away first. Triggering is rising or falling edge only, so catching one short glitch in a stream of normal pulses is a matter of luck. And with no decoding listed, reading an I2C address means counting bits by eye.

What the DSO2D10 adds

At 100MHz the DSO2D10 is comfortable to roughly 20–33MHz, which takes in multi-MHz SPI clocks, the ringing on a fast logic edge and the switching node of many small DC-DC converters. AC coupling and 2 mV/div make the ripple job straightforward (see how to measure ripple voltage). Pulse-width, under-amplitude and pattern triggers isolate faults, and the serial triggers and decoders turn UART, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN traffic into readable values. The generator lets you sweep a sine into an RC filter on one channel while watching the output on the other, without a second instrument. The trade-offs our DSO2D10 review raises still apply: two channels only, and a smaller user community than Rigol or Siglent.

Grounding on both scopes

The DSO2D10's probe ground is normally tied to mains earth through its power cord, so clipping it to a point that isn't at earth potential, such as anywhere on the primary side of a mains supply, causes a short. Hantek doesn't list USB isolation for the 6022BE, so assume its probe ground is joined to your laptop's ground and, through a charger, possibly to earth as well. Keep both on low-voltage circuits and use a differential probe for mains.

Choose the Hantek 6022BE if…

  • ✓You want the lowest-cost way to see real waveforms and already own a Windows laptop
  • ✓Your signals are audio, slow PWM, UART and standard-mode I2C, well under a few MHz
  • ✓You need something that lives in a drawer and powers itself from a USB port

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓You want a standalone instrument with knobs and a screen, with no computer to boot
  • ✓You work with SPI, CAN or fast logic edges that need 100MHz and 1 GSa/s
  • ✓You want to inject test signals into filters and amplifiers from the same box
  • ✓You need AC coupling or 2 mV/div sensitivity for small signals riding on DC

The bottom line

Buy the Hantek 6022BE only if cost is the deciding factor, you already have a Windows laptop and your signals stay below a few MHz: it shows real waveforms on two channels for very little money. For anything beyond that, the DSO2D10 is the far more capable scope, with five times the bandwidth, about 21 times the sample rate, AC coupling, serial decoding and a generator, in a box that works without a computer.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Hantek DSO2D10 worth it over the 6022BE?+

If your work goes past slow hobby signals, yes. The DSO2D10 has 100MHz against 20MHz, 1 GSa/s against 48 MS/s, AC coupling, a 2 mV/div setting, serial decoding and a 25MHz generator. The 6022BE makes sense when the budget is very tight and a Windows laptop is already on your desk.

Can the Hantek 6022BE be used without a computer?+

No. It has no screen, buttons or power supply of its own: it takes power over USB 2.0 and displays everything in Hantek's software, which Hantek lists for Windows only. The DSO2D10 is a complete instrument with a 7-inch screen and front-panel controls.

Can the 6022BE measure power supply ripple?+

Only coarsely. Hantek lists DC coupling only and a most sensitive setting of 20 mV/div, so a small ripple sitting on a 5V or 12V rail is hard to separate from the DC level. The DSO2D10 offers AC coupling and 2 mV/div, which suits ripple checks far better.

Does either Hantek decode I2C or UART?+

Hantek lists UART/RS232, I2C, SPI, CAN and LIN triggering and decoding as standard on the DSO2D10. The 6022BE's published trigger options are rising and falling edge, and Hantek doesn't state protocol decoding for it, so plan on reading bits by eye if you buy the USB model.

Why is the 6022BE's sample rate a bigger limit than its bandwidth?+

At 48 MS/s it gets only about 2.4 samples per cycle of a 20MHz signal, too few to show a waveform's shape. Around ten samples per cycle is a comfortable minimum, which puts its practical limit nearer 5MHz. The DSO2D10 keeps ten samples per cycle at 100MHz with one channel on.
