HOW-TO GUIDE

How to Solder Pin Headers Straight

To solder pin headers straight, hold them in a jig that keeps them square to the board, most simply by pushing the long ends into a breadboard and laying the module on top, then solder just one end pin, check the angle, correct it while that single joint is still the only one, and only then solder the opposite end and the rest. Tacking one pin first is the whole trick: one joint is easy to reheat and nudge, twenty are not. This guide covers jigs for male and female headers, iron settings, a good joint on each pin, and how to fix a header that is already crooked.

Heat, fumes and lead

  • The tip runs at well over 300 °C. Always return the iron to its stand, keep the cord away from the tip, and never reach for a dropped iron.
  • Flux smoke irritates the eyes and lungs. Work with a fume extractor or good ventilation and keep your face out of the plume.
  • If you use leaded solder, wash your hands after soldering and before eating, and don't eat or drink at the bench.
  • Unplug the module from any power or USB before soldering, and wear eye protection when clipping pins or reflowing joints; molten solder and pin offcuts can fly.

Why pin headers end up crooked

A header row has a lot of leverage. The pins on a standard 2.54 mm (0.1 in) pitch header are long compared with the plastic spacer that should sit flat on the board, so a gap under one end of the spacer turns into a visible tilt at the pin tips. Most crooked headers come from soldering the first pin while the board is held at an angle by hand, then soldering a second pin before checking. After two joints the header is locked in place, and correcting it means reheating both at once.

The chart shows how far a pin tip moves sideways for small tilts, using an illustrative 6 mm of pin above the spacer (offset = length × sin angle). A 5° tilt already moves the tip half a millimeter, about a fifth of the pin spacing, which is enough to make a module fight its way into a breadboard.

1Build a jig that holds the pins square

For male headers, push the long ends of the header strips into a solderless breadboard at the right spacing, then lay the module over them so the short ends come up through its holes. The breadboard holds every pin vertical and both rows parallel at once. For a module with two rows, insert both rows before you solder either, so they end up exactly the right distance apart for a breadboard.

For female headers, plug the socket onto a row of male pins or onto the board it will mate with. If the module sits unevenly because a component on the back touches the breadboard, raise the opposite edge with a scrap of card so the board lies flat on the spacer. Our guide to keeping components from moving during hand soldering has more ways to hold parts still.

2Set up the iron

A small chisel tip of about 1.5 to 2 mm, or a fine conical tip, fits between 2.54 mm pins and still carries enough heat. Clean and tin the tip, and use thin rosin-core solder, such as 0.5 to 0.8 mm (0.020 to 0.032 in), which makes it easy to feed just enough. The settings below are common starting points, not rules; adjust until each pin wets in two to three seconds.

3Tack one end pin only

Press the board flat onto the spacer with a fingertip on the far end, away from the iron. Put the tip against both the pad and the pin of the first end pin, wait about a second, then feed solder into the junction, not onto the iron. Stop when the solder forms a small cone around the pin, remove the solder, then the iron. One pin only. Resist the temptation to do the next one while you're there.

4Check the angle and correct it

Look along the header from the end and from the side. The spacer should sit flat with no light under it, and the pins should be square to the board. If not, press the board where it needs to move, touch the iron to the single tacked joint until the solder melts, let the board settle, and remove the iron while still pressing. Hold for a couple of seconds as the joint freezes. This is the step that makes headers straight: with one joint, a correction takes seconds.

5Tack the opposite end, then solder the rest

Solder the pin at the other end of the row, check once more, then work along the row. On a double row, tack one pin on each row before filling in. Each joint should take two to three seconds. On a long row sitting in a breadboard, pause halfway so the plastic doesn't heat up. Pins tied to a large ground plane draw heat away and may need a second or two longer or a slightly hotter tip.

6Inspect and test the joints

A good joint is a smooth, concave cone that wets both the pad and the pin, like a small volcano. A ball sitting on the pin is a cold joint; reflow it with fresh flux and heat on the pad as well as the pin; our guide to fixing a cold solder joint shows the difference. Run a continuity check between neighboring pins to catch hidden bridges, then clean off flux residue if your flux needs it; see how to clean flux residue.

How to fix a header that is already crooked

If only the end pins are soldered, add fresh solder to both so they hold heat, then heat them alternately while pressing the header square, or use a wide chisel tip that spans both if they're close. If every pin is soldered, don't try to bend the header: the pads lift. Instead, cut the plastic spacer away, then remove the pins one at a time while heating each joint, and clear the holes with wick or a desoldering pump. Fit a new header with the jig method. Our guide to removing solder from a plated through-hole covers the clean-out, and repairing lifted pads helps if one comes up.

One joint, then check

Headers go crooked because two pins get soldered before anyone looks. Tack one end pin, check it from two sides, correct it while it's the only joint, and the rest of the row follows.

Frequently asked questions

Which side of the header do you solder?

Solder the short ends. Push the short pins through the module's holes from the component side and solder them on the back, leaving the long ends free to plug into a breadboard or socket. The black plastic spacer should sit flat against the board. For boards meant to plug in from the top, check the maker's photos before you start, because a reversed header is tedious to remove.

Can I solder headers without a breadboard?

Yes. You can use a second, spare header or the female socket the module will plug into as a jig, prop the board on a piece of card cut to the spacer height, or use helping hands with care. The key is the same: something holds the pins square while you tack one pin, check, and correct before soldering the rest.

Will soldering melt the breadboard?

Not if you work quickly. The breadboard contacts sit below the header spacer and the board, so heat reaches them through the pin for only a few seconds per joint. Holding the iron on one pin for a long time, or using a very high temperature, can soften the plastic. Let a long row cool briefly halfway through, or use a sacrificial old breadboard.

How do I solder female headers straight?

Plug the female header onto a row of male pins, or onto the board it will mate with, and use that as the jig. The mating part holds the socket square while you tack one pin. Female sockets have thicker plastic close to the joint, so keep each joint short and avoid pressing the iron against the housing, which melts and lets the contacts shift.

What temperature should I use to solder header pins?

A common starting point is around 320 to 350 °C (about 610 to 660 °F) for leaded solder, and a little higher for lead-free, adjusted until each pin wets in two or three seconds. Ground pins connected to copper planes may need more. If the plastic spacer softens or discolors, the temperature is too high or the iron is staying on too long.
