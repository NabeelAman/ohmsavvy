Hantek DSO2D15 vs DSO2D10

Hantek DSO2D15 vs DSO2D10 is the simplest comparison in Hantek's bench range: one spec table, one chassis, one 25 MHz generator, and a single real difference. The DSO2D15 is rated 150 MHz with a 2.4 ns rise time; the DSO2D10 is rated 100 MHz with 3.5 ns. Sample rate, memory, triggers, decoding, screen and weight are identical. So the question is narrow: will you ever probe a signal where those extra 50 MHz change what you see?

The only spec that changes

Hantek prints both models in one column-by-column table, and only two rows differ: bandwidth and rise time. The charts below show those two, plus the one derived figure that quietly favors the cheaper model.

What 50 extra megahertz buys on a square wave

A sine wave only needs bandwidth at its own frequency. A square wave, which is what every clock, PWM output and data line really is, is a sum of odd harmonics: the 3rd at a third of the size, the 5th at a fifth, and so on. A scope that cuts off the higher harmonics rounds the corners and slows the edges on screen. The usual rule for a square wave that still looks square is to keep at least the 5th harmonic, which means bandwidth of five times the clock.

Run the five-times rule against each scope and the practical ceilings fall out: 100 MHz ÷ 5 = 20 MHz of clock on the DSO2D10, 150 MHz ÷ 5 = 30 MHz on the DSO2D15. Below 20 MHz both draw a clean square; between 20 and 30 MHz the D15 still does and the D10 starts to round the corners; above 30 MHz both are showing you frequency and timing, not edge shape. For a 16 MHz Arduino crystal or an 8 MHz SPI clock, the two scopes will look the same.

Rise time is the same story in nanoseconds

The standard approximation is rise time ≈ 0.35 ÷ bandwidth, and Hantek's numbers match it: 0.35 ÷ 100 MHz = 3.5 ns, 0.35 ÷ 150 MHz = 2.3 ns (Hantek lists 2.4 ns). An edge needs to be roughly three to five times slower than the scope's own rise time to read accurately. That puts the DSO2D10's floor near 10 to 18 ns and the DSO2D15's near 7 to 12 ns. If you work with 74HC logic, gate drivers or anything you plan to time with rise-time cursors, that is the range where the two models part ways.

What each one can and can't measure

Hantek DSO2D15: the same box with a higher ceiling

With one channel on, 1 GSa/s against 150 MHz gives about 6.7 samples per cycle, comfortable for timing work. It shows 30 MHz clocks as squares, catches edges down to roughly 7 ns with fair accuracy and lets you see ringing on a fast gate signal that the D10 would partly smooth away. It can't make 8-bit vertical resolution any finer, decode a bus Hantek doesn't list (no USB, no I2S), or keep more than 4 Mpts per channel with both channels running.

Hantek DSO2D10: 100 MHz covers most benches

Two channels at 500 MSa/s give 5 samples per cycle at 100 MHz, a healthier ratio than the D15 at its own limit. It handles audio, motor PWM, CAN and LIN, sensor outputs, microcontroller clocks to about 20 MHz and switching supplies, which is the work most people buy a two-channel bench scope for. Its limits are the same 8-bit converter and 4 Mpts dual-channel memory, plus softer edges on anything faster than about 10 ns.

Probe setting decides more than the model

Hantek's table notes that bandwidth drops to 6 MHz with a probe set to 1X, on both models. At 1X a 150 MHz DSO2D15 is a 6 MHz scope. Set the probe and the channel menu to 10X for anything fast. Both inputs are rated 300 V CAT II, with 300 Vrms maximum through a 10X probe; for mains-side work, use a differential probe rather than clipping the ground lead to a live conductor.

The weak point: 500 MSa/s across 150 MHz

The DSO2D15's extra bandwidth comes without extra sampling. With both channels on, each gets 500 MSa/s, which is 3.3 samples per cycle at 150 MHz. Hantek lists sin(x)/x interpolation, which reconstructs a sine well at that ratio, but a single-shot glitch near the top of the band gets only two or three real samples. For repeating signals that is fine; for one-off events, switch off the second channel to get 1 GSa/s back. Hantek also doesn't publish a waveform update rate for either model, so we can't say how quickly either redraws intermittent faults.

Choose the Hantek DSO2D15 if…

  • ✓You look at digital clocks above about 20 MHz and want their edges drawn close to true shape
  • ✓You measure rise times in the 5 to 10 ns range, where a 3.5 ns scope adds visible error
  • ✓You want the 100X P4100 probe that this listing bundles for higher-voltage work
  • ✓You'd rather buy the headroom once than outgrow 100 MHz in a year

Choose the Hantek DSO2D10 if…

  • ✓Your signals are audio, PWM, UART, I2C, SPI under 10 MHz or power-supply switching
  • ✓You want the same generator, decoding and memory for less money
  • ✓Most of your probing is at 1X, where Hantek limits both models to 6 MHz anyway
  • ✓You want to read our full DSO2D10 review before buying

Neither if…

You need more than two inputs at once, for example SPI's clock, chip select and both data lines together. Both are two-channel scopes; a four-channel model such as the Rigol DS1054Z fits that job better.

The bottom line

The Hantek DSO2D10 is the right buy for most two-channel benches: 100 MHz, a 25 MHz generator, five serial decoders and 8 Mpts, all identical to its sibling. Pay for the Hantek DSO2D15 only if you work with clocks from 20 to 30 MHz or time edges faster than about 10 ns, where its 150 MHz and 2.4 ns rise time show a cleaner signal.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Hantek DSO2D15 just a DSO2D10 with more bandwidth?+

On Hantek's published spec table, yes. Both have two channels, 1 GSa/s, 8 Mpts, the same 25 MHz generator, 14 trigger types, five serial decoders and a 7-inch 800 × 480 screen. The table differs only in bandwidth, 150 MHz against 100 MHz, and rise time, 2.4 ns against 3.5 ns.

Can the DSO2D10's own generator show its limits?+

Partly. The generator's square and arbitrary waves stop at 10 MHz, and a 10 MHz square keeps its 9th harmonic on the DSO2D10 and its 15th on the DSO2D15, so the D15 draws slightly sharper corners. Its 25 MHz sine sits well inside both bandwidths and will look the same on either scope.

Does 150 MHz help with Arduino or ESP32 projects?+

Rarely. A 16 MHz Arduino clock needs about 80 MHz to keep its 5th harmonic, and the buses you probe on these boards, UART, I2C and SPI at a few MHz, sit far lower. The DSO2D10 shows them just as well. The extra bandwidth matters for faster clocks and sharp edges, not typical maker projects.

Why does Hantek list 6 MHz bandwidth for both models?+

That figure applies with the probe set to 1X. A 1X probe loads the input with its cable capacitance, which limits bandwidth to about 6 MHz on both scopes, per Hantek's table. Switch the probe and the channel setting to 10X to get the rated 100 MHz or 150 MHz.

Which one holds its value better as I learn more?+

The DSO2D15 has more room before bandwidth becomes the limit, since a 30 MHz clock still looks square on it. But both share the same two-channel, 8-bit, 8 Mpts core, so if you outgrow one because you need four channels or deeper memory, you'll outgrow the other at the same point.
