Fluke 27 II vs Fluke 28 II: Differences Explained

The Fluke 27 II and 28 II share one physical meter: the same IP67 case, 3 m drop rating, MSHA approval, −15 to +55 °C operating range, three AA cells for about 800 hours, 698.5 g holstered weight, CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V rating and limited lifetime warranty. The difference is inside. The 28 II measures AC as True RMS and adds a low-pass filter for motor drives, a −200 to +1090 °C temperature input with a probe in the box, 250 µs peak capture, a 20,000-count high-resolution mode and tighter DC volts. The 27 II is average responding, so it is only accurate on clean sine waves.

One body, two measuring engines

Fluke built these two as a pair. Its product pages print the same holstered dimensions, the same 698.5 g, the same IP67 seal and the same 10 ft drop test for both, and the 28 II page links its sibling as the "AVG Multimeter" next to its own "True-rms" label. So the buying decision isn't about toughness at all. It comes down to the kind of AC you measure and whether you want the 28 II's extra functions.

Why average responding goes wrong on distorted AC

An average-responding meter rectifies the AC, takes the average, and multiplies by about 1.111, which is the ratio between RMS and average for a pure sine. That shortcut is exact on a sine and only on a sine. Feed it a different shape and the multiplier is wrong for that shape. A True RMS meter computes the heating-equivalent value directly, so it doesn't care what the waveform looks like, within its crest-factor and bandwidth limits.

The numbers come from simple ratios. A square wave of 100 V peak has an RMS of 100 V and an average of 100 V, so the meter shows 100 × 1.111 = 111 V. A pulse train that is on for a quarter of the time, scaled to 100 V RMS, has an average one-half of its RMS (the square root of 0.25), so the meter shows 0.5 × 1.111 × 100, about 56 V. The narrow-pulse case is the one that bites in the field: the current drawn by rectifier front ends in drives, computers and LED drivers arrives in short, tall peaks, so an average-responding meter tends to read that current low.

The 28 II's extra tools, in practice

Low-pass filter. On the output side of a variable speed drive, the voltage is a train of fast switched pulses. The filter strips the high-frequency switching content so the 28 II reads something close to the voltage the motor actually responds to. The 27 II lists no filter, and its higher 30 kHz bandwidth would let more of that switching noise into the reading.

Peak capture. At 250 µs, the 28 II can hold short spikes and dips that a normal Min/Max would average away, which helps when a contactor or a motor start drags a supply down for a moment.

Temperature. With the included 80BK-A bead probe, the 28 II covers bearing, enclosure and pipe checks without a second tool. The meter's range is −200 to +1090 °C; the probe you plug in sets the practical limit.

High resolution. The 20,000-count mode adds a digit for slow-changing readings such as sensor voltages, and the backlit keypad helps in a dark vault or mine heading.

The only question that matters

Ask whether any AC you measure comes from, or feeds, power electronics. If the honest answer is "yes, sometimes", the 28 II is the meter. If your AC is line-fed and linear and most of your work is DC, ohms and continuity, the 27 II gives you the identical sealed, MSHA-approved body without paying for functions you won't use.

Safety: a wrong AC reading is a safety problem, not only an accuracy problem

An average-responding meter can understate current on a circuit feeding electronic loads, which can hide an overloaded conductor or neutral. When the load is a drive, UPS or bank of LED drivers, confirm current with a True RMS instrument before deciding a circuit is within its rating. Both meters are CAT IV 600V / CAT III 1000V; use leads with a matching rating, verify the meter on a known live source before and after proving a circuit dead, and follow your site's lockout/tagout and arc-flash rules. IP67 keeps water out of the meter, not off your hands or probes. If the work is beyond your training, call a qualified electrician.

The bottom line

The Fluke 27 II and 28 II are the same rugged meter outside: IP67, a 3 m drop rating, MSHA approval, 800 hours on three AAs and a lifetime warranty. Buy the 28 II if any of your AC involves drives, inverters or electronic loads, or if you want temperature, peak capture and a hi-res mode. Buy the 27 II only if your AC is clean line power and most of your readings are DC, resistance and continuity.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Fluke 27 II True RMS?+

No. Fluke's Amazon listing calls it the 27II AVG multimeter, and Fluke's own 28 II page labels the 27 II as the AVG model next to the True RMS 28 II. Average responding is accurate on clean sine waves but drifts on distorted waveforms such as drive outputs or the current drawn by electronic loads.

Do the Fluke 27 II and 28 II have the same case?+

Yes. Fluke lists both at 6.35 × 10.0 × 19.81 cm and 698.5 g with the holster, both IP67 and both tested for a 3 m (10 ft) drop. Both are MSHA approved, rated −15 to +55 °C with 20-minute excursions to −40 °C, and run about 800 hours on three AA cells.

Can the Fluke 27 II measure temperature?+

Fluke doesn't list a temperature function for the 27 II, and none of its included accessories is a probe. The 28 II reads −200 to +1090 °C and ships with the 80BK-A type-K bead probe, so if you need temperature in a sealed meter, the 28 II is the one to buy.

Can I use the Fluke 27 II on a VFD?+

Not for reliable output readings. A drive's output is a switched pulse train, and an average-responding meter without a low-pass filter can read it well off. The 28 II is True RMS and lists a low-pass filter for variable speed drives. On the drive's line-side voltage, a clean sine, the 27 II is fine.

Why is the 27 II's AC volts accuracy better than the 28 II's?+

On paper it is: 0.5% + 3 against 0.7% + 4. That figure only holds for a sine wave, because an average-responding meter is calibrated assuming that shape. On distorted AC its real error can be far larger than any published line, while the 28 II's True RMS reading stays within its spec.

Are both the Fluke 27 II and 28 II MSHA approved?+

Yes, Fluke lists MSHA approval on both product pages. Neither is intrinsically safe for explosive atmospheres; Fluke sells a separate 28 II EX for classified areas. Check with your mine's safety department which approvals your site's electrical program requires before you buy.
