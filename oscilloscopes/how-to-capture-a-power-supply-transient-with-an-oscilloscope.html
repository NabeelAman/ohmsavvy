How to Capture a Power Supply Transient With an Oscilloscope

A regulator that measures a perfect 5.00 V on a multimeter can still sag for a few hundred microseconds every time a motor, radio or processor core wakes up, and that brief dip is often what resets the circuit. To capture a power supply transient with an oscilloscope you provoke the event on purpose with a controlled load step, magnify the rail so a small dip fills the screen, and trigger on the dip itself. This guide focuses on load transients, the most common case, with a short section on input-side events.

By the OhmSavvy Research Team · Updated October 22, 2026

What a load transient looks like

When load current jumps, the output capacitors supply the difference for the first moments, and the rail sags until the regulator's control loop responds. The loop then pulls the rail back, sometimes with a little ringing. When the load drops away, the opposite happens: the rail briefly rises above nominal. Two numbers describe the result: the undershoot (or overshoot) amplitude and the recovery time back to within a tolerance band. Start-up behavior is a separate test, covered in its own guide.

1Build a simple load step

An N-channel MOSFET switching a power resistor gives a clean, repeatable step. Choose the resistor for the step current you want (R = V ÷ I; a 5 V rail and a 1 A step needs 5 Ω), drive the gate from a function generator with a low-frequency square wave, and keep the on-time short so the resistor and MOSFET stay cool: at 1 A from 5 V the resistor dissipates 5 W while on, but at a 10% duty cycle the average is 0.5 W. A small resistor in the source leg lets you watch the current as a voltage. Connect the step close to the output capacitors, where the real load would sit.

2Probe the rail without picking up noise

The standard 15 cm ground clip forms a loop that picks up magnetic fields from switching regulators and adds spikes that look like part of the transient. Remove the probe's hat and ground clip and use a tip-and-barrel connection: the tip touches the positive terminal of the output capacitor, and a short ground spring wrapped around the barrel touches its ground terminal. This is the same technique used for measuring ripple voltage, and it matters just as much here. Turn on the channel's 20 MHz bandwidth limit to hold back switching noise; load transients play out over microseconds to milliseconds, so the limit removes noise without changing their shape much.

3Zoom on the rail: DC with offset, or AC coupling

A 100 mV dip on a 5 V rail is invisible at 1 V/div. You need around 20 to 50 mV/div with the rail centered on screen, and there are two ways to get there.

MethodHowStrengthWeakness
DC coupling with offsetSet the channel offset to the nominal rail voltage, then raise sensitivityShows the true level, slow recoveries and load regulationOffset range is limited at high sensitivity on some scopes
AC couplingBlock the DC level and raise sensitivityWorks on any scope, no offset neededDistorts slow recoveries and hides the DC shift from load regulation

Prefer DC with offset whenever the scope's offset range reaches the rail voltage at your chosen V/div; it is the only way to see that the rail settled at a slightly lower level under load, which is part of the answer.

4Trigger on the dip, or on the load-switch gate

With a controlled load step, trigger on CH2, the MOSFET gate, rising edge, Normal mode. The gate is clean, so the transient lands in exactly the same place every sweep and you can even average. For a transient you do not control, such as a dip caused by firmware switching something on, trigger on the rail itself: edge trigger, falling slope, level a little below nominal, Single mode. Only a real sag crosses that line.

5Measure undershoot amplitude and recovery time

Stop the acquisition. Put one horizontal cursor on the rail level before the step and the other at the bottom of the dip: the difference is the undershoot. Then decide on a tolerance band, such as ±1% of nominal or the regulation the load needs, and place vertical cursors from the load step edge to the moment the rail re-enters that band for good. Repeat on the falling edge of the gate for overshoot. Peak detect or a single capture shows the worst dip; averaging shows a cleaner typical response.

Deep dip or slow recovery?

The two numbers point to different parts of the supply. A deep, fast dip usually involves the output capacitance and its inductance. A shallow dip with a long, sluggish or ringing recovery points more toward the regulator's control loop. Our guide to switching supply waveforms shows how to check the switching behavior at the same time.

Input-side transients

Disturbances can also arrive from the input: a supply input sagging when a motor on the same source starts, a hot-plug spike when a connector is pushed in, or a battery dipping under load. Put CH1 on the input and CH2 on the output, trigger on the input with a falling edge just below its normal level (or rising for a spike), and check whether the output holds regulation through the event. For narrow input spikes, the techniques in capturing a brief voltage spike apply.

Mains-powered supplies and hot loads

Measure on the low-voltage output side of a mains-powered supply, where the output is isolated and referenced to earth by your scope ground. Never clip the scope ground to the primary side; that needs a differential probe. Load resistors and MOSFETs can get hot enough to burn, even at low duty cycles, so mount them on a heatsink and keep fingers clear.

Scopes for transient work

Load-step testing benefits from more than two channels, so the rail, the gate drive and the sense resistor sit on one screen, and from fine vertical resolution when the dip is only tens of millivolts on a multi-volt rail.

Frequently asked questions

How big a load step should I use?

Match what the real circuit does. If the datasheet of the load, or of the regulator, specifies a step such as 10% to 90% of rated current, use that. Otherwise step between the circuit's idle current and its peak current. A step larger than the regulator's rating tells you about current limiting, not transient response.

Does the speed of the load step matter?

Yes. A faster current step usually produces a deeper initial dip, because the output capacitors and their inductance carry the change before the control loop can react. Regulator datasheets often state the slew rate of their test step in amps per microsecond. Note your step's edge speed, visible on the gate or current channel, alongside the result.

Why is there a sharp spike at the very edge of the dip?

Partly real and partly measurement. The parasitic inductance of the output capacitors and board traces produces a sharp voltage step when current changes quickly. A long probe ground lead adds its own pickup on top. Re-measure with a tip-and-barrel connection right at the output capacitor; whatever remains is the circuit.

Can I use AC coupling to measure the recovery time?

For fast recoveries, yes. AC coupling blocks the DC level through a high-pass filter, so a dip that lasts a long time compared with that filter's time constant gets distorted and appears to recover on its own. If the recovery takes milliseconds, use DC coupling with offset instead.

How do I capture inrush current when a power supply turns on?

A scope measures voltage, so put a small, low-value sense resistor in the supply's input or output return and probe across it, or use a current probe. Current equals the measured voltage divided by the resistance. Set Single mode with a rising-edge trigger just above zero, choose a timebase of a few milliseconds per division, then switch the supply on and read the peak.
