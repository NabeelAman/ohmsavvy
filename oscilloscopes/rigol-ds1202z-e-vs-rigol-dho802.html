Rigol DS1202Z-E vs Rigol DHO802

Rigol DS1202Z-E vs Rigol DHO802 sets bandwidth against resolution inside one maker's two-channel range. The DS1202Z-E is the faster scope: 200 MHz with a 1.75 ns calculated rise time, but an 8-bit converter. The DHO802 is the finer one: 12 bits, 16 times smaller steps, but 70 MHz and a 5 ns rise time. The DHO802 also brings a 1024 × 600 touchscreen, USB-C power, CAN decoding and a capture rate up to 1,000,000 waveforms per second. We work the step-size math on a real ripple job to show when 12 bits beats 200 MHz.

Spec by spec

The DS1202Z-E wins the speed rows; the DHO802 wins resolution, capture rate and almost everything physical. Capture rate needs a log scale.

When 12 bits beats 200 MHz: a ripple job worked through

Converter step size = full-scale range ÷ number of levels. On an 8-division screen at 1 V/div, full scale is 8 V. The DS1202Z-E divides that into 256 steps of about 31 mV; the DHO802 into 4,096 steps of about 2 mV. Now look at a 5 V rail with 10 mV of ripple, DC-coupled so you can watch the rail come up and the ripple at the same setting.

The last row is the catch: if you can AC-couple and zoom to 5 mV/div, the 8-bit scope sees the ripple well. The 12-bit advantage is for signals where you can't zoom in that far: small detail riding on a large level, a startup transient and its ripple in one capture, or audio distortion a few tenths of a percent below a big sine.

High Resolution mode isn't the same thing

The DS1202Z-E lists a High Resolution mode of up to 12 bits. Modes like this generally work by averaging neighboring samples, which trades away bandwidth and only helps on signals slow enough to average. The DHO802's 12 bits are native, at its full 70 MHz.

What each one can and can't measure

Rigol DS1202Z-E: the faster eye

The DS1202Z-E handles clocks to about 40 MHz and edges to roughly 5 to 9 ns, with 2 ns/div as its fastest timebase. It's the better scope for signal integrity on fast digital lines. With both channels on it has 2.5 samples per cycle at 200 MHz, which is thin for single shots. Its 8 bits limit it whenever you need small detail on a big signal.

Rigol DHO802: the finer eye

The DHO802 suits analog, power and audio work, sensors and mixed embedded debugging up to about 14 MHz clocks (70 ÷ 5). With both channels on it keeps 625 MSa/s, almost 9 samples per cycle at 70 MHz, and Rigol lists 33 automatic measurements and a 1 Mpt FFT. Its 5 ns rise time reads edges accurately only down to about 15 to 25 ns.

Neither input is isolated

Rigol rates the DHO802's inputs CAT I 300 Vrms, 400 Vpk, and the DS1202Z-E's CAT I 300 Vrms and CAT II 100 Vrms. The DHO802's USB-C supply doesn't make it a floating instrument. On mains or high-voltage DC, use a differential probe; call an electrician for building wiring.

The trust question: whose datasheet says what

Both scopes come from current Rigol datasheets, and on the points that decide this comparison they don't conflict. The weak spots are smaller: the DS1202Z-E's Amazon listing in our Pick is sold through a reseller, so check that it includes Rigol's standard 350 MHz probes, and the DHO802's million-waveform figure applies only in UltraAcquire mode, with 30,000 wfms/s in normal vector display.

Choose the Rigol DS1202Z-E if…

  • ✓You look at clocks above 15 MHz or edges faster than about 15 ns
  • ✓You check signal integrity: overshoot, ringing and reflections on fast lines
  • ✓You want 350 MHz probes in the standard kit, per Rigol's datasheet
  • ✓You prefer physical knobs and a conventional mains-powered bench scope

Choose the Rigol DHO802 if…

  • ✓You measure small changes on larger signals: ripple, sensor drift, audio distortion
  • ✓You want CAN decoding and up to four decoders at once
  • ✓You want a lighter scope that runs from a USB-C 15 V supply
  • ✓You hunt rare glitches and value 1,000,000 wfms/s in UltraAcquire mode

Neither if…

You need more than two channels. Both are two-channel scopes; the Rigol DHO804 gives four 12-bit channels and the Rigol DS1054Z four 8-bit ones.

The bottom line

Pick the Rigol DHO802 for analog, power, audio and general embedded work: 12-bit resolution, CAN decoding, up to 1,000,000 wfms/s, a touchscreen and USB-C power in a 1.78 kg body. Pick the Rigol DS1202Z-E when speed matters more than fine detail, for 20 to 40 MHz clocks, fast edges and ringing, where its 200 MHz and 1.75 ns rise time see what the DHO802 smooths away.

Frequently asked questions

Is 12-bit better than 200 MHz?+

For different jobs. 12 bits gives 16 times finer vertical steps, which shows small detail on larger signals such as ripple on a DC rail or audio distortion. 200 MHz shows fast edges and high-frequency clocks faithfully. If your signals stay under about 14 MHz, the DHO802's resolution usually helps more.

Does the DS1202Z-E's High Resolution mode match the DHO802?+

Not in general. Rigol lists up to 12 bits in High Resolution mode for the DS1202Z-E, but modes like this typically average neighboring samples, which lowers effective bandwidth and doesn't help single fast events. The DHO802 samples at 12 bits natively across its 70 MHz bandwidth.

Can the DHO802 run from a power bank?+

Rigol lists its power input as USB Type-C at DC 15 V, 3 A, with up to 45 W drawn. A power bank would need to supply 15 V over USB-C Power Delivery at that current. The DS1202Z-E needs 100 to 240 V AC mains at up to 50 W.

Which one decodes CAN bus?+

The DHO802. Rigol's DHO800 datasheet lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C, SPI and CAN decoding as standard, with up to four decodes enabled at once. The DS1000Z-E datasheet lists parallel, RS232/UART, I2C and SPI decoding on two buses for the DS1202Z-E, without CAN.

How much smaller is the DHO802?+

Rigol lists the DHO802 at 265.35 × 161.75 × 77.38 mm and 1.78 kg without packaging, against 313.1 × 160.8 × 122.4 mm and 2.9 kg for the DS1202Z-E. The DHO802 is about 45 mm shallower and 1.1 kg lighter, which helps on a crowded bench.
